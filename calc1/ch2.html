Chapter 2 · Derivatives and Rates of Change

← previousoutlinenext →

The derivative is the tangent slope of Chapter 1, now computed at every point at once. This chapter defines it as a limit, then builds rules (power, product, quotient, chain) so we rarely need the limit again, and finishes with three uses: implicit curves, related rates, and linear approximation.

2.1 Derivatives and Rates of Change

GeometryThe derivative of $f$ at $a$ is the slope of the tangent line at $(a, f(a))$, the limit of the secant slopes:

$\displaystyle f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} = \lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a},$

provided the limit exists. The two forms are the same limit with $x = a + h$. The tangent line to $y = f(x)$ at $(a, f(a))$ is

$y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a).$

Rate of change. If $y = f(x)$, the average rate of change of $y$ over $[x_1, x_2]$ is $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1}$, and the instantaneous rate of change at $x = a$ is $f'(a)$. Its units are (units of $y$) per (unit of $x$). If $s(t)$ is position, $s'(a)$ is the velocity at time $a$ and $|s'(a)|$ is the speed.

Example. $f(x) = \dfrac{x + 2}{x - 1}$ at $a = 3$. $\displaystyle f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\frac{5 + h}{2 + h} - \frac52}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{2(5 + h) - 5(2 + h)}{2h(2 + h)} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{-3}{2(2 + h)} = -\frac34.$ The tangent line is $y - \tfrac52 = -\tfrac34(x - 3)$.

2.2 The Derivative as a Function

ConceptLetting $a$ vary gives a new function, the derivative $f'$:

$\displaystyle f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}.$

Other notations for $f'(x)$ with $y = f(x)$: $y'$, $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}f(x)$. The symbol $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ is a reminder that $f'$ is a limit of $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$.

Reading $f'$ off the graph of $f$. The height of $f'$ at $x$ is the slope of $f$ at $x$. Where $f$ rises, $f' > 0$. Where $f$ falls, $f' < 0$. Where $f$ has a horizontal tangent, $f' = 0$. Steep means $|f'|$ is large.

Slope becomes height. Top: $f(x) = x^3/3 - x$ and its tangent line. Bottom: $f'(x) = x^2 - 1$, traced by recording each tangent slope as a height. The tangent is horizontal at $x = \pm 1$, where $f'$ crosses $0$.

Example. $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$. Combining fractions, $\dfrac{1}{h}\left(\dfrac{1}{(x + h)^2} - \dfrac{1}{x^2}\right) = \dfrac{x^2 - (x + h)^2}{h\,x^2(x + h)^2} = \dfrac{-2x - h}{x^2(x + h)^2} \to \dfrac{-2x}{x^4}$, so $f'(x) = -\dfrac{2}{x^3}$ for $x \neq 0$. Here $f'$ has the same domain as $f$. In general the domain of $f'$ can be smaller.

Differentiability. $f$ is differentiable at $a$ if $f'(a)$ exists, and differentiable on an open interval if it is differentiable at every number in it.

Theorem
If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

Why. $f(a + h) - f(a) = \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\cdot h \to f'(a)\cdot 0 = 0$ as $h \to 0$, so $f(a + h) \to f(a)$.

How differentiability fails. A differentiable function looks more and more like a straight line as we zoom in on a point (the tangent line). It fails at

  • a discontinuity (by the theorem above),
  • a corner, where the left and right secant slopes approach different values, as for $|x|$ at $0$ (slopes $-1$ and $1$),
  • a cusp, where the slopes approach $-\infty$ on one side and $\infty$ on the other, as for $x^{2/3}$ at $0$,
  • a vertical tangent, where the slopes approach $\infty$ (or $-\infty$) from both sides, as for $x^{1/3}$ at $0$. The function is continuous there.
Zoom in at the origin. The smooth graph straightens into its tangent line. The corner stays a V, the cusp stays a point, and the vertical tangent stays vertical, so none of these has a derivative at $0$.
Common pitfall

Continuity does not imply differentiability. $|x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there. Only the converse, differentiable implies continuous, is true.

Higher derivatives. $f'' = (f')'$ is the second derivative, also written $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$. If $s(t)$ is position, $v(t) = s'(t)$ is velocity and $a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t)$ is acceleration.

2.3 Basic Differentiation Formulas

RulesFor constants $c$ and $n$, and differentiable $f$, $g$:

$\dfrac{d}{dx}(c) = 0, \qquad \dfrac{d}{dx}(x^n) = n x^{n - 1}, \qquad (cf)' = c f', \qquad (f \pm g)' = f' \pm g'.$

Why the power rule. For a positive integer $n$, $(x + h)^n = x^n + n x^{n - 1}h + (\text{terms with } h^2, h^3, \ldots)$ by the binomial theorem. Subtract $x^n$, divide by $h$, and every term except $n x^{n - 1}$ still contains $h$, so it goes to $0$. The rule holds for every real $n$ (where $x^n$ is defined), e.g. $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sqrt x = \dfrac{d}{dx}x^{1/2} = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2}$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{1}{x^5} = \dfrac{d}{dx}x^{-5} = -5x^{-6}$.

Two trigonometric limits ($\theta$ in radians):

$\displaystyle\lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1, \qquad \lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\cos\theta - 1}{\theta} = 0.$

Why the first. On the unit circle with $0 < \theta < \pi/2$, the arc of length $\theta$ is longer than its height $\sin\theta$ and shorter than the tangent segment $\tan\theta$. So $\sin\theta < \theta < \tan\theta$, which rearranges to $\cos\theta < \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta} < 1$. Both bounds approach $1$, so the Squeeze Theorem gives $1$ (the left side follows from evenness). For the second, multiply by $\dfrac{\cos\theta + 1}{\cos\theta + 1}$ to get $-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\cdot\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta + 1} \to -1\cdot 0 = 0$.

Sine and cosine.

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x, \qquad \dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x.$

Why. $\dfrac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} = \sin x\cdot\dfrac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x\cdot\dfrac{\sin h}{h} \to \sin x\cdot 0 + \cos x\cdot 1$, using $\sin(x + h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h$. Picture: the slope of $\sin$ is $1$ at $0$, $0$ at the peak $\pi/2$, and $-1$ at $\pi$, exactly the values of $\cos$.

Example. $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(4x^3 - \dfrac{2}{\sqrt x} + 5\cos x\right) = 12x^2 + x^{-3/2} - 5\sin x.$ Limits like $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 5x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} 5\cdot\frac{\sin 5x}{5x} = 5$ come from the first trig limit with $\theta = 5x$.

Common pitfall

The power rule needs a constant exponent on the variable. It does not apply to $2^x$ (variable exponent), and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\pi^2 = 0$ since $\pi^2$ is a constant. Rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers first: $\dfrac{1}{x^4} = x^{-4}$, $\sqrt[3]{x^2} = x^{2/3}$.

2.4 The Product and Quotient Rules

RulesFor differentiable $f$ and $g$:

$(fg)' = f'g + fg', \qquad \left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2} \quad (g \neq 0).$

Why the product rule. Think of $f g$ as the area of an $f$ by $g$ rectangle. Changing $x$ by $h$ changes the sides by $\Delta f$ and $\Delta g$. The area gains two strips, $\Delta f\cdot g$ and $f\cdot\Delta g$, plus a corner $\Delta f\,\Delta g$. Dividing by $h$ and letting $h \to 0$, the strips give $f'g + fg'$ and the corner gives $f'\cdot\Delta g \to 0$.

The quotient rule follows by applying the product rule to $f = \dfrac{f}{g}\cdot g$ and solving for $\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)'$.

The other trigonometric derivatives follow from the quotient rule:

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec^2 x, \quad \dfrac{d}{dx}\sec x = \sec x\tan x, \quad \dfrac{d}{dx}\csc x = -\csc x\cot x, \quad \dfrac{d}{dx}\cot x = -\csc^2 x.$

Why. Write each one in terms of $\sin$ and $\cos$ (for instance $\sec x = 1/\cos x$) and apply the quotient rule, then simplify with $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$. The "co" functions get the minus signs.

Example. $\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{3x - 1}{x^2 + 2} = \dfrac{3(x^2 + 2) - (3x - 1)\cdot 2x}{(x^2 + 2)^2} = \dfrac{-3x^2 + 2x + 6}{(x^2 + 2)^2}.$ And $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sqrt x\,\sin x) = \dfrac{\sin x}{2\sqrt x} + \sqrt x\cos x$.

Common pitfall

$(fg)' \neq f'g'$ and $\left(\dfrac fg\right)' \neq \dfrac{f'}{g'}$. Test with $f = g = x$: $(x\cdot x)' = 2x$, but $f'g' = 1$. In the quotient rule, the order in the numerator matters. It is $f'g - fg'$, "low d-high minus high d-low".

2.5 The Chain Rule

RulesIf $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x)$, then the composition $F = f \circ g$, $F(x) = f(g(x))$, is differentiable at $x$ and

$F'(x) = f'(g(x))\, g'(x). \qquad\text{In Leibniz notation, with } u = g(x),\ y = f(u):\quad \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du}\,\dfrac{du}{dx}.$

Why. A derivative is a stretch factor: near $x$, $g$ multiplies small changes by $g'(x)$. Then near $u = g(x)$, $f$ multiplies small changes by $f'(u)$. Doing one after the other multiplies the factors. If $u$ changes twice as fast as $x$ and $y$ three times as fast as $u$, then $y$ changes six times as fast as $x$.

Stretch factors multiply. A short interval on the $x$-line is sent by $u = x^2$ to an interval about $2x$ times as long, then by $y = \sin u$ to one about $|\cos u|$ times as long again. A negative factor ($\cos u < 0$) also flips the interval's direction.

Outside-inside. Differentiate the outer function, leave the inside alone, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. Power form: $\dfrac{d}{dx}[g(x)]^n = n[g(x)]^{n - 1}g'(x)$.

Examples. $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2 - 4x)^3 = 3(x^2 - 4x)^2(2x - 4)$. $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(x^2) = \cos(x^2)\cdot 2x$, while $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin^2 x = 2\sin x\cos x$. Longer chains multiply more factors: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin^3(2x) = 3\sin^2(2x)\cdot\cos(2x)\cdot 2$.

Where $f'$ is evaluated. With $f(u) = \sqrt u$ and $g(x) = x^2 + 1$, $f'(u) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt u}$ must be evaluated at $u = g(x)$: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x^2 + 1} = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}\cdot 2x = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}$.

Common pitfall

Forgetting the inner derivative: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(4x) = 4\cos(4x)$, not $\cos(4x)$. And evaluating the outer derivative at the wrong place: $f'(g(x))$, not $f'(x)$.

2.6 Implicit Differentiation

TechniqueAn equation in $x$ and $y$, such as $x^2 + y^2 = 25$, defines curves that need not be graphs of one function. Near most points, though, the curve is the graph of some differentiable function $y = y(x)$. To find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ without solving for $y$:

  1. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a function of $x$. Every time $y$ is differentiated, the chain rule attaches a factor $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$: $\dfrac{d}{dx}y^2 = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}$.
  2. Solve the result for $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$.

Why it is legal. Both sides are equal as functions of $x$ along the curve, so their derivatives are equal.

Example. The ellipse $4x^2 + y^2 = 8$ gives $8x + 2y\,y' = 0$, so $y' = -\dfrac{4x}{y}$. At $(1, 2)$ the slope is $-2$. The formula gives a slope at every point with $y \neq 0$, on the upper and the lower half at once, without splitting the ellipse into two functions.

Example (a folium). $x^3 + y^3 = 9xy$. Differentiating, $3x^2 + 3y^2 y' = 9y + 9x y'$ (product rule on the right). Collect the $y'$ terms: $y'(3y^2 - 9x) = 9y - 3x^2$, so

$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{3y - x^2}{y^2 - 3x}.$

The point $(2, 4)$ is on the curve ($8 + 64 = 72 = 9\cdot 8$), and the slope there is $\dfrac{12 - 4}{16 - 6} = \dfrac45$. The tangent is horizontal where the numerator is $0$ (and the denominator is not) and vertical where the denominator is $0$ (and the numerator is not).

Tangents to an implicit curve. The folium $x^3 + y^3 = 9xy$ fails the vertical line test, yet $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{3y - x^2}{y^2 - 3x}$ gives the tangent slope at each point. At the origin the formula is $0/0$: the curve crosses itself there.
Common pitfall

Dropping the factor $y'$: $\dfrac{d}{dx}y^3 = 3y^2y'$, not $3y^2$. And a product such as $xy$ needs the product rule: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(xy) = y + xy'$.

2.7 Related Rates

ApplicationIn a related rates problem several quantities change with time $t$ and are tied together by an equation. Differentiating the equation with respect to $t$ (implicit differentiation in $t$) ties their rates together, so a known rate gives an unknown one.

  1. Draw a picture. Name every quantity that changes with a variable, and every fixed quantity with its number.
  2. Write down the given rates and the wanted rate as derivatives with respect to $t$.
  3. Write an equation relating the variables (geometry: Pythagoras, similar triangles, area, volume).
  4. Differentiate with respect to $t$.
  5. Only now substitute the values at the instant in question, and solve.

Example. A $13$ ft ladder leans on a wall, and its foot slides away at $2$ ft/s. How fast does the top slide down when the foot is $5$ ft from the wall? Let $x$ be the distance from the wall to the foot and $y$ the height of the top. Then $x^2 + y^2 = 169$, so $2x\dfrac{dx}{dt} + 2y\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0$, i.e. $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = -\dfrac{x}{y}\dfrac{dx}{dt}$. At $x = 5$, $y = 12$ and $\dfrac{dx}{dt} = 2$, giving $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = -\dfrac{5}{12}\cdot 2 = -\dfrac56$ ft/s. The top slides down at $\tfrac56$ ft/s.

Same speed at the foot, faster and faster at the top. $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = -\dfrac{x}{y}\dfrac{dx}{dt}$, and $\dfrac xy$ grows without bound as the top nears the floor.

Example. A circular ripple's radius $r$ grows at $2$ cm/s. With $A = \pi r^2$, $\dfrac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi r\dfrac{dr}{dt}$, so at $r = 5$ the area grows at $2\pi(5)(2) = 20\pi$ cm²/s.

Common pitfall

Substituting the instant's values before differentiating. If we set $x = 5$ first, $x$ becomes a constant and $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$ disappears. Fixed quantities (the ladder's $13$ ft) may be substituted early. Changing ones may not.

2.8 Linear Approximations and Differentials

ApproximationNear $x = a$ the graph of a differentiable $f$ is nearly its tangent line. The linearization of $f$ at $a$ is that line,

$L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a), \qquad f(x) \approx L(x) \text{ for } x \text{ near } a.$

Why it works. This is the zooming picture from 2.2. Close enough to $a$, the graph and the tangent line are indistinguishable, and the error shrinks faster than $x - a$ does.

Zooming in on $(9, 3)$. $f(x) = \sqrt x$ and $L(x) = 3 + \tfrac16(x - 9)$. Each zoom shrinks the gap $L - f$ (pink) much faster than the window.

Example. Estimate $\sqrt{9.3}$. With $f(x) = \sqrt x$ and $a = 9$: $f(9) = 3$, $f'(9) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt 9} = \dfrac16$, so $\sqrt{9.3} \approx L(9.3) = 3 + \tfrac16(0.3) = 3.05$. (The true value is $3.0496\ldots$) The estimate is too big since $\sqrt x$ bends below its tangent lines.

Differentials. Let $dx$ be a change in $x$ (an independent variable). The differential $dy$ is the corresponding change along the tangent line,

$dy = f'(x)\,dx, \qquad \Delta y = f(x + dx) - f(x) \approx dy.$

Here $\Delta y$ is the actual change in $y$. Example. A cube's side is measured as $10$ cm with possible error $0.1$ cm. With $V = s^3$, $dV = 3s^2\,ds = 3(100)(0.1) = 30$ cm³, so the computed volume $1000$ cm³ may be off by about $30$ cm³. The relative error is $\dfrac{dV}{V} = \dfrac{30}{1000} = 3\%$.

Common pitfall

Choose $a$ so that $f(a)$ and $f'(a)$ are easy to compute exactly and $a$ is close to the target. For $\sqrt{26}$ use $a = 25$, not $a = 26$.

Worked example

Tangent line, with a check. Find the tangent line to $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{x + 2}$ at $x = 2$.
Quotient rule: $f'(x) = \dfrac{(x + 2) - x}{(x + 2)^2} = \dfrac{2}{(x + 2)^2}$, so $f'(2) = \dfrac{2}{16} = \dfrac18$.
Check with the product and chain rules: $f = x\,(x + 2)^{-1}$, so $f' = (x + 2)^{-1} - x(x + 2)^{-2}$, which at $x = 2$ is $\tfrac14 - \tfrac{2}{16} = \tfrac18$.
$f(2) = \tfrac12$, so the tangent line is $y - \tfrac12 = \tfrac18(x - 2)$.

A shadow. A person $6$ ft tall walks away from a $15$ ft streetlight at $3$ ft/s. How fast does the tip of the shadow move?
Let $x$ be the person's distance from the light and $s$ the length of the shadow. The light, the person, and the tip form similar triangles: $\dfrac{s}{6} = \dfrac{x + s}{15}$, so $15s = 6x + 6s$ and $s = \tfrac23 x$. The tip is at distance $x + s = \tfrac53 x$ from the light.
Differentiate in $t$: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac53 x\right) = \tfrac53\cdot 3 = 5$ ft/s, at every instant. (The shadow itself lengthens at $\tfrac23\cdot 3 = 2$ ft/s.)

Practice problems

  1. Use the limit definition to find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^2 - 3x$, then check with the power rule.
  2. Use the limit definition to find $f'(1)$ for $f(x) = x^3 + 2x$.
  3. Sketch the graph of $f'$ for $f(x) = |x - 2|$. Where is $f$ not differentiable, and why?
  4. Differentiate $y = x^3\cos x$, $y = \dfrac{x^2}{1 + x}$, $y = (2x^3 - 1)^4$, and $y = \tan(\sqrt x)$.
  5. Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 3x}{2x}$.
  6. Find the tangent line to $x^2 + xy + y^2 = 7$ at $(1, 2)$.
  7. A spherical balloon is inflated at $8\pi$ cm³/s. How fast is the radius growing when $r = 2$ cm? ($V = \tfrac43\pi r^3$.)
  8. Find the linearization of $f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}$ at $a = 8$ and use it to estimate $\sqrt[3]{8.3}$.

Strategies

Limit definition problems. Write $\dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$ in full, simplify until a factor of $h$ cancels (expand, combine fractions, or rationalize), then let $h \to 0$.

Choosing a rule. Look at the last operation in the formula. A sum: differentiate term by term. A product: product rule. A quotient: quotient rule (or rewrite as a product with a negative power). A function of something other than $x$: chain rule. Rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers first.

Tangent line. Needs a point $(a, f(a))$ and a slope $f'(a)$, then $y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)$.

Implicit curves. Differentiate, collect every $y'$ term on one side, factor out $y'$, divide.

Related rates. Picture, variables, equation, differentiate in $t$, then substitute. For cones and similar shapes, use similar triangles to remove a variable before differentiating.

Approximations. $f(a + \Delta x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\Delta x$. The change $\Delta f$ is approximately $f'(a)\,\Delta x$.
