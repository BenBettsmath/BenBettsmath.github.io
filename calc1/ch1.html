Chapter 1 · Functions and Limits

← previousoutlinenext →

Limits are the one new idea in Calculus I. Every later definition (derivative, integral) is a limit. This chapter builds the limit from the slope of a tangent line, gives the rules for computing limits, and uses limits to define continuity.

1.1 The Tangent and Velocity Problems

GeometryA secant line to the graph of $f$ is a line through two points of the graph. Through $P = (a, f(a))$ and $Q = (x, f(x))$ its slope is

$m_{PQ} = \dfrac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}.$

The tangent line at $P$ is the line through $P$ whose slope is the value the secant slopes $m_{PQ}$ approach as $Q$ slides along the curve toward $P$. Writing $x = a + h$, so $h$ is the horizontal gap between $Q$ and $P$, the tangent slope is the value $\dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$ approaches as $h \to 0$.

Why we cannot just plug in $h = 0$. A slope needs two points. At $h = 0$ the two points coincide and the quotient is $0/0$. The limit is how we get a slope from one point: we use nearby second points and watch what the slopes settle on.

Example. $f(x) = \sqrt x$ at $P = (4, 2)$. The secant slope is $\dfrac{\sqrt{4 + h} - 2}{h}$. Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt{4 + h} + 2$ to get $\dfrac{(4 + h) - 4}{h(\sqrt{4 + h} + 2)} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4 + h} + 2}$, which approaches $\tfrac14$ as $h \to 0$. The tangent line is $y - 2 = \tfrac14 (x - 4)$.

Secants to the tangent. $Q$ approaches $P$ from the right ($h > 0$) and from the left ($h < 0$). The secant slope approaches $\tfrac14$ either way.

Velocity. If $s(t)$ is the position of an object at time $t$, its average velocity over $[a, a + h]$ is $\dfrac{s(a + h) - s(a)}{h}$, the slope of a secant line on the graph of $s$. Its instantaneous velocity at time $a$ is the value these average velocities approach as $h \to 0$, the slope of the tangent line. Tangent slope and velocity are the same limit.

1.2 The Limit of a Function

ConceptWe write $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ and say "the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ is $L$" if we can make $f(x)$ as close to $L$ as we like by taking $x$ close enough to $a$, but not equal to $a$.

Why "not equal to $a$". The limit describes what $f$ does near $a$. The value $f(a)$ may differ from $L$, or $f(a)$ may not exist at all (as in the secant slope at $h = 0$). The limit ignores it.

One-sided limits. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L$ means $f(x)$ gets close to $L$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from the left ($x < a$), and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L$ means the same from the right ($x > a$).

$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \quad\text{if and only if}\quad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L \ \text{ and } \ \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L.$

If the two one-sided limits differ, $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ does not exist (DNE).

A jump. $f(x) = x + 1$ for $x < 1$, $f(x) = \tfrac12 + (x - 1)^2$ for $x > 1$, and $f(1) = 3$. The left limit is $2$, the right limit is $\tfrac12$, so the two-sided limit does not exist. The value $f(1) = 3$ plays no role.

Infinite limits. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \infty$ means $f(x)$ becomes arbitrarily large (and positive) as $x$ approaches $a$, $x \neq a$. Similarly for $-\infty$ and for one-sided versions. The limit still does not exist as a number. Writing $\infty$ records how it fails.

Vertical asymptote. The line $x = a$ is a vertical asymptote of $y = f(x)$ if at least one of $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ or $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ is $\infty$ or $-\infty$.

How to find the sign. For a quotient whose denominator approaches $0$ while the numerator approaches a nonzero number, the size blows up. The sign comes from the signs of the numerator and denominator on the side we approach from. Example: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3^-}\frac{x + 3}{x - 3}$. The numerator approaches $6 > 0$, and for $x < 3$ the denominator is a small negative number, so the limit is $-\infty$.

Vertical asymptote at $x = 2$. $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x - 2}$. From the left the denominator is small and negative, so $f \to -\infty$. From the right it is small and positive, so $f \to \infty$.
Common pitfall

A table of values or a calculator can mislead. $\sin(\pi/x)$ equals $0$ at every $x = 1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \ldots$, yet $\lim_{x \to 0}\sin(\pi/x)$ does not exist since $\sin(\pi/x)$ also equals $1$ at $x = \tfrac{2}{5}, \tfrac{2}{9}, \ldots$. Limits are settled by algebra and theorems, not by a few sample points.

1.3 Calculating Limits Using the Limit Laws

AlgebraLimit laws. Suppose $c$ is a constant and $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x)$ both exist. Then

  • $\lim (f \pm g) = \lim f \pm \lim g$ and $\lim (c f) = c \lim f$
  • $\lim (f g) = \lim f \cdot \lim g$
  • $\lim (f / g) = \lim f / \lim g$, provided $\lim g \neq 0$
  • $\lim f^n = (\lim f)^n$ and $\lim \sqrt[n]{f} = \sqrt[n]{\lim f}$ ($n$ a positive integer, and $\lim f > 0$ when $n$ is even)

Why. If $f$ is close to $L$ and $g$ is close to $M$, then $f + g$ is close to $L + M$, $fg$ is close to $LM$, and so on. Arithmetic does not destroy closeness (except division by something close to $0$).

Direct substitution. If $f$ is a polynomial or a rational function and $a$ is in the domain of $f$, then $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$. The same holds for roots and trigonometric functions at numbers in their domains.

When substitution gives $0/0$. The form $0/0$ says nothing yet. Rewrite $f$ as a function $g$ with $f(x) = g(x)$ for $x \neq a$, then substitute into $g$. This is legal because the limit ignores $x = a$.

  • Factor and cancel. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 5}\frac{x^2 - 25}{x - 5} = \lim_{x \to 5}(x + 5) = 10.$
  • Rationalize. Multiply by the conjugate, as in the $\sqrt{4 + h}$ example of 1.1.
  • Combine fractions. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2}\frac{\frac1x - \frac12}{x - 2} = \lim_{x \to 2}\frac{2 - x}{2x(x - 2)} = \lim_{x \to 2}\frac{-1}{2x} = -\frac14.$
  • Expand. $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{(2 + h)^2 - 4}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{4h + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(4 + h) = 4.$

Piecewise functions. At a break point, compute each one-sided limit with the formula that applies on that side, then compare.

Squeeze Theorem
If $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ for all $x$ near $a$ (except possibly at $a$) and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = \lim_{x \to a} h(x) = L$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$.

Why. $f$ is trapped between two functions that are both forced to $L$, so it has nowhere else to go.

Example. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} x^2\sin(1/x)$. The limit laws fail since $\lim_{x \to 0}\sin(1/x)$ does not exist. But $-1 \le \sin(1/x) \le 1$, so $-x^2 \le x^2\sin(1/x) \le x^2$ for $x \neq 0$. Both bounds approach $0$, so the limit is $0$.

Squeezed to zero. $y = x^2\sin(1/x)$ oscillates infinitely often near $0$ but stays between $y = -x^2$ and $y = x^2$. Zoom in and both bounds flatten onto the $x$-axis.
Common pitfall

The laws apply only when the individual limits exist. $\lim_{x \to 0} x \cdot \tfrac{1}{x} = 1$, but splitting it as $\lim x \cdot \lim \tfrac1x$ is meaningless since $\lim_{x \to 0}\tfrac1x$ does not exist. Likewise $\dfrac{\lim f}{\lim g}$ needs $\lim g \neq 0$.

1.4 The Precise Definition of a Limit

Concept"Close" in 1.2 is made exact with two tolerances. $\varepsilon$ (epsilon) is how close we demand $f(x)$ be to $L$, and $\delta$ (delta) is how close $x$ must be to $a$ to guarantee it.

Definition
$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a $\delta > 0$ such that if $0 < |x - a| < \delta$, then $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$.

Picture. Draw the horizontal band $L - \varepsilon < y < L + \varepsilon$. The limit is $L$ if, however thin the band, some vertical band $a - \delta < x < a + \delta$ (minus $x = a$) keeps the graph inside it. The condition $0 < |x - a|$ is the "$x \neq a$" of 1.2.

Example. $\lim_{x \to 3}(2x + 1) = 7$. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, we need $|(2x + 1) - 7| = 2|x - 3| < \varepsilon$. Choosing $\delta = \varepsilon/2$ works: $|x - 3| < \varepsilon/2$ gives $2|x - 3| < \varepsilon$.

1.5 Continuity

ConceptA function $f$ is continuous at $a$ if

$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a).$

This one equation asks for three things: $f(a)$ is defined, $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists, and the two are equal. To show continuity at a point, check all three. $f$ is continuous from the left at $a$ if $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = f(a)$, and from the right if $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = f(a)$. $f$ is continuous on an interval if it is continuous at every number in it (one-sided at an endpoint).

Picture. The graph can be drawn through $x = a$ without lifting the pencil. Continuity is exactly what makes direct substitution correct.

Kinds of discontinuity. A removable discontinuity is a hole: the limit exists but $f(a)$ is missing or wrong (fixable by redefining one value). A jump has unequal one-sided limits. An infinite discontinuity is at a vertical asymptote.

Continuous functions. Polynomials, rational functions, roots, and trigonometric functions are continuous on their domains. Sums, differences, products, constant multiples, and quotients (where the denominator is not $0$) of continuous functions are continuous, and so are compositions: if $g$ is continuous at $a$ and $f$ is continuous at $g(a)$, then $f \circ g$ is continuous at $a$.

Example. Choose $c$ so that $f(x) = cx + 1$ for $x \le 2$ and $f(x) = x^2 - 1$ for $x > 2$ is continuous. Each piece is a polynomial, so only $x = 2$ matters. The left limit and $f(2)$ are $2c + 1$, the right limit is $3$. Setting $2c + 1 = 3$ gives $c = 1$.

Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)
Suppose $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$ and $N$ is any number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, with $f(a) \neq f(b)$. Then there is at least one number $c$ in $(a, b)$ with $f(c) = N$.

Why. A graph drawn without lifting the pencil from height $f(a)$ to height $f(b)$ must cross every horizontal line $y = N$ in between.

Use: locating roots. If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $f(a)$, $f(b)$ have opposite signs, then $N = 0$ is between them, so $f$ has a root in $(a, b)$. Example: $f(x) = x^3 + x - 1$ has $f(0) = -1 < 0$ and $f(1) = 1 > 0$, so $x^3 + x - 1 = 0$ has a solution between $0$ and $1$.

Every height is reached. $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1$ on $[-2, 2]$. Each $N$ between $f(-2) = -1$ and $f(2) = 3$ is hit at least once, sometimes three times. The theorem promises existence, not how many.
Common pitfall

The IVT needs continuity on the whole closed interval. $f(x) = 1/x$ has $f(-1) = -1$ and $f(1) = 1$ but never equals $0$, because of the discontinuity at $x = 0$. Also, the IVT says a $c$ exists. It does not find $c$.

Worked example

A piecewise function, checked three ways. Let $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - x - 2}{x - 2}$ for $x \neq 2$ and $f(2) = 1$.
Limit: for $x \neq 2$, $\dfrac{x^2 - x - 2}{x - 2} = \dfrac{(x - 2)(x + 1)}{x - 2} = x + 1$, so $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 3$.
Continuity: $f(2) = 1 \neq 3$, so $f$ is not continuous at $2$. The limit exists, so the discontinuity is removable: redefining $f(2) = 3$ makes $f$ continuous.
Tangent: on $x \neq 2$ the graph is the line $y = x + 1$, so at $x = 0$ the tangent slope is $\lim_{h \to 0}\dfrac{(h + 1) - 1}{h} = 1$.

An infinite limit. $\displaystyle\lim_{t \to 1}\frac{t + 1}{(t - 1)^2}$. The numerator approaches $2$ and the denominator approaches $0$ through positive values from both sides (it is a square). So the limit is $\infty$, and $t = 1$ is a vertical asymptote.

Practice problems

  1. Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^+}\frac{5}{x}$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^-}\frac{5}{x}$. Does $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0}\frac5x$ exist?
  2. Write the slope of the tangent line to $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ at $x = 3$ as a limit of secant slopes, evaluate it, and find the tangent line.
  3. Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 4}\frac{x^2 - 16}{x - 4}$, $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sqrt{9 + h} - 3}{h}$, and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 5}\frac{\frac1x - \frac15}{x - 5}$.
  4. Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2^+}\frac{x + 1}{x(x - 2)}$ and name the vertical asymptotes of $\dfrac{x + 1}{x(x - 2)}$.
  5. Use the Squeeze Theorem to find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} x^4\cos(2/x)$.
  6. Find $k$ so that $f(x) = x^2 + k$ for $x < 1$ and $f(x) = 3x$ for $x \ge 1$ is continuous everywhere.
  7. Show that $x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0$ has a root between $2$ and $3$.

Strategies

Evaluating $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$. Try direct substitution first. A number means done (if $f$ is continuous at $a$). $0/0$ means rewrite: factor, rationalize, or combine fractions. (nonzero)/$0$ means an infinite limit or DNE, and the signs of the one-sided limits decide which.

Piecewise and absolute value. Compute both one-sided limits separately. Rewrite $|x - a|$ as $x - a$ for $x > a$ and $-(x - a)$ for $x < a$.

Bounded times something going to $0$. Use the Squeeze Theorem with $-1 \le \sin(\cdot), \cos(\cdot) \le 1$.

Proving continuity at a point. State the definition $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$, then verify each of the three parts with a computation.

Showing an equation has a solution. Move everything to one side, $f(x) = 0$. Check $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, find a sign change, and cite the IVT.
