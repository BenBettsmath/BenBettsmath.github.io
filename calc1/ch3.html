What the derivative tells us about the function. The sign of $f'$ says where $f$ rises and falls, the sign of $f''$ says which way it bends, and the zeros of $f'$ locate the highest and lowest points. These answer optimization problems and let us sketch graphs by hand. The chapter ends by running differentiation backward: antiderivatives.

3.1 Maximum and Minimum Values

ConceptLet $c$ be a number in the domain $D$ of $f$. Then $f(c)$ is the

  • absolute maximum value of $f$ on $D$ if $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for all $x$ in $D$, and the absolute minimum if $f(c) \le f(x)$ for all $x$ in $D$,
  • local maximum value if $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for all $x$ near $c$ (in some open interval containing $c$), and local minimum if $f(c) \le f(x)$ for all $x$ near $c$.

Maximum and minimum values together are extreme values. Local is a comparison with neighbors only. Absolute is a comparison with the whole domain.

Extreme Value Theorem
If $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a, b]$, then $f$ attains an absolute maximum value $f(c)$ and an absolute minimum value $f(d)$ at some numbers $c$ and $d$ in $[a, b]$.

Why both hypotheses. $f(x) = x$ on the open interval $(0, 1)$ has no largest value (it creeps toward $1$ but never reaches it). $f(x) = 1/x$ on $[-1, 1]$ with a discontinuity at $0$ is unbounded. Closed and continuous rule both out.

Fermat's Theorem
If $f$ has a local maximum or minimum at $c$, and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c) = 0$.

Why. At a local maximum, secant slopes to points on the right are $\le 0$ (the graph is not higher there) and to points on the left are $\ge 0$. The common limit $f'(c)$ is both $\le 0$ and $\ge 0$, so it is $0$. Picture: the tangent at a peak is horizontal.

A critical number of $f$ is a number $c$ in the domain of $f$ with $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ undefined. By Fermat, every local extreme value occurs at a critical number.

Common pitfall

The converse of Fermat is false. $f(x) = x^3$ has $f'(0) = 0$ but no maximum or minimum at $0$ (it keeps rising). And an extremum can occur where $f'$ does not exist: $|x|$ has a minimum at $0$ with no tangent line there. Critical numbers are candidates, not guarantees.

The Closed Interval Method. To find the absolute maximum and minimum values of a continuous $f$ on a closed interval $[a, b]$:

  1. Find the values of $f$ at the critical numbers of $f$ in $(a, b)$.
  2. Find the values $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ at the endpoints.
  3. The largest value from steps 1 and 2 is the absolute maximum value, the smallest is the absolute minimum value.

Why it works. By the Extreme Value Theorem the extremes exist. An extreme in the interior is a local extreme, so by Fermat it is at a critical number. Otherwise it is at an endpoint. So the list of candidates is complete.

Example. $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 1$ on $[-\tfrac12, 4]$. $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x = 3x(x - 2)$, so the critical numbers are $0$ and $2$. The values are $f(-\tfrac12) = \tfrac18$, $f(0) = 1$, $f(2) = -3$, $f(4) = 17$. The absolute maximum is $f(4) = 17$ and the absolute minimum is $f(2) = -3$.

Which candidate wins depends on the interval. $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 1$ on $[-1, b]$. For small $b$ the absolute maximum is at the critical number $0$. Once $b > 3$ the right endpoint takes over. The critical number $2$ counts only once it lies inside the interval.

3.2 The Mean Value Theorem

Rolle's Theorem
Suppose $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, differentiable on $(a, b)$, and $f(a) = f(b)$. Then there is a number $c$ in $(a, b)$ with $f'(c) = 0$.

Why. By the Extreme Value Theorem $f$ has a maximum and a minimum on $[a, b]$. If both are at the endpoints, $f$ is constant and $f' = 0$ everywhere. Otherwise one of them is in the interior, and Fermat gives $f'(c) = 0$ there.

Mean Value Theorem (MVT)
Suppose $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$. Then there is a number $c$ in $(a, b)$ with $$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.$$

Picture. Somewhere between $a$ and $b$ the tangent line is parallel to the secant line through $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$. Tilt the picture of Rolle's Theorem so the secant is no longer horizontal. Motion. If a car's average velocity over a trip is $60$ mph, then at some instant its speedometer read exactly $60$.

Tangent parallel to secant. $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on $[-2, 2.5]$ has secant slope $2.25$. Slide $c$: the tangent turns yellow where $f'(c) = 3c^2 - 3 = 2.25$, at $c = \pm\sqrt{1.75}$. There can be more than one such $c$.

Example. $f(x) = x^3 - x$ on $[0, 2]$. The secant slope is $\dfrac{6 - 0}{2 - 0} = 3$, and $f'(c) = 3c^2 - 1 = 3$ gives $c = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt 3}$, which is in $(0, 2)$.

Consequences.

  • If $f'(x) = 0$ for all $x$ in an interval $(a, b)$, then $f$ is constant on $(a, b)$. (For any $x_1 < x_2$ there, the MVT gives $f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1) = 0$.)
  • If $f'(x) = g'(x)$ for all $x$ in $(a, b)$, then $f - g$ is constant on $(a, b)$: $f(x) = g(x) + C$.
  • Bounds: if $f(0) = 1$ and $f'(x) \le 4$ for all $x$, then $f(3) - f(0) = 3f'(c) \le 12$, so $f(3) \le 13$.
Common pitfall

Check the hypotheses. $f(x) = |x|$ on $[-1, 1]$ has $f(-1) = f(1)$ but $f'(c) = 0$ nowhere, since $f$ is not differentiable at $0$. The theorems guarantee a $c$ only when $f$ is continuous on the closed interval and differentiable on the open one.

3.3 How Derivatives Affect the Shape of a Graph

Shape$f$ is increasing on an interval if $x_1 < x_2$ implies $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$ there, and decreasing if $x_1 < x_2$ implies $f(x_1) > f(x_2)$.

Increasing/Decreasing Test. If $f'(x) > 0$ on an interval, $f$ is increasing there. If $f'(x) < 0$ on an interval, $f$ is decreasing there. Why. By the MVT, $f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1)$, which has the sign of $f'(c)$.

First Derivative Test. Let $c$ be a critical number of a continuous $f$.

  • If $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $c$, then $f$ has a local maximum at $c$.
  • If $f'$ changes from negative to positive at $c$, then $f$ has a local minimum at $c$.
  • If $f'$ has the same sign on both sides of $c$, then $f$ has no local extremum at $c$.

Picture. Up then down is a peak, down then up is a valley.

Concavity. $f$ is concave upward on an interval if its graph lies above all of its tangent lines there, and concave downward if the graph lies below all of its tangent lines there. An inflection point is a point $(c, f(c))$ on a continuous curve where the concavity changes (from upward to downward or the reverse).

Concavity Test. If $f''(x) > 0$ on an interval, $f$ is concave upward there. If $f''(x) < 0$, concave downward. Why. $f'' > 0$ means $f'$ is increasing, so the tangent slopes keep growing, and the graph turns left (upward) away from each tangent line.

Tangent below, tangent above. $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ has $f''(x) = 6x$. On the green part ($x > 0$) each tangent lies below the graph, on the pink part ($x < 0$) above it. At the inflection point $(0, 0)$ the tangent cuts through the graph.

Second Derivative Test. Suppose $f''$ is continuous near $c$ and $f'(c) = 0$. If $f''(c) > 0$, $f$ has a local minimum at $c$ (concave up, a valley). If $f''(c) < 0$, a local maximum. If $f''(c) = 0$, the test says nothing.

Example. $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3$. $f'(x) = 4x^3 - 12x^2 = 4x^2(x - 3)$, critical numbers $0$ and $3$. Since $4x^2 \ge 0$, the sign of $f'$ is the sign of $x - 3$: $f$ is decreasing on $(-\infty, 3)$ and increasing on $(3, \infty)$. So $f(3) = -27$ is a local (in fact absolute) minimum and there is no extremum at $0$. $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 24x = 12x(x - 2)$: concave upward on $(-\infty, 0)$ and $(2, \infty)$, downward on $(0, 2)$, with inflection points $(0, 0)$ and $(2, -16)$. Note $f''(0) = 0$, so the Second Derivative Test is silent at $0$, while the First Derivative Test answers.

Common pitfall

$f''(c) = 0$ does not make $(c, f(c))$ an inflection point. $f(x) = x^4$ has $f''(0) = 0$ but is concave upward on both sides. Check that $f''$ actually changes sign. Also, increasing/decreasing and concavity are properties of intervals of $x$, so answer with intervals, not points.

3.4 Limits at Infinity; Horizontal Asymptotes

Concept$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ means $f(x)$ can be made as close to $L$ as we like by taking $x$ large enough. Similarly $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L$ for $x$ large and negative. The line $y = L$ is a horizontal asymptote of $y = f(x)$ if $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ or $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L$.

Basic fact. For $r > 0$, $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{1}{x^r} = 0$ (and the same as $x \to -\infty$ when $x^r$ is defined). Why. Dividing $1$ by a huge number gives a tiny one.

Rational functions. Divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator, then use the basic fact.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{3x^2 - x}{5x^2 + 2} = \lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{3 - 1/x}{5 + 2/x^2} = \frac{3}{5}.$

In general, with $p$ the degree of the numerator and $q$ the degree of the denominator: if $p < q$ the limit is $0$, if $p = q$ it is the ratio of leading coefficients, and if $p > q$ it is $\infty$ or $-\infty$ (no horizontal asymptote). Why. For large $|x|$ each polynomial is dominated by its leading term.

Zoom out. $f(x) = \dfrac{2x^2 + 3x}{x^2 + 1}$ approaches $y = 2$ as $x \to \pm\infty$. Near the origin it crosses that line at $x = \tfrac23$.

Roots. Use $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$, which is $x$ for $x > 0$ and $-x$ for $x < 0$. Example: for $x < 0$, $\dfrac{\sqrt{4x^2 + 1}}{x + 3} = \dfrac{|x|\sqrt{4 + 1/x^2}}{x(1 + 3/x)} = \dfrac{-\sqrt{4 + 1/x^2}}{1 + 3/x}$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty}\frac{\sqrt{4x^2 + 1}}{x + 3} = -2$, while the limit as $x \to \infty$ is $2$.

Oscillation. $\lim_{x \to \infty}\sin x$ does not exist. But $\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ is squeezed between $-\dfrac1x$ and $\dfrac1x$, so it approaches $0$.

Common pitfall

A graph may cross a horizontal asymptote, as in the figure. An asymptote describes the behavior for large $|x|$ only. And $\infty - \infty$ is not $0$: rewrite first, e.g. $\sqrt{x^2 + x} - x = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + x} + x} \to \tfrac12$.

3.5 Curve Sketching

ChecklistTo sketch $y = f(x)$ by hand, collect:

  1. Domain. Where is $f$ defined?
  2. Intercepts. $y$-intercept $f(0)$, $x$-intercepts from $f(x) = 0$.
  3. Symmetry. Even ($f(-x) = f(x)$, mirror in the $y$-axis) or odd ($f(-x) = -f(x)$, symmetric about the origin).
  4. Asymptotes. Vertical (where a denominator is $0$ and the numerator is not), horizontal ($\lim_{x \to \pm\infty}$).
  5. Increasing/decreasing from the sign of $f'$, and local extrema by the First Derivative Test.
  6. Concavity from the sign of $f''$, and inflection points.
  7. Sketch. Plot the special points, draw the asymptotes, and join them following the shape information.

Example. $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2}{x^2 - 4}$.

  • Domain: $x \neq \pm 2$. Intercept: $(0, 0)$ only. Even, so symmetric about the $y$-axis.
  • Vertical asymptotes $x = \pm 2$. Horizontal asymptote $y = 1$ (equal degrees, leading coefficients $1$ and $1$).
  • $f'(x) = \dfrac{2x(x^2 - 4) - x^2\cdot 2x}{(x^2 - 4)^2} = \dfrac{-8x}{(x^2 - 4)^2}$. Increasing on $(-\infty, -2)$ and $(-2, 0)$, decreasing on $(0, 2)$ and $(2, \infty)$. Local maximum $f(0) = 0$.
  • $f''(x) = \dfrac{8(3x^2 + 4)}{(x^2 - 4)^3}$. The numerator is always positive, so $f''$ has the sign of $(x^2 - 4)^3$: concave upward for $|x| > 2$, downward for $|x| < 2$. No inflection points, since $x = \pm 2$ are not in the domain.
Sign charts

To find the sign of $f'$ (or $f''$), factor it, mark its zeros and undefined points on a number line, and test one number in each piece. The sign is constant on each piece because $f'$ can only change sign where it is $0$ or undefined (by the IVT, if it is continuous between them).

3.6 Optimization Problems

ApplicationAn optimization problem asks for the largest or smallest value of a quantity. The method turns words into a function of one variable on an interval, then applies 3.1 or 3.3.

  1. Draw a picture and name the quantities with variables.
  2. Write the quantity $Q$ to be maximized or minimized (the objective) in terms of the variables.
  3. Use the given conditions (the constraint) to write $Q$ as a function of one variable, and find that variable's domain.
  4. Find the absolute max or min of $Q$: the Closed Interval Method on a closed domain, or the First Derivative Test (one critical number, with $Q'$ changing sign there, gives an absolute extremum).
  5. Answer the question asked, with units.

Example (open box). From a $12$ by $12$ inch sheet, cut a square of side $x$ from each corner and fold up the sides. The box has base $(12 - 2x)$ by $(12 - 2x)$ and height $x$, so $V(x) = x(12 - 2x)^2$ for $0 \le x \le 6$. $V'(x) = (12 - 2x)^2 - 4x(12 - 2x) = (12 - 2x)(12 - 6x)$, which is $0$ at $x = 2$ and $x = 6$. Values: $V(0) = 0$, $V(2) = 128$, $V(6) = 0$. The largest box has $x = 2$ in and volume $128$ in³.

Too shallow or too narrow. Small $x$ gives a flat box, large $x$ a tall thin one. The volume $V(x) = x(12 - 2x)^2$ peaks in between, at $x = 2$.

Example (fence along a river). $1200$ ft of fence encloses a rectangle on three sides, the river forming the fourth. With $x$ the two sides perpendicular to the river and $y$ the side parallel, the constraint is $2x + y = 1200$ and the area is $A = xy = x(1200 - 2x)$ for $0 \le x \le 600$. $A'(x) = 1200 - 4x = 0$ at $x = 300$, and $A(0) = A(600) = 0$, so the maximum area is $A(300) = 180{,}000$ ft² with $y = 600$ ft.

Common pitfall

A critical number is not automatically the answer. Verify that it gives a maximum (or minimum), by comparing with endpoints or by a derivative test, and make sure it is in the domain the picture allows.

3.7 Antiderivatives

BackwardA function $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ on an interval $I$ if $F'(x) = f(x)$ for all $x$ in $I$.

Theorem
If $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ on an interval $I$, then the most general antiderivative of $f$ on $I$ is $F(x) + C$, where $C$ is an arbitrary constant.

Why. If $G' = f = F'$ on $I$, then $G - F$ is constant by the MVT consequence in 3.2. Picture: the graphs of all antiderivatives are vertical shifts of one another, with the same slope at each $x$.

One slope pattern, many curves. The purple segments have slope $x^2 - 1$ at each point. Every curve $F(x) = \tfrac{x^3}{3} - x + C$ follows them. Fixing one value, such as $F(0)$, picks out one curve.

Antiderivative table (read the derivative table backward, with $n \neq -1$):

$x^n \mapsto \dfrac{x^{n + 1}}{n + 1}, \quad \cos x \mapsto \sin x, \quad \sin x \mapsto -\cos x, \quad \sec^2 x \mapsto \tan x, \quad \sec x\tan x \mapsto \sec x,$

each plus $C$. Constants multiply through and sums split term by term. Check any antiderivative by differentiating it.

Example. The most general antiderivative of $f(x) = 6x^2 - \dfrac{3}{\sqrt x} + \cos 2x$ is $F(x) = 2x^3 - 6\sqrt x + \tfrac12\sin 2x + C$. (Check: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\tfrac12\sin 2x = \cos 2x$ by the chain rule.)

Initial value problems. Given $f'$ and one value of $f$, find $f$. Example: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 4x$, $f(1) = 2$. Then $f(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 + C$ and $1 - 2 + C = 2$, so $C = 3$ and $f(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 + 3$. Given $f''$, antidifferentiate twice with two constants: $f''(x) = 6x$, $f'(0) = 2$, $f(0) = 1$ give $f'(x) = 3x^2 + 2$ and $f(x) = x^3 + 2x + 1$.

Motion. With position $s$, velocity $v = s'$, and acceleration $a = v'$: velocity is an antiderivative of acceleration and position an antiderivative of velocity. A ball thrown up at $20$ m/s from height $5$ m, with $a = -10$ m/s², has $v(t) = -10t + 20$ and $s(t) = -5t^2 + 20t + 5$.

Common pitfall

Forgetting $+ C$, and antidifferentiating a product or quotient factor by factor. There is no product rule for antiderivatives: an antiderivative of $\dfrac{x^3 + \sqrt x}{x}$ comes from first splitting it as $x^2 + x^{-1/2}$, giving $\tfrac{x^3}{3} + 2\sqrt x + C$.

Worked example

A complete analysis. $f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x$ on $[0, 4]$.
$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 3(x - 1)(x - 3)$, critical numbers $1$ and $3$. Sign chart: $f' > 0$ on $(0, 1)$, $f' < 0$ on $(1, 3)$, $f' > 0$ on $(3, 4)$. So $f$ has a local maximum $f(1) = 4$ and a local minimum $f(3) = 0$.
$f''(x) = 6x - 12$, negative for $x < 2$ and positive for $x > 2$: concave downward on $(0, 2)$, upward on $(2, 4)$, with inflection point $(2, 2)$. The Second Derivative Test agrees: $f''(1) = -6 < 0$ (max) and $f''(3) = 6 > 0$ (min).
Closed Interval Method: $f(0) = 0$, $f(1) = 4$, $f(3) = 0$, $f(4) = 4$. Absolute maximum $4$ (at $x = 1$ and $x = 4$), absolute minimum $0$ (at $x = 0$ and $x = 3$).
MVT on $[0, 4]$: the secant slope is $\tfrac{4 - 0}{4} = 1$, and $3c^2 - 12c + 9 = 1$ gives $c = 2 \pm \tfrac{2}{\sqrt 3}$, both in $(0, 4)$.

Practice problems

  1. Find the absolute maximum and minimum values of $f(x) = x^3 - 12x + 1$ on $[-3, 5]$.
  2. Find the critical numbers of $f(x) = x^{2/3}(x - 5)$ and classify each.
  3. Verify that $f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 1$ satisfies the hypotheses of the MVT on $[0, 3]$ and find every $c$ the theorem promises.
  4. For $f(x) = 3x^4 - 4x^3$, find the intervals of increase and decrease, the local extrema, the intervals of concavity, and the inflection points.
  5. Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{4x^3 - x}{2x^3 + 7}$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty}\frac{\sqrt{9x^2 + 1}}{2x - 1}$.
  6. Sketch $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{x^2 + 1}$ using the checklist.
  7. An open box with a square base must have volume $32$ ft³. Find the dimensions that use the least material.
  8. Find $f$ if $f'(x) = 4x^3 - \sin x$ and $f(0) = 3$.

Strategies

Absolute vs local. Absolute extrema on a closed interval: Closed Interval Method (critical numbers and endpoints). Local extrema: First or Second Derivative Test at the critical numbers.

Finding critical numbers. Factor $f'$. Include numbers in the domain where $f'$ is undefined (e.g. where a fractional power has $0$ in the denominator of its derivative).

Sign charts. Mark where $f'$ (or $f''$) is $0$ or undefined, test one point per piece, read off the shape.

Second Derivative Test or First? The second is quick when $f''$ is easy and nonzero at $c$. The first always works at a critical number and also handles points where $f'$ is undefined.

Limits at infinity. Divide by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator. With roots, remember $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$, which matters as $x \to -\infty$.

Optimization. Picture, objective, constraint, one-variable function with its domain, then verify the maximum or minimum.

Antiderivatives. Rewrite as a sum of powers and basic trig functions, antidifferentiate each term, add $C$, and check by differentiating. Use the given values to find the constants.
