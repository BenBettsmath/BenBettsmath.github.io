Chapter 7 · Techniques of Integration

← previousoutlinenext →

Methods for finding antiderivatives that substitution alone cannot reach, a way to estimate integrals we cannot do exactly, and integrals over infinite intervals or of unbounded functions. Each technique undoes a differentiation rule or exploits an identity. By parts undoes the product rule, trig substitution uses the Pythagorean identities, and partial fractions breaks a rational function into pieces we already know how to integrate.

7.1 Integration by Parts

The rule. For differentiable functions $u = f(x)$ and $v = g(x)$,

$\displaystyle \int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du, \qquad \int_a^b f(x)g'(x)\,dx = f(x)g(x)\Big|_a^b - \int_a^b g(x)f'(x)\,dx.$

Why. The product rule says $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$. Integrate both sides and solve for $\int fg'$. In a picture, let the point $(u, v)$ run along a curve from $(u_1, v_1)$ to $(u_2, v_2)$ with both coordinates increasing. The area under the curve, $\int v\,du$, and the area to its left, $\int u\,dv$, together fill the big rectangle $u_2v_2$ minus the small one $u_1v_1$.

$\int u\,dv + \int v\,du = u_2v_2 - u_1v_1$.Slide the right endpoint $u_2$. The two shaded areas always add up to the difference of the rectangles.

Choosing $u$ and $dv$. Pick $u$ so that $du$ is simpler than $u$, and $dv$ so that $v$ is easy to find. A rough order of preference for $u$ is LIATE: Logarithms, Inverse trig, Algebraic (polynomials), Trig, Exponentials.

Examples.

  • $\int xe^x\,dx$: $u = x$, $dv = e^x dx$, so $du = dx$, $v = e^x$, and $\int xe^x\,dx = xe^x - \int e^x\,dx = (x - 1)e^x + C$.
  • $\int \ln x\,dx$: $u = \ln x$, $dv = dx$, so $du = dx/x$, $v = x$, and $\int\ln x\,dx = x\ln x - \int 1\,dx = x\ln x - x + C$. The same choice handles $\int\tan^{-1}x\,dx$.
  • $\int x^2\sin x\,dx$ needs two rounds, each lowering the power of $x$: $-x^2\cos x + 2x\sin x + 2\cos x + C$.
  • $\int e^x\sin x\,dx$ returns to itself after two rounds: $I = e^x\sin x - e^x\cos x - I$, so $I = \tfrac12e^x(\sin x - \cos x) + C$.
Shortcut

For a polynomial times $e^{ax}$, $\sin ax$, or $\cos ax$, a table does repeated parts at once. List the derivatives of the polynomial down to $0$ in one column and repeated antiderivatives of the other factor in the next, then add the diagonal products with alternating signs $+, -, +, \dots$

Warning

The minus sign in $-\int v\,du$ applies to the whole integral, and with repeated parts the signs alternate. A bad choice of $u$ is easy to spot: in $\int xe^x\,dx$, taking $u = e^x$ gives $\int \tfrac12x^2e^x\,dx$, which is worse. Switch the choice when the new integral is harder.

7.2 Trigonometric Integrals

Powers of sine and cosine, $\int\sin^m x\cos^n x\,dx$ with $m, n \ge 0$ integers.

  • If $n$ is odd: save one $\cos x$, write the rest with $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$, and let $u = \sin x$.
  • If $m$ is odd: save one $\sin x$, write the rest with $\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$, and let $u = \cos x$.
  • If both are even: use the half-angle identities $\sin^2 x = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2x)$, $\cos^2 x = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2x)$, and sometimes $\sin x\cos x = \tfrac12\sin 2x$.

Why. The saved factor becomes $du$, and the Pythagorean identity turns everything else into powers of $u$. When both powers are even there is nothing to save, so we lower the powers by doubling the angle instead.

Example. $\int\sin^3x\cos^2x\,dx = \int(1 - \cos^2 x)\cos^2 x\,\sin x\,dx$. With $u = \cos x$, $du = -\sin x\,dx$, this is $-\int(u^2 - u^4)\,du = -\tfrac13\cos^3x + \tfrac15\cos^5x + C$.

Example. Both powers even:

$\displaystyle\int_0^\pi\sin^2x\,dx = \frac12\int_0^\pi(1 - \cos 2x)\,dx = \frac12\Bigl[x - \frac12\sin 2x\Bigr]_0^\pi = \frac{\pi}{2}.$

Powers of tangent and secant, $\int\tan^m x\sec^n x\,dx$.

  • If $n$ is even ($n \ge 2$): save $\sec^2 x$, write the rest with $\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x$, and let $u = \tan x$.
  • If $m$ is odd: save $\sec x\tan x$, write the rest with $\tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1$, and let $u = \sec x$.

The same reason applies, since $\frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec^2 x$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\sec x = \sec x\tan x$. Two integrals worth knowing:

$\displaystyle\int\tan x\,dx = \ln|\sec x| + C, \qquad \int\sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + C.$

The second comes from multiplying by $\dfrac{\sec x + \tan x}{\sec x + \tan x}$. The numerator becomes the derivative of the denominator.

Products with different angles. For $\int\sin mx\cos nx\,dx$ and similar, use

$\sin A\cos B = \tfrac12[\sin(A - B) + \sin(A + B)], \quad \sin A\sin B = \tfrac12[\cos(A - B) - \cos(A + B)], \quad \cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)].$
Warning

Keep the factor from the doubled angle. $\int\cos 2x\,dx = \tfrac12\sin 2x + C$, not $\sin 2x + C$. Also $\sin^2 x$ is $(\sin x)^2$, while $\sin x^2$ is $\sin(x^2)$, which has no elementary antiderivative.

7.3 Trigonometric Substitution

The idea. A root of the form $\sqrt{a^2 \pm x^2}$ or $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$ (with $a > 0$) disappears if $x$ is replaced by a trig function of a new variable $\theta$, because of a Pythagorean identity. This is an inverse substitution: we set $x = g(\theta)$ rather than $u = g(x)$, so $g$ must be one-to-one, and that is what the restriction on $\theta$ ensures.

$\begin{array}{lll} \text{expression} & \text{substitution} & \text{identity}\\ \hline \sqrt{a^2 - x^2} & x = a\sin\theta,\ \ -\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2} & 1 - \sin^2\theta = \cos^2\theta\\ \sqrt{a^2 + x^2} & x = a\tan\theta,\ \ -\tfrac{\pi}{2} < \theta < \tfrac{\pi}{2} & 1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta\\ \sqrt{x^2 - a^2} & x = a\sec\theta,\ \ 0 \le \theta < \tfrac{\pi}{2} \text{ or } \pi \le \theta < \tfrac{3\pi}{2} & \sec^2\theta - 1 = \tan^2\theta \end{array}$

On each range of $\theta$ the trig function the root simplifies to ($\cos\theta$, $\sec\theta$, or $\tan\theta$) is $\ge 0$, so for example $\sqrt{a^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta} = a\cos\theta$ with no absolute value.

Back to $x$. Draw the right triangle that encodes the substitution, with angle $\theta$, and label the sides by Pythagoras. Every trig function of $\theta$ in the answer can be read off as a ratio of sides in terms of $x$.

The reference triangle for each substitution.Choose the form of the root. The third side always comes from Pythagoras.

Example. $\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{x^2 + 4}}$. Let $x = 2\tan\theta$, so $dx = 2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{x^2 + 4} = 2\sec\theta$. Then

$\displaystyle\int\frac{2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta}{4\tan^2\theta\cdot 2\sec\theta} = \frac14\int\frac{\sec\theta}{\tan^2\theta}\,d\theta = \frac14\int\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin^2\theta}\,d\theta = -\frac{1}{4\sin\theta} + C.$

The triangle has opposite side $x$, adjacent side $2$, hypotenuse $\sqrt{x^2 + 4}$, so $\sin\theta = x/\sqrt{x^2 + 4}$ and the answer is $-\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2 + 4}}{4x} + C$.

Completing the square. A quadratic $x^2 + bx + c$ under a root becomes one of the three forms after completing the square. For example $\sqrt{x^2 + 2x + 5} = \sqrt{(x + 1)^2 + 4}$, and then $x + 1 = 2\tan\theta$.

Warning

Do not stop with an answer in $\theta$. Convert back with the triangle, and if $\theta$ itself appears, use $\theta = \sin^{-1}(x/a)$ (or $\tan^{-1}$, $\sec^{-1}$). For a definite integral, either convert back or change the limits to $\theta$ values, but not a mix. Also check whether plain substitution works first: $\int\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 4}}\,dx$ only needs $u = x^2 + 4$.

7.4 Integration of Rational Functions by Partial Fractions

Setup. A rational function is $R(x) = P(x)/Q(x)$ with $P$, $Q$ polynomials. It is proper if $\deg P < \deg Q$. If it is not proper, divide first: long division gives $R(x) = S(x) + \frac{\tilde P(x)}{Q(x)}$ with $S$ a polynomial and $\deg\tilde P < \deg Q$ (the tilde is our notation for the remainder).

Next factor $Q$ into linear factors $ax + b$ and irreducible quadratics $ax^2 + bx + c$, meaning $b^2 - 4ac < 0$, so they have no real roots. Then the proper fraction is a sum of the following pieces.

  • Distinct linear factors $(x - r)$: one term $\dfrac{A}{x - r}$ each.
  • Repeated linear factor $(x - r)^k$: $\dfrac{A_1}{x - r} + \dfrac{A_2}{(x - r)^2} + \cdots + \dfrac{A_k}{(x - r)^k}$.
  • Irreducible quadratic $ax^2 + bx + c$: one term $\dfrac{Ax + B}{ax^2 + bx + c}$. A repeated quadratic gets one such term for each power, as in the linear case.

Why it works. Algebra guarantees such a decomposition exists, and each piece has a known integral: $\frac{A}{x - r}$ gives $A\ln|x - r|$, higher powers give powers of $x - r$, and a quadratic piece gives a logarithm plus an arctangent.

$\displaystyle\int\frac{Ax + B}{x^2 + a^2}\,dx = \frac{A}{2}\ln(x^2 + a^2) + \frac{B}{a}\tan^{-1}\frac{x}{a} + C.$

To find the constants, multiply through by $Q(x)$ and either compare coefficients or plug in convenient values of $x$. For a distinct linear factor $x - r$, plugging in $x = r$ kills every other term, so $A$ is the value at $r$ of $(x - r)R(x)$ (the cover-up method).

Example. $\displaystyle\int\frac{x + 5}{x^2 + x - 2}\,dx$. Here $x^2 + x - 2 = (x - 1)(x + 2)$, so $\dfrac{x + 5}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} = \dfrac{A}{x - 1} + \dfrac{B}{x + 2}$. Cover-up: $A = \frac{1 + 5}{1 + 2} = 2$ and $B = \frac{-2 + 5}{-2 - 1} = -1$. So the integral is $2\ln|x - 1| - \ln|x + 2| + C$.

Rationalizing substitutions. An integrand with $\sqrt[n]{g(x)}$ can sometimes be made rational by $u = \sqrt[n]{g(x)}$. Example: in $\int\frac{dx}{1 + \sqrt x}$, put $u = \sqrt x$, so $x = u^2$ and $dx = 2u\,du$.

Warning

Three common setup errors. Skipping long division when $\deg P \ge \deg Q$. Giving a repeated factor $(x - r)^2$ only the term $\frac{A}{(x - r)^2}$ and forgetting $\frac{B}{x - r}$. Giving a quadratic factor a constant numerator $A$ instead of $Ax + B$.

7.5 Strategy for Integration

No single method always works. A reasonable order of attack:

  1. Simplify. Expand, cancel, split a fraction, or use an identity. $\int\frac{\tan\theta}{\sec^2\theta}\,d\theta = \int\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta$.
  2. Look for an obvious substitution. A function and (a constant multiple of) its derivative both appear, such as $\int\frac{x}{x^2 - 1}\,dx$ with $u = x^2 - 1$.
  3. Classify the integrand. Powers of trig functions: 7.2. Rational function: 7.4. Product of two different kinds of function: by parts, 7.1. A root of $\pm x^2 \pm a^2$: 7.3. A root of a linear expression: rationalizing substitution.
  4. Try again. Try a different substitution, multiply by a clever form of $1$, or combine methods (a substitution followed by parts is common: $\int e^{\sqrt x}\,dx$ with $u = \sqrt x$ becomes $\int 2ue^u\,du$).

Not every integral has an elementary answer. A function is elementary if it is built from polynomials, roots, exponentials, logarithms, trig and inverse trig functions by arithmetic and composition. The antiderivatives of $e^{x^2}$, $\sin(x^2)$, $\frac{\sin x}{x}$, $\frac{1}{\ln x}$, and $\sqrt{1 + x^3}$ exist (the integrands are continuous on their domains) but are not elementary. For definite integrals of these we approximate.

7.7 Approximate Integration

The rules. Divide $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals of width $\Delta x = (b - a)/n$, with endpoints $x_i = a + i\,\Delta x$ and midpoints $\bar x_i = \tfrac12(x_{i-1} + x_i)$.

$\begin{aligned} L_n &= \Delta x\,[f(x_0) + f(x_1) + \cdots + f(x_{n-1})] &&\text{left endpoints}\\ R_n &= \Delta x\,[f(x_1) + f(x_2) + \cdots + f(x_n)] &&\text{right endpoints}\\ M_n &= \Delta x\,[f(\bar x_1) + f(\bar x_2) + \cdots + f(\bar x_n)] &&\text{midpoints}\\ T_n &= \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}\,[f(x_0) + 2f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + \cdots + 2f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)] &&\text{trapezoids}\\ S_n &= \tfrac{\Delta x}{3}\,[f(x_0) + 4f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + 4f(x_3) + \cdots + 4f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)] &&\text{Simpson, } n \text{ even} \end{aligned}$

Where they come from. $T_n = \tfrac12(L_n + R_n)$ replaces $f$ on each subinterval by the chord. Simpson's rule replaces $f$ on each pair of subintervals by the parabola through three points, which is why $n$ must be even. It also equals $S_{2n} = \tfrac13T_n + \tfrac23M_n$.

Over or under?

  • If $f$ is increasing on $[a, b]$, then $L_n \le \int_a^b f\,dx \le R_n$. Each left rectangle sits below the graph and each right rectangle reaches above it. If $f$ is decreasing, the inequalities reverse.
  • If $f$ is concave up on $[a, b]$ ($f'' \ge 0$), then $M_n \le \int_a^b f\,dx \le T_n$. The chords lie above the graph, so trapezoids overestimate. A midpoint rectangle has the same area as the trapezoid under the tangent line at the midpoint, and that tangent lies below the graph. If $f$ is concave down, the inequalities reverse.
Five rules, with the error shaded.Red marks where the shape is above the curve, green where it is below. Pick a function and a rule, and slide $n$. Simpson rounds $n$ up to the next even number.

Error bounds. Let $E_T$, $E_M$, $E_S$ denote the exact integral minus $T_n$, $M_n$, $S_n$. If $|f''(x)| \le K$ on $[a, b]$, then

$|E_T| \le \dfrac{K(b - a)^3}{12n^2}, \qquad |E_M| \le \dfrac{K(b - a)^3}{24n^2}.$

If $|f^{(4)}(x)| \le K$ on $[a, b]$, then $|E_S| \le \dfrac{K(b - a)^5}{180n^4}$.

Why the powers of $n$. On one subinterval the chord misses a curve of bounded bending by an area of size about $(\Delta x)^3$. There are $n$ subintervals, so the total is about $n(\Delta x)^3 \sim 1/n^2$. Doubling $n$ cuts the trapezoid error by about $4$ and the Simpson error by about $16$. The midpoint bound is half the trapezoid bound, matching the picture where the midpoint error is the smaller one.

Example. How large must $n$ be for $T_n$ to approximate $\int_1^3\frac{dx}{x}$ within $0.001$? Here $f''(x) = 2/x^3 \le 2$ on $[1, 3]$, so take $K = 2$. We need $\dfrac{2\cdot 2^3}{12n^2} = \dfrac{4}{3n^2} \le 0.001$, that is $n^2 \ge 1333.\overline{3}$. So $n = 37$ is enough ($36^2 = 1296$ is too small).

Warning

$K$ must bound $|f''|$ (or $|f^{(4)}|$) on the whole interval, so take the largest value of $|f''|$, not its value at one point. Simpson's rule with odd $n$ is undefined. And the error bound gives a guaranteed $n$, not the smallest $n$ that happens to work.

7.8 Improper Integrals

Type 1: infinite interval. If $\int_a^t f(x)\,dx$ exists for every $t \ge a$, define

$\displaystyle\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^t f(x)\,dx,$

and similarly $\int_{-\infty}^b f\,dx = \lim_{t\to-\infty}\int_t^b f\,dx$. The integral is convergent if the limit exists as a finite number and divergent otherwise. For $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f\,dx$ pick any $c$ and write it as $\int_{-\infty}^c f\,dx + \int_c^\infty f\,dx$. It converges only if both pieces converge.

The $p$-integrals.

$\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^p} \ \text{ converges (to } \tfrac{1}{p - 1}\text{) if } p > 1 \text{ and diverges if } p \le 1.$

Why. For $p \ne 1$, $\int_1^t x^{-p}\,dx = \dfrac{t^{1 - p} - 1}{1 - p}$, and $t^{1-p} \to 0$ exactly when $1 - p < 0$. For $p = 1$ the integral is $\ln t \to \infty$. The curve $1/x$ goes to $0$, but not fast enough to enclose finite area.

Type 2: unbounded integrand. If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b)$ and unbounded near $b$, define $\int_a^b f\,dx = \lim_{t\to b^-}\int_a^t f\,dx$. Similarly at $a$ with $t \to a^+$. If the bad point $c$ is inside $(a, b)$, split: $\int_a^b = \int_a^c + \int_c^b$, and both must converge. The mirror image of the $p$-integral result is

$\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^p} \ \text{ converges (to } \tfrac{1}{1 - p}\text{) if } p < 1 \text{ and diverges if } p \ge 1.$
Finite or infinite area under $1/x^p$.Push the bound toward $\infty$ (type 1) or toward $0$ (type 2) and watch whether the area settles. Change $p$ across $1$.

Examples. Using parts, and then L'Hôpital for $te^{-t} \to 0$,

$\displaystyle\int_0^\infty xe^{-x}\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\bigl[-(x + 1)e^{-x}\bigr]_0^t = \lim_{t\to\infty}\bigl(1 - (t + 1)e^{-t}\bigr) = 1.$

And $\int_{-1}^8 x^{-2/3}\,dx$ is improper at $0$. Since $\int x^{-2/3}\,dx = 3x^{1/3}$, the piece on $[-1, 0]$ is $\lim_{t \to 0^-}(3t^{1/3} + 3) = 3$ and the piece on $[0, 8]$ is $\lim_{t\to 0^+}(6 - 3t^{1/3}) = 6$. Total $9$.

Comparison theorem. Suppose $f$ and $g$ are continuous with $f(x) \ge g(x) \ge 0$ for $x \ge a$.

  • If $\int_a^\infty f\,dx$ converges, then $\int_a^\infty g\,dx$ converges.
  • If $\int_a^\infty g\,dx$ diverges, then $\int_a^\infty f\,dx$ diverges.

Why. The region under $g$ sits inside the region under $f$. A finite area cannot contain an infinite one. The same holds for type 2 integrals.

Comparison.$e^{-x^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative, but its area on $[1, \infty)$ sits inside the finite area under $e^{-x}$.
Warning

Look for hidden bad points before using the Fundamental Theorem. $\int_{-1}^1\frac{dx}{x^2}$ is not $\bigl[-\tfrac1x\bigr]_{-1}^1 = -2$ (a negative area for a positive function). The integrand blows up at $0$, and $\int_0^1 x^{-2}\,dx$ diverges, so the whole integral diverges.

Also $\int_{-\infty}^\infty x\,dx$ diverges, even though $\int_{-t}^t x\,dx = 0$ for every $t$. The two halves must converge separately.

In the comparison theorem, being smaller than a divergent integral, or bigger than a convergent one, tells us nothing.

Practice problems

  1. Evaluate $\int x\cos 3x\,dx$ and $\int_1^e x^2\ln x\,dx$.
  2. Evaluate $\int e^{2x}\cos x\,dx$.
  3. Evaluate $\int\sin^2x\cos^3x\,dx$ and $\int\cos^4x\,dx$.
  4. Evaluate $\int\tan^3x\sec^4x\,dx$.
  5. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int\frac{\sqrt{9 - x^2}}{x^2}\,dx$ and $\displaystyle\int_0^{1}\frac{dx}{(x^2 + 1)^{3/2}}$.
  6. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int\frac{3x - 1}{x^2 - x - 6}\,dx$ and $\displaystyle\int\frac{x^2 + 1}{x(x - 1)^2}\,dx$.
  7. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int\frac{x^3}{x^2 + 4}\,dx$ (divide first).
  8. Compute $T_4$ and $M_4$ for $\int_0^2 e^{x^2}\,dx$. Which is an overestimate, and why?
  9. Decide convergence, and evaluate when convergent: $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^3}$, $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}$, $\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1 + x^2}$, $\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x\ln x}$.
  10. Use comparison to decide whether $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{2 + \sin x}{x}\,dx$ converges.
