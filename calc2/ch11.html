Chapter 11 · Sequences and Series

← previousoutlinenext →

Infinite lists of numbers, infinite sums, and functions written as "polynomials of infinite degree". The chapter has two halves. First, tests that decide whether an infinite sum adds up to a finite number. Second, power series and Taylor series, which use those tests to represent $e^x$, $\sin x$, $\ln(1 + x)$ and many other functions as series. The guiding picture is the sequence of partial sums: a series converges exactly when its partial sums settle down to a limit.

11.1 Sequences

Sequence. A sequence $\{a_n\}$ is an ordered list $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$, one term $a_n$ for each positive integer $n$ (sometimes starting at $n = 0$). Equivalently it is a function $a$ whose domain is the positive integers, with $a_n = a(n)$.

Limit. $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n = L$ means: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is an integer $N$ such that $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ whenever $n > N$. Then $\{a_n\}$ converges to $L$. Otherwise it diverges. In words, every band of half-width $\varepsilon$ around $L$, however thin, eventually contains all the terms.

The $\varepsilon$ band.Shrink $\varepsilon$. For a convergent sequence the cutoff index moves right but always exists. For $(-1)^n$ no band of half-width less than $1$ works around any $L$.

Theorem (sequences from functions). If $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x) = L$ and $a_n = f(n)$ for integers $n$, then $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n = L$. This lets us use L'Hôpital's rule: $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\ln n}{n} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1/x}{1} = 0$.

Tools. The limit laws and the squeeze theorem hold for sequences. Also:

  • If $\lim|a_n| = 0$, then $\lim a_n = 0$ (squeeze $-|a_n| \le a_n \le |a_n|$).
  • If $a_n \to L$ and $f$ is continuous at $L$, then $f(a_n) \to f(L)$.
  • $\{r^n\}$ converges to $0$ if $-1 < r < 1$, to $1$ if $r = 1$, and diverges for all other $r$.

Monotone and bounded. $\{a_n\}$ is increasing if $a_n < a_{n+1}$ for all $n$, decreasing if $a_n > a_{n+1}$ for all $n$, and monotonic if it is one or the other. It is bounded above if some $M$ has $a_n \le M$ for all $n$, bounded below if some $m$ has $a_n \ge m$ for all $n$, and bounded if both.

Monotonic sequence theorem. Every bounded monotonic sequence converges. Why. An increasing sequence bounded above cannot jump past its bound, so its terms crowd toward the smallest number that is not exceeded by any term. That number is the limit. This is a property of the real numbers (completeness), and it is how we know a sequence converges before we know its limit.

Examples. $\dfrac{n}{n + 1} = \dfrac{1}{1 + 1/n} \to 1$. $\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n} \to 0$ since $\bigl|\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\bigr| = \frac1n \to 0$. $(-1)^n$ diverges, alternating between $1$ and $-1$. $\bigl(1 + \frac1n\bigr)^n \to e$ (6.4).

Warning

L'Hôpital's rule does not apply to a sequence directly, since $a_n$ is defined only at integers and has no derivative. Apply it to the function $f(x)$ with $f(n) = a_n$, then use the theorem above. Also, the converse of that theorem fails: $\sin(\pi n) = 0$ for every integer $n$, but $\lim_{x\to\infty}\sin(\pi x)$ does not exist.

11.2 Series

Series and partial sums. Given a sequence $\{a_n\}$, the series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots$ is the "infinite sum" of its terms. Its $n$th partial sum is $s_n = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i = a_1 + \cdots + a_n$. If the sequence $\{s_n\}$ converges to a number $s$, the series is convergent with sum $s$, and we write $\sum a_n = s$. Otherwise the series is divergent. So a series is really the sequence of its partial sums.

Geometric series. For a first term $a \ne 0$ and a common ratio $r$ of consecutive terms,

$\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty ar^{n-1} = a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \frac{a}{1 - r} \ \text{ if } |r| < 1, \qquad \text{divergent if } |r| \ge 1.$

Why. $s_n - rs_n = a - ar^n$, since everything else cancels. So $s_n = \dfrac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}$ for $r \ne 1$, and $r^n \to 0$ exactly when $|r| < 1$.

Example. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty 3\Bigl(\frac25\Bigr)^n$ has first term $a = \tfrac65$ and ratio $r = \tfrac25$, so its sum is $\dfrac{6/5}{1 - 2/5} = 2$. The repeating decimal $0.363636\ldots$ is geometric with $a = \tfrac{36}{100}$ and $r = \tfrac{1}{100}$:

$0.363636\ldots = \dfrac{36}{100} + \dfrac{36}{100^2} + \cdots = \dfrac{36/100}{1 - 1/100} = \dfrac{4}{11}.$

Telescoping series. If each term is a difference $b_n - b_{n+1}$, the partial sums collapse to $s_n = b_1 - b_{n+1}$. Example: $\dfrac{1}{n(n + 1)} = \dfrac1n - \dfrac{1}{n + 1}$, so $s_n = 1 - \dfrac{1}{n + 1} \to 1$.

The harmonic series diverges. $\sum\frac1n = 1 + \tfrac12 + \bigl(\tfrac13 + \tfrac14\bigr) + \bigl(\tfrac15 + \cdots + \tfrac18\bigr) + \cdots$. Each group in parentheses is at least $\tfrac12$ (the group ending at $\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}$ has $2^k$ terms, each at least $\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}$), so $s_{2^k} \ge 1 + \tfrac k2 \to \infty$.

Terms and partial sums.Slide $N$ and switch series. The harmonic terms shrink to $0$, but its partial sums keep climbing.

Theorem. If $\sum a_n$ converges, then $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n = 0$. Why. $a_n = s_n - s_{n-1}$, and both partial sums tend to the same $s$.

Test for divergence. The contrapositive: if $\lim a_n$ does not exist or is not $0$, then $\sum a_n$ diverges.

Linearity. If $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converge, so do $\sum ca_n = c\sum a_n$ and $\sum(a_n \pm b_n) = \sum a_n \pm \sum b_n$.

Warning

$a_n \to 0$ does not imply convergence. The harmonic series is the standard counterexample. The test for divergence can only prove divergence.

Keep the sequence and the series apart. $\frac{n}{n+1} \to 1$ as a sequence, so the series $\sum\frac{n}{n + 1}$ diverges.

11.3 The Integral Test and Estimates of Sums

Integral test. Suppose $f$ is continuous, positive, and decreasing on $[1, \infty)$, and $a_n = f(n)$. Then $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n$ converges if and only if $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx$ converges.

Why. Rectangles of width $1$ and height $a_n$ compare the sum to the area. Placed to the left of $x = n$, on $[n - 1, n]$, they fit under the curve, so $a_2 + \cdots + a_n \le \int_1^n f\,dx$. Placed to the right, on $[n, n + 1]$, they cover the curve, so $\int_1^{n+1} f\,dx \le a_1 + \cdots + a_n$.

Sum versus area for $a_n = 1/n^p$.Toggle between rectangles under and over the curve, and move $p$ across $1$.

$p$-series. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^p}$ converges if $p > 1$ and diverges if $p \le 1$. This is the integral test applied to the $p$-integral of 7.8.

Remainder estimate. Let $s = \sum a_n$ and let $R_n = s - s_n$ be the remainder after $n$ terms. Under the hypotheses of the integral test,

$\displaystyle\int_{n+1}^\infty f(x)\,dx \le R_n \le \int_n^\infty f(x)\,dx.$

Examples. $\sum\frac{1}{n^2 + 1}$ converges, since $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2 + 1} = \lim_{t\to\infty}\tan^{-1}t - \frac\pi4 = \frac\pi4$. $\sum_{n=2}^\infty\frac{1}{n\ln n}$ diverges, since $\int_2^t\frac{dx}{x\ln x} = \ln(\ln t) - \ln(\ln 2) \to \infty$.

Warning

The integral test tells us whether the series converges, not its sum. $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2} = 1$, but $\sum\frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$. Also check the hypotheses: $f$ must be positive and decreasing (at least for $x$ beyond some point).

11.4 The Comparison Tests

Comparison test. Suppose $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ have positive terms.

  • If $\sum b_n$ converges and $a_n \le b_n$ for all $n$, then $\sum a_n$ converges.
  • If $\sum b_n$ diverges and $a_n \ge b_n$ for all $n$, then $\sum a_n$ diverges.

Why. With positive terms the partial sums increase. In the first case they are bounded by $\sum b_n$, so they converge by the monotonic sequence theorem. In the second they are at least the partial sums of $\sum b_n$, which go to $\infty$.

Limit comparison test. Suppose $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ have positive terms and $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n} = c$ with $0 < c < \infty$. Then both series converge or both diverge.

Why. For large $n$, $\frac{c}{2}b_n < a_n < 2c\,b_n$, and the ordinary comparison test applies in both directions.

Choosing $b_n$. Keep only the dominant term of the numerator and of the denominator. Example: $\displaystyle\sum\frac{2n + 3}{n^3 + 1}$ looks like $\frac{2n}{n^3} = \frac{2}{n^2}$. With $b_n = \frac{1}{n^2}$, $\dfrac{a_n}{b_n} = \dfrac{2n^3 + 3n^2}{n^3 + 1} \to 2$, and $\sum\frac{1}{n^2}$ converges, so the series converges.

Warning

A comparison in the wrong direction proves nothing. Showing $\frac{1}{n^2 + 1} \le \frac1n$ is true but useless, because being smaller than a divergent series says nothing. Before writing an inequality, decide whether you expect convergence (bound above by a convergent series) or divergence (bound below by a divergent one).

11.5 Alternating Series

Definition. An alternating series has terms that alternate in sign, $\sum(-1)^{n-1}b_n = b_1 - b_2 + b_3 - \cdots$ with $b_n > 0$.

Alternating series test. If (i) $b_{n+1} \le b_n$ for all $n$ and (ii) $\lim_{n\to\infty}b_n = 0$, then the series converges.

Why. Each step goes the opposite way and no farther than the last one. So the even partial sums $s_2 \le s_4 \le s_6 \le \cdots$ increase, the odd ones $s_1 \ge s_3 \ge \cdots$ decrease, and every even one is below every odd one. Both are bounded monotonic, hence converge, and their limits agree because $s_{2n+1} - s_{2n} = b_{2n+1} \to 0$.

Alternating series estimation theorem. If $s = \sum(-1)^{n-1}b_n$ satisfies the hypotheses of the test, then

$|R_n| = |s - s_n| \le b_{n+1}.$

The sum $s$ lies between any two consecutive partial sums, so the error after $n$ terms is at most the size of the first omitted term.

Partial sums zigzag around the sum.The yellow band between $s_N$ and $s_{N+1}$ always contains $s$ (green). Its width is $b_{N+1}$.

Examples. The alternating harmonic series $1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \cdots$ converges. (Its sum is $\ln 2$.) How many terms of $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n!}$ guarantee error at most $0.001$? We need $b_{n+1} = \frac{1}{(n + 1)!} \le 0.001$, that is $(n + 1)! \ge 1000$. Since $6! = 720$ and $7! = 5040$, $n + 1 = 7$, so $n = 6$ terms.

Warning

Check both conditions. If $b_n \not\to 0$, the series diverges by the test for divergence (for example $\sum(-1)^n\frac{n}{n + 1}$). If $b_n$ is not decreasing, the test does not apply, though the series might still converge by another test. The estimate $|R_n| \le b_{n+1}$ holds only for alternating series that pass the test.

11.6 Absolute Convergence and the Ratio and Root Tests

Definitions. $\sum a_n$ is absolutely convergent if $\sum|a_n|$ converges. It is conditionally convergent if it converges but $\sum|a_n|$ diverges. Example: $\sum\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}$ is conditionally convergent, while $\sum\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n^2}$ is absolutely convergent.

Theorem. Absolutely convergent series are convergent. Why. $0 \le a_n + |a_n| \le 2|a_n|$, so $\sum(a_n + |a_n|)$ converges by comparison, and $a_n = (a_n + |a_n|) - |a_n|$ is a difference of two convergent series.

Ratio test. Let $L = \displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Bigl|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\Bigr|$.

  • If $L < 1$, $\sum a_n$ is absolutely convergent.
  • If $L > 1$ or $L = \infty$, $\sum a_n$ diverges.
  • If $L = 1$, the test is inconclusive.

Why. If $L < 1$, pick $r$ with $L < r < 1$. Eventually each $|a_{n+1}| \le r|a_n|$, so the tail is bounded by a geometric series with ratio $r$. If $L > 1$, the terms eventually grow, so they do not tend to $0$.

Root test. Let $L = \displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}$. The same three conclusions hold, for the same reason (eventually $|a_n| \le r^n$).

Examples. $\sum\frac{n^3}{3^n}$: $\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \dfrac{(n + 1)^3}{3n^3} \to \dfrac13 < 1$, absolutely convergent. $\sum\Bigl(\dfrac{2n + 1}{3n + 2}\Bigr)^n$: $\sqrt[n]{a_n} = \dfrac{2n + 1}{3n + 2} \to \dfrac23 < 1$, absolutely convergent.

Rearrangements. The terms of an absolutely convergent series can be reordered without changing the sum. A conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to have any sum at all (Riemann). Order matters when convergence relies on cancellation.

Warning

The ratio test gives $L = 1$ for every $p$-series and for every rational function of $n$, whether they converge or not, so it tells nothing there. Use it for factorials and for constants raised to the $n$th power. Use the root test when the whole term is an $n$th power.

11.7 Strategy for Testing Series

Classify $\sum a_n$ by its form.

  1. If $\lim a_n \ne 0$ (or it does not exist), stop: divergent by the test for divergence.
  2. If it is a $p$-series $\sum\frac{1}{n^p}$ or a geometric series $\sum ar^{n-1}$, use the known answers.
  3. If it is a rational or algebraic function of $n$ (roots of polynomials), compare with a $p$-series, usually by limit comparison.
  4. If it has the form $\sum(-1)^{n-1}b_n$, try the alternating series test, and check absolute convergence separately if asked.
  5. If it has factorials or constants to the $n$th power, use the ratio test.
  6. If $a_n = (b_n)^n$, use the root test.
  7. If $a_n = f(n)$ with $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx$ easy to evaluate, and $f$ positive and decreasing, use the integral test.

Example classifications: $\sum\frac{n - 1}{2n + 1}$ (terms $\to \frac12$, step 1), $\sum\frac{\sqrt n}{n^2 + 1}$ (compare with $\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}$, step 3), $\sum\frac{2^n}{n!}$ (ratio, step 5), $\sum ne^{-n^2}$ (integral, step 7).

11.8 Power Series

Definition. A power series centered at $a$ is

$\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n(x - a)^n = c_0 + c_1(x - a) + c_2(x - a)^2 + \cdots,$

where $x$ is a variable and the coefficients $c_n$ are constants. For each $x$ it is a series of numbers, which may converge or diverge. Its sum is a function of $x$ whose domain is the set of $x$ where it converges. (By convention $(x - a)^0 = 1$ even at $x = a$.)

Theorem. For a power series $\sum c_n(x - a)^n$ exactly one of the following holds.

  1. It converges only at $x = a$.
  2. It converges for all $x$.
  3. There is a number $R > 0$ such that it converges if $|x - a| < R$ and diverges if $|x - a| > R$.

$R$ is the radius of convergence ($R = 0$ in case 1, $R = \infty$ in case 2). The interval of convergence is the set of all $x$ where the series converges. In case 3 it is $(a - R, a + R)$ together with whichever endpoints $x = a \pm R$ give convergent series, and each endpoint must be checked on its own.

Why the convergence set is an interval around $a$. If the series converges at some $x_1$, its terms are bounded there. For any $x$ closer to $a$, each term is smaller than a bounded term times $\bigl|\frac{x - a}{x_1 - a}\bigr|^n$, a geometric ratio less than $1$, so the series converges absolutely at $x$.

Finding $R$. Apply the ratio (or root) test to $\sum|c_n(x - a)^n|$, get a limit $L$ depending on $x$, and solve $L < 1$.

Example. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(x + 1)^n}{n\,2^n}$. Ratio test:

$\Bigl|\dfrac{(x + 1)^{n+1}}{(n + 1)2^{n+1}}\cdot\dfrac{n\,2^n}{(x + 1)^n}\Bigr| = \dfrac{n}{n + 1}\cdot\dfrac{|x + 1|}{2} \to \dfrac{|x + 1|}{2}.$

So it converges for $|x + 1| < 2$: center $a = -1$, radius $R = 2$. At $x = 1$ the series is $\sum\frac1n$, divergent. At $x = -3$ it is $\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n}$, convergent. Interval of convergence $[-3, 1)$.

Interval of convergence.Drag the yellow point along the number line. Below it, the partial sums at that $x$ settle (blue) or run away (red). Endpoints snap into place.
Warning

The ratio test always gives $L = 1$ at the endpoints, so it cannot decide them. Substitute each endpoint and use another test (often $p$-series or alternating series). And $R$ is a radius, half the length of the interval, not the interval itself.

11.9 Representations of Functions as Power Series

The basic series. The geometric series with $a = 1$, $r = x$:

$\displaystyle\frac{1}{1 - x} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots, \qquad |x| < 1.$

Substituting for $x$ and multiplying by powers of $x$ gives many more. Example: $\dfrac{1}{1 + x^2} = \dfrac{1}{1 - (-x^2)} = \displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-1)^nx^{2n}$ for $|x| < 1$. Example: $\dfrac{x^3}{2 + x} = \dfrac{x^3}{2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{1 - (-x/2)} = \displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nx^{n+3}}{2^{n+1}}$, valid for $|x/2| < 1$, so $R = 2$.

Theorem (term by term calculus). If $f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n(x - a)^n$ has radius of convergence $R > 0$, then $f$ is differentiable on $(a - R, a + R)$ and

$\displaystyle f'(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty nc_n(x - a)^{n-1}, \qquad \int f(x)\,dx = C + \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n\frac{(x - a)^{n+1}}{n + 1},$

both with the same radius $R$. Power series can be differentiated and integrated like polynomials, inside the interval.

Examples. Integrating $\frac{1}{1 + x} = \sum(-1)^nx^n$ from $0$ to $x$:

$\displaystyle\ln(1 + x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n-1}x^n}{n} = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} - \cdots, \qquad |x| < 1.$

Integrating $\frac{1}{1 + x^2}$ from $0$ to $x$:

$\displaystyle\tan^{-1}x = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{2n + 1} = x - \frac{x^3}{3} + \frac{x^5}{5} - \cdots, \qquad |x| < 1.$

Both also hold at $x = 1$ (by Abel's theorem, beyond this course), giving $\ln 2 = 1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \cdots$ and $\frac\pi4 = 1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \cdots$. Differentiating $\frac{1}{1 - x}$ gives $\dfrac{1}{(1 - x)^2} = \displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty nx^{n-1}$.

Warning

The radius stays the same, but the endpoints can change: $\sum x^n$ diverges at $x = -1$, while its integral series $\sum\frac{x^{n+1}}{n + 1}$ converges there. After integrating, find $C$ by evaluating both sides at the center ($\ln(1 + 0) = 0$ gives $C = 0$ above).

11.10 Taylor and Maclaurin Series

Theorem (the coefficients are forced). If $f$ has a power series representation $f(x) = \sum c_n(x - a)^n$ for $|x - a| < R$, then

$c_n = \dfrac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}.$

Why. Differentiate $n$ times term by term and set $x = a$. Every term with a power of $(x - a)$ left over vanishes, and the term $c_n(x - a)^n$ has become $n!\,c_n$.

Definitions. The Taylor series of $f$ at $a$ is $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n$. At $a = 0$ it is called the Maclaurin series. The $n$th degree Taylor polynomial is its partial sum

$\displaystyle T_n(x) = \sum_{i=0}^n\frac{f^{(i)}(a)}{i!}(x - a)^i = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x - a)^2 + \cdots + \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n,$

and the remainder is $R_n(x) = f(x) - T_n(x)$. $T_1$ is the tangent line, and $T_n$ is the polynomial of degree $\le n$ that matches $f$ and its first $n$ derivatives at $a$.

When does $f$ equal its Taylor series? Exactly at those $x$ where $R_n(x) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. The tool for showing this is

Taylor's inequality. If $|f^{(n+1)}(x)| \le M$ for $|x - a| \le d$, then

$|R_n(x)| \le \dfrac{M}{(n + 1)!}\,|x - a|^{n+1} \qquad \text{for } |x - a| \le d.$

Example. For $f = e^x$ at $a = 0$ every derivative is $e^x \le e^d$ on $|x| \le d$, so $|R_n(x)| \le e^d\frac{d^{n+1}}{(n + 1)!} \to 0$ (factorials beat powers). Hence $e^x$ equals its Maclaurin series for every $x$.

Table of Maclaurin series.

$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{1 - x} &= \sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots && R = 1\\ e^x &= \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x^n}{n!} = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2!} + \cdots && R = \infty\\ \sin x &= \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{(2n + 1)!} = x - \frac{x^3}{3!} + \frac{x^5}{5!} - \cdots && R = \infty\\ \cos x &= \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n}}{(2n)!} = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} - \cdots && R = \infty\\ \tan^{-1}x &= \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{2n + 1} = x - \frac{x^3}{3} + \frac{x^5}{5} - \cdots && R = 1\\ \ln(1 + x) &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n-1}x^n}{n} = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} - \cdots && R = 1\\ (1 + x)^k &= \sum_{n=0}^\infty\binom kn x^n = 1 + kx + \frac{k(k - 1)}{2!}x^2 + \cdots && R = 1 \end{aligned}$

In the last line, the binomial series, $k$ is any real number and $\binom kn = \frac{k(k - 1)\cdots(k - n + 1)}{n!}$ with $\binom k0 = 1$. If $k$ is a nonnegative integer the series stops and is the binomial theorem.

Taylor polynomials $T_n$ at $0$.Raise the degree. For $\sin$, $\cos$, $e^x$ the polynomials hug the curve on a wider and wider interval, and the green Taylor bound is computed at the probe point. For the others, nothing outside $|x| < 1$ improves.

New series from old. Substitute, multiply, or differentiate the table instead of computing derivatives. Example: $x\cos(x^2) = \displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nx^{4n+1}}{(2n)!}$. Series also evaluate limits: $\dfrac{\sin x - x}{x^3} = \dfrac{-x^3/3! + x^5/5! - \cdots}{x^3} \to -\dfrac16$ as $x \to 0$. And they sum series:

$\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n\pi^{2n}}{4^n(2n)!} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n(\pi/2)^{2n}}{(2n)!} = \cos\frac\pi2 = 0.$
Warning

Do not drop the $n!$. The coefficient is $f^{(n)}(a)/n!$, not $f^{(n)}(a)$. And a Taylor series can converge without equaling $f$: the function equal to $e^{-1/x^2}$ for $x \ne 0$ and $0$ at $x = 0$ has every derivative $0$ at $0$, so its Maclaurin series is $0$. Equality needs $R_n \to 0$.

11.11 Applications of Taylor Polynomials

Approximation. Near $a$, $f(x) \approx T_n(x)$. Higher $n$ and $x$ closer to $a$ give better accuracy. To bound the error $|R_n(x)|$ use

  • Taylor's inequality, with $M$ the largest value of $|f^{(n+1)}|$ on the interval between $a$ and $x$, or
  • the alternating series estimate, when the series at that $x$ is alternating with decreasing terms.

Example. Approximate $\sin(0.2)$ by $T_3(x) = x - \frac{x^3}{6}$: $T_3(0.2) = 0.2 - \frac{0.008}{6} \approx 0.198667$. Since the $x^4$ coefficient of $\sin$ is $0$, $T_3 = T_4$, so Taylor's inequality with $n = 4$ and $M = 1$ (every derivative of $\sin$ is bounded by $1$) gives $|R_4(0.2)| \le \frac{(0.2)^5}{5!} \approx 2.7\times10^{-6}$. The true value is $\sin(0.2) = 0.1986693\ldots$

Physics. Many formulas are first Taylor terms. For example the relativistic kinetic energy $mc^2\bigl[(1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2} - 1\bigr]$ becomes $\tfrac12mv^2$ when $(1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ is replaced by the first two terms $1 + \frac{v^2}{2c^2}$ of its binomial series. Here $m$ is mass, $v$ speed, and $c$ the speed of light.

Warning

$M$ must bound $|f^{(n+1)}|$ on the whole interval from $a$ to $x$, not just at $a$. For $e^x$ on $[0, 0.5]$ take $M = e^{0.5}$ (or the cruder $M = 2$), not $M = e^0 = 1$.

Practice problems

  1. Determine whether each sequence converges, and find the limit: $\dfrac{3n^2 - 1}{n^2 + 5n}$, $\dfrac{n}{e^n}$, $\cos\Bigl(\dfrac{\pi n}{2n + 1}\Bigr)$, $\Bigl(1 + \dfrac2n\Bigr)^n$.
  2. Find the sum: $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{2^{n+1}}{5^n}$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\Bigl(\frac{1}{n + 1} - \frac{1}{n + 2}\Bigr)$.
  3. Use the integral test on $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n}{(n^2 + 1)^2}$.
  4. Use a comparison test on $\displaystyle\sum\frac{1}{3^n + n}$ and on $\displaystyle\sum\frac{\sqrt{n + 1}}{n}$.
  5. Decide absolute convergence, conditional convergence, or divergence: $\displaystyle\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n}$, $\displaystyle\sum\frac{(-1)^nn!}{10^n}$, $\displaystyle\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2 + 1}$.
  6. How many terms of $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n\,2^n}$ guarantee error at most $0.0005$?
  7. Find the radius and interval of convergence of $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(x - 3)^n}{n\,4^n}$ and of $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{n!\,x^n}{2^n}$.
  8. Find a power series for $\dfrac{x}{1 + 4x^2}$ and its radius of convergence.
  9. Find the Maclaurin series of $e^{-x^2}$ and use it to write $\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,dx$ as an alternating series.
  10. Find the Taylor polynomial $T_3$ of $\sqrt x$ at $a = 4$, and use Taylor's inequality to bound the error on $[4, 4.2]$.
