Chapter 13 · Vector Functions and Space Curves

← previousoutlinenext →

This chapter covers vector-valued functions $\vec r(t)$ and their calculus, including tangents, arc length, curvature, motion, projectiles, and Taylor approximation of curves. The main object is a curve in space traced by the tip of $\vec r(t)$, and each derivative tells us something new about how it moves and turns.

13.1 Vector Functions and Space Curves

1D in 3DA vector function $\vec r(t) = \langle f(t), g(t), h(t)\rangle$ assigns a vector to each $t$ in its domain. The tip of $\vec r(t)$ traces a space curve in $\mathbb R^3$ as $t$ varies.

Limits and continuity act component by component.

$\displaystyle \lim_{t \to a} \vec r(t) = \langle \lim_{t \to a} f,\ \lim_{t \to a} g,\ \lim_{t \to a} h\rangle.$

The function $\vec r$ is continuous at $a$ if and only if each of $f$, $g$, $h$ is continuous at $a$.

Direction of traversal. Arrowheads show the direction of increasing $t$. Replacing $t$ by $-t$ (or running a parameter interval $[a, b]$ from $b$ to $a$) flips the arrow.

Example. The helix $\vec r(t) = \langle \cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle$ projects to a circle in the $xy$-plane while $z$ climbs linearly with $t$.

Curves as intersections. A space curve often arises as the intersection of two surfaces. Below, the cylinder $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ meets the plane $z = x + y$ in the ellipse $\vec r(t) = \langle\cos t, \sin t, \cos t + \sin t\rangle$.

13.2 Derivatives and Integrals of Vector Functions

1D in 3DDerivative. $\vec r\,'(t) = \displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{\vec r(t + h) - \vec r(t)}{h} = \langle f'(t),\ g'(t),\ h'(t)\rangle.$ Where $\vec r\,'(t) \ne \vec 0$, it is tangent to the curve and points in the direction of motion.

Unit tangent vector. $\vec T(t) = \dfrac{\vec r\,'(t)}{|\vec r\,'(t)|}.$

Differentiation rules have the same form as the scalar ones. Here $\vec u$, $\vec v$ are differentiable vector functions, $c$ is a constant, and $f$ is a differentiable scalar function.

  • $(\vec u + \vec v)' = \vec u\,' + \vec v\,'$
  • $(c\vec u)' = c\vec u\,'$ and $(f \vec u)' = f'\vec u + f \vec u\,'$
  • $(\vec u \cdot \vec v)' = \vec u\,' \cdot \vec v + \vec u \cdot \vec v\,'$
  • $(\vec u \times \vec v)' = \vec u\,' \times \vec v + \vec u \times \vec v\,'$

Theorem. If $|\vec r(t)| = c$ for every $t$, where $c$ is a constant, then $\vec r\,'(t) \perp \vec r(t)$ for every $t$. Proof. Differentiate $\vec r \cdot \vec r = c^2$ to get $2\vec r\,' \cdot \vec r = 0$.

Integral. $\displaystyle \int_a^b \vec r(t)\,dt = \langle \int_a^b f\,dt,\ \int_a^b g\,dt,\ \int_a^b h\,dt\rangle.$

13.3 Arc Length and Curvature

1D in 3DArc length. A small step has length $ds \approx |\vec r\,'(t)|\,dt$ by Pythagoras applied to the components, so

$\displaystyle L = \int_a^b |\vec r\,'(t)|\,dt = \int_a^b \sqrt{(f')^2 + (g')^2 + (h')^2}\,dt.$

The arc-length function is $s(t) = \int_a^t |\vec r\,'(u)|\,du$, so $ds/dt = |\vec r\,'(t)|$ is the speed.

Curvature. The curvature $\kappa$ measures how fast the unit tangent direction turns per unit of arc length. A line has $\kappa = 0$, and a circle of radius $R$ has $\kappa = 1/R$, so $1/\kappa$ is the radius of the best-fitting circle.

$\kappa = \left|\dfrac{d\vec T}{ds}\right| = \dfrac{|\vec T\,'(t)|}{|\vec r\,'(t)|} = \dfrac{|\vec r\,'(t) \times \vec r\,''(t)|}{|\vec r\,'(t)|^3}.$

Why the cross-product form works. Only the component $\vec r\,''_\perp$ of $\vec r\,''$ perpendicular to the motion turns the curve, and the cross product with $\vec r\,'$ keeps exactly that part, so $|\vec r\,'\times\vec r\,''| = |\vec r\,'|\,|\vec r\,''_\perp|$. At speed $v$ the turning component of acceleration is $\kappa v^2$, so the numerator is $\kappa v^3$ and dividing by $|\vec r\,'|^3 = v^3$ leaves $\kappa$.

Warning

The denominator $|\vec r\,'|^3$ is the cube of the speed, not the speed. Curvature is always $\ge 0$, and the absolute values in both $\dfrac{|\vec r\,'\times\vec r\,''|}{|\vec r\,'|^3}$ and $\dfrac{|f''|}{(1+(f')^2)^{3/2}}$ are there to keep it nonnegative.

For a plane curve $y = f(x)$ the curvature is $\;\kappa = \dfrac{|f''(x)|}{(1 + (f'(x))^2)^{3/2}}.$

Principal unit normal. $\vec N(t) = \dfrac{\vec T\,'(t)}{|\vec T\,'(t)|}.$ It points toward the concave side of the curve, toward the center of the best-fitting circle.

Radius of curvature. $\rho = 1/\kappa$. The osculating circle has radius $\rho$ and center $\vec r(t) + \rho \vec N(t)$. It is the best-fitting circle, matching the curve's first and second derivatives at the point.

Below, $\vec r(t) = \langle\cos t,\ \sin t,\ 0.05\,t^2\rangle$ climbs faster and faster. The osculating circle, of radius $1/\kappa$, grows as the speed increases and the bending weakens.

13.4 Motion in Space

1D in 3DIf $\vec r(t)$ is the position of a particle, then $\vec v(t) = \vec r\,'(t)$ is velocity, $|\vec v(t)| = ds/dt$ is speed, and $\vec a(t) = \vec v\,'(t) = \vec r\,''(t)$ is acceleration.

Tangential and normal decomposition.

$\vec a = a_T \vec T + a_N \vec N,\qquad a_T = \dfrac{d|\vec v|}{dt} = \dfrac{\vec r\,' \cdot \vec r\,''}{|\vec r\,'|},\qquad a_N = \kappa |\vec v|^2 = \dfrac{|\vec r\,' \times \vec r\,''|}{|\vec r\,'|}.$

Where these come from. The dot product keeps the part of $\vec a$ along the motion and the cross product keeps the part across it. The turning term $a_N = \kappa|\vec v|^2$ is the circular motion formula $v^2/R$ with $\kappa = 1/R$.

Shortcut

Because $\vec T\perp\vec N$, the two components satisfy $|\vec a|^2=a_T^2+a_N^2$. So once we have $a_T$ we can get $a_N=\sqrt{|\vec a|^2-a_T^2}$ without computing a cross product.

The component $a_T$ changes the speed, and $a_N$ is the turning (centripetal) part.

Projectile motion. In the plane, with $g \approx 9.8$ m/s$^2$ the acceleration due to gravity, $\vec a = -g\hat\jmath$. Integrate twice, using the initial values $\vec r(0)$ and $\vec v(0)$.

Fire from the origin at angle $\alpha$ with speed $v_0$, so $\vec v(0) = (v_0 \cos\alpha)\hat\imath + (v_0\sin\alpha)\hat\jmath$. Then

$\vec v(t) = \langle v_0\cos\alpha,\ v_0\sin\alpha - g t\rangle,\qquad \vec r(t) = \langle v_0\cos\alpha\cdot t,\ v_0\sin\alpha\cdot t - \tfrac12 g t^2\rangle.$

The range is $R = v_0^2 \sin 2\alpha / g$, largest at $\alpha = \pi/4$.

Warning

The range formula assumes equal launch and landing height. $R=v_0^2\sin 2\alpha/g$ holds only when the projectile returns to its starting height. If it lands higher or lower, don't use it. Instead solve $y(t)=y_{\text{land}}$, the landing height, for the flight time and substitute into $x(t)$.

13.5 Taylor Approximation of Space Curves

1D in 3DApply the scalar Taylor expansion component by component. Since $\vec r(t) = \langle f, g, h\rangle$, each component satisfies, for a base point $t_0$ and a step $\delta$,

$f(t_0 + \delta) = f(t_0) + \delta f'(t_0) + \tfrac{\delta^2}{2}f''(t_0) + \cdots$

Combining the components gives

$\vec r(t_0 + \delta) = \vec r(t_0) + \delta\,\vec r\,'(t_0) + \tfrac{\delta^2}{2}\,\vec r\,''(t_0) + \tfrac{\delta^3}{6}\,\vec r\,'''(t_0) + \cdots$

Geometric meaning. The linear truncation gives the tangent line. Adding the quadratic term gives the osculating parabola in the plane of $\vec T$ and $\vec N$. Higher-order terms approximate the curve more closely.

Example. Take $\vec r(t) = \langle t,\ t^2/2,\ t^3/6\rangle$, which is its own Taylor series at $t_0 = 0$. The linear truncation is the tangent line along the $x$-axis, the quadratic term adds the parabola in the $xy$-plane, and the cubic term adds the $z$-component, which gives the full curve.

Worked example

Tangential and normal acceleration on a projectile. $\vec r(t) = \langle v_0\cos\alpha\,t,\ v_0\sin\alpha\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2\rangle$.
$\vec v = \vec r\,' = \langle v_0\cos\alpha,\ v_0\sin\alpha - gt\rangle$, with speed $|\vec v| = \sqrt{(v_0\cos\alpha)^2 + (v_0\sin\alpha - gt)^2}$. $\vec a = \vec r\,'' = \langle 0,\ -g\rangle$, so $|\vec a| = g$ for every $t$. The tangential component is $a_T = \vec a \cdot \vec T = \dfrac{\vec a \cdot \vec v}{|\vec v|} = \dfrac{-g(v_0\sin\alpha - gt)}{|\vec v|}$. At the apex $t = v_0\sin\alpha / g$, $\vec v = \langle v_0\cos\alpha, 0\rangle$ is horizontal and $\vec a = \langle 0, -g\rangle$ is perpendicular to it, so $a_T = 0$ and $a_N = g$, and all of gravity goes into turning the projectile.

Arc length and curvature on a helix. $\vec r(t) = \langle \cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle$.
$\vec r\,' = \langle -\sin t, \cos t, 1\rangle$, $|\vec r\,'| = \sqrt 2$, so $L = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\sqrt 2\,dt = 2\sqrt 2\,\pi$. $\vec r\,'' = \langle -\cos t,\ -\sin t, 0\rangle$, $\vec r\,'\times\vec r\,'' = \langle\sin t,\ -\cos t,\ 1\rangle$ of magnitude $\sqrt 2$, so $\kappa = \sqrt 2/(\sqrt 2)^3 = 1/2$.

Practice problems

  1. Sketch the directed curve $\vec r(t) = \langle 3\cos t,\ 3\sin t,\ 2\rangle$, $0 \le t \le 2\pi$. Indicate the direction.
  2. Given $\vec r(t) = \langle t^2,\ \sin t,\ e^t\rangle$, find $\vec v(t)$ and $\vec a(t)$ at $t = 0$.
  3. Find the unit tangent vector $\vec T(t)$ and unit normal $\vec N(t)$ for the helix $\vec r(t) = \langle \cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle$.
  4. Find the curvature of the parabola $y = x^2$ at the origin.
  5. Find the length of the arc of the helix $\vec r(t) = \langle \cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle$ from $t = 0$ to $t = 2\pi$.
  6. Find the tangential and normal components of acceleration for $\vec r(t) = \langle t,\ t^2,\ 3t\rangle$.
  7. A projectile is fired with initial speed $500$ m/s at angle $30^\circ$. Find the range.

Strategies

Smooth curve. $\vec r$ is smooth on an interval $I$ when $\vec r\,'$ is continuous and $\vec r\,' \ne \vec 0$ on $I$.

Tangent line at $t_0$. If $\vec r\,'(t_0) \ne \vec 0$, the tangent line is $\vec L(u) = \vec r(t_0) + u\,\vec r\,'(t_0)$.

Reparametrize by arc length. Compute $s(t) = \int_a^t |\vec r\,'(u)|\,du$, and invert to get $t(s)$. Then $\vec r(t(s))$ has unit speed. Curvature is defined with respect to $s$ so that it only depends on the shape of the curve, not on how fast we travel along it.

Choosing a curvature formula. $\kappa = |d\vec T/ds|$ is the definition, $\kappa = |\vec T\,'(t)|/|\vec r\,'(t)|$ converts to an arbitrary parameter, and $\kappa = |\vec r\,'\times\vec r\,''|/|\vec r\,'|^3$ avoids computing $\vec T$. For $y = f(x)$ the last reduces to $|f''|/(1+(f')^2)^{3/2}$.

Osculating, normal, rectifying planes. With the binormal $\vec B = \vec T \times \vec N$, these are the planes through the point spanned by $\vec T, \vec N$, by $\vec N, \vec B$, and by $\vec T, \vec B$ respectively.

Acceleration has no $\vec B$ component. $\vec a = a_T\vec T + a_N\vec N$ always lies in the osculating plane.
