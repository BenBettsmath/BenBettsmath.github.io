3D coordinates and vectors

Problem. Point $C$ lies on the segment from $A$ to $B$ with $|AC|=\tfrac12|CB|$. Writing $\vec a,\vec b,\vec c$ for the position vectors of $A,B,C$, show $\vec c=\tfrac23\vec a+\tfrac13\vec b$.

A point on a segment is a weighted average of the endpoints, and the weight on each endpoint is proportional to the length of the piece on the opposite side. Here $C$ cuts $AB$ so that $AC:CB=1:2$, so $C$ is one third of the way from $A$ to $B$.

$$ \vec c=\vec a+\tfrac13(\vec b-\vec a)=\tfrac23\vec a+\tfrac13\vec b. $$

The nearer endpoint gets the larger weight. Since $C$ is nearer to $A$, the weight on $\vec a$ is $\tfrac23$. The midpoint formula works the same way.

Problem. Let $\vec a=\langle 1,0,2\rangle$, $\vec b=\langle -1,3,1\rangle$, $\vec c=\langle 2,1,0\rangle$. Find the volume of the parallelepiped they span, then the length of its main diagonal.

The scalar triple product is the signed volume of the parallelepiped, which is base area times height. The vector $\vec b\times\vec c$ is normal to the base, and taking its dot product with $\vec a$ accounts for the height.

$$ \vec b\times\vec c=\begin{vmatrix}\hat\imath&\hat\jmath&\hat k\\-1&3&1\\2&1&0\end{vmatrix}=\langle -1,2,-7\rangle $$

$$ \vec a\cdot(\vec b\times\vec c)=-1+0-14=-15,\qquad V=15. $$

The sign records the orientation. The main diagonal runs from the origin to $\vec a+\vec b+\vec c=\langle 2,4,3\rangle$, so its length is $\sqrt{4+16+9}=\sqrt{29}$.

Drag to rotate. The slant edge $\vec a$ rises and falls while $\vec b,\vec c$ hold the base fixed. The volume is base area times the height of $\vec a$ above the base, which is what the triple product computes.

Lines and planes

Problem. Find the plane through $A(1,0,0),\ B(0,2,0),\ C(0,0,3)$.

The two edge vectors from $A$ span the plane, and their cross product is a normal vector, which gives the equation of the plane.

$$ \overrightarrow{AB}=\langle -1,2,0\rangle,\quad \overrightarrow{AC}=\langle -1,0,3\rangle,\quad \vec n=\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=\langle 6,3,2\rangle. $$

Using the point $A$ gives $6(x-1)+3y+2z=0$, that is $6x+3y+2z=6$.

Problem. Are the lines $\vec r_1=\langle 1,2,0\rangle+t\langle 1,-1,2\rangle$ and $\vec r_2=\langle 2,1,3\rangle+s\langle 2,1,-1\rangle$ parallel, intersecting, or skew?

Two lines in space can be parallel, intersecting, or skew, so we check the cases in order. The directions $\langle 1,-1,2\rangle$ and $\langle 2,1,-1\rangle$ are not multiples of each other, so the lines are not parallel. To intersect, the lines must agree in all three coordinates for the same $s$ and $t$. First match $x$ and $y$.

$$ 1+t=2+2s,\qquad 2-t=1+s\ \Longrightarrow\ s=0,\ t=1. $$

Now test $z$. The first line gives $2t=2$ and the second gives $3-s=3$. These disagree, so the lines have no common point. The lines are not parallel and do not intersect, so they are skew.

Drag to rotate. The lines pass each other without meeting. The dashed segment is the shortest segment joining them, and it is perpendicular to both.

Problem. Find the line where the planes $x+2y+z=4$ and $2x-y+z=3$ meet, and the angle between them.

The common line lies in both planes, so it is perpendicular to both normals. Its direction is therefore the cross product of the normals.

$$ \vec n_1=\langle 1,2,1\rangle,\quad \vec n_2=\langle 2,-1,1\rangle,\quad \vec n_1\times\vec n_2=\langle 3,1,-5\rangle. $$

To find a point on the line, set $z=0$ and solve $x+2y=4,\ 2x-y=3$, which gives $(2,1,0)$. So the line is

$$ x=2+3t,\quad y=1+t,\quad z=-5t. $$

The angle between the planes is the angle between the normals.

$$ \cos\theta=\frac{\vec n_1\cdot\vec n_2}{|\vec n_1||\vec n_2|}=\frac{2-2+1}{\sqrt6\,\sqrt6}=\frac16,\qquad \theta\approx 80.4^\circ. $$

Drag to rotate. The two planes meet along their common line, which is perpendicular to both normals. This is why the cross product gives its direction.

Problem. Does the line $x=1+t,\ y=2-t,\ z=3t$ cross the plane $2x+y-z=5$, run parallel to it, or lie inside it?

A quick test is to take the dot product of the direction with the normal. If the dot product isn't $0$ the line crosses the plane, and if it is $0$ the line is parallel to the plane or inside it. We can also substitute the line into the equation of the plane and solve for the parameter.

$$ 2(1+t)+(2-t)-3t=5\ \Longrightarrow\ 4-2t=5\ \Longrightarrow\ t=-\tfrac12. $$

One solution for $t$ means the line hits the plane once, at $\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac52,-\tfrac32\bigr)$. If the $t$ terms had cancelled and left a false statement, the line would be parallel to the plane and outside it. If they had left a true statement, the line would lie in the plane.

Quadric surfaces

Problem. Sketch the ellipsoid $\dfrac{x^2}{4}+y^2+\dfrac{z^2}{9}=1$.

We identify a quadric surface from its traces. Set one variable equal to a constant and see what curve we get. Here every coordinate plane cuts the surface in an ellipse, and the semi-axes are $2,1,3$ along $x,y,z$.

Problem. Identify and sketch $z=y^2-x^2$, the saddle, by assembling it from slices.

We build the surface from its slices. Along $y=0$ we get the downward parabola $z=-x^2$, and along $x=0$ we get the upward parabola $z=y^2$. The surface goes down in one direction and up in the other, so the origin is neither a maximum nor a minimum.

  • $z=k>0$ gives the hyperbola $y^2-x^2=k$, opening along $\pm y$.
  • $z=k<0$ gives a hyperbola opening along $\pm x$.
  • $y=k$ gives the downward parabola $z=k^2-x^2$.
  • $x=k$ gives the upward parabola $z=y^2-k^2$.

The two lines $y=\pm x$ at height zero separate the part of the surface above the $xy$-plane from the part below it. This is the shape that the second derivative test calls a saddle.

Problem. For $\dfrac{x^2}{9}-\dfrac{y^2}{4}+z^2=1$, find all coordinate plane traces and identify the surface.

  • $z=0$ gives $\dfrac{x^2}{9}-\dfrac{y^2}{4}=1$, a hyperbola opening along $\pm x$.
  • $y=0$ gives $\dfrac{x^2}{9}+z^2=1$, an ellipse with semi-axes $3$ and $1$.
  • $x=0$ gives $z^2-\dfrac{y^2}{4}=1$, a hyperbola opening along $\pm z$.
  • $y=k$ gives $\dfrac{x^2}{9}+z^2=1+\dfrac{k^2}{4}$, an ellipse that grows with $|k|$.

The slices perpendicular to one axis are ellipses, and the slices perpendicular to the other two axes are hyperbolas. This is a hyperboloid of one sheet, here with axis along the $y$-axis.

Problem. Identify $x^2+y^2-z^2=1$ by its horizontal slices and its slice at $x=0$.

At height $z=k$,

$$ x^2+y^2=1+k^2, $$

a circle of radius $\sqrt{1+k^2}$. The smallest circle, at $z=0$, is the unit circle, and the circles grow as we move away from it in either direction. The vertical slice $x=0$ is $y^2-z^2=1$, a hyperbola. Horizontal slices that are circles growing away from a smallest one, together with vertical slices that are hyperbolas, give a hyperboloid of one sheet with axis along $z$.

Drag to rotate. A horizontal plane sweeps up and down. The circular trace is smallest at height zero, and its radius grows as $\sqrt{1+k^2}$ in either direction.

Space curves and vector functions

Problem. Let $\vec r(t)=\langle\ln(t-1),\tan^{-1} t,\sqrt{4-t^2}\rangle$. Find the domain and sketch the $xy$ projection.

The domain is the intersection of the domains of the components. The first component $\ln(t-1)$ needs $t>1$, and the third component $\sqrt{4-t^2}$ needs $t\le 2$, so the domain is $(1,2]$.

As $t\to 1^+$, $x=\ln(t-1)\to -\infty$ while $y=\tan^{-1} t\to \pi/4$. At $t=2$, $(x,y)=(0,\tan^{-1} 2)$. The curve comes in from the far left, approaching the line $y=\pi/4$, and ends at that point.

Problem. An electron has velocity $\vec v(t)=\bigl\langle -\sin t,\tfrac{\cos t}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{\cos t}{\sqrt2}\bigr\rangle$ and starts at $\vec r(0)=\langle 1,0,0\rangle$. Find $\vec r(t)$ and show it stays on a sphere.

Integrate each component, then use the initial position to find the constants of integration.

$$ \vec r(t)=\bigl\langle\cos t,\tfrac{\sin t}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}\bigr\rangle,\qquad |\vec r|^2=\cos^2 t+\tfrac{\sin^2 t}{2}+\tfrac{\sin^2 t}{2}=1. $$

The length is $1$ for every $t$, so the electron stays on the unit sphere. The length is constant because the velocity is always perpendicular to the position, which is the converse of the theorem from 13.2. The path is the great circle in the plane $y=z$.

Problem. The cylinder $x^2+y^2=1$ and the plane $z=x+y$ meet in a curve. Parametrize it and find the tangent line at $(1,0,1)$.

The cylinder gives $x$ and $y$ in terms of a parameter, and the plane then gives $z$. On the cylinder $x=\cos t,\ y=\sin t$, and on the plane $z=\cos t+\sin t$.

$$ \vec r(t)=\langle\cos t,\sin t,\cos t+\sin t\rangle,\qquad \vec r\,'(t)=\langle -\sin t,\cos t,-\sin t+\cos t\rangle. $$

The point $(1,0,1)$ corresponds to $t=0$, where $\vec r\,'(0)=\langle 0,1,1\rangle$. So the tangent line is

$$ \langle 1,0,1\rangle+u\langle 0,1,1\rangle. $$

The projection of the curve onto the $xy$-plane is the unit circle, which is the cylinder seen from above. The curve itself is an ellipse in the slanted plane.

Problem. Working from the definitions, find the unit tangent $\vec T$ and unit normal $\vec N$ for the circle $\vec r(t)=\langle a\cos t,a\sin t\rangle$ and the helix $\vec r(t)=\langle\cos t,\sin t,t\rangle$. Which way does $\vec N$ point?

The unit tangent $\vec T$ is the direction of travel, that is, the velocity scaled to unit length. The unit normal $\vec N$ is the direction in which $\vec T$ is turning, and it always points toward the inside of the curve.

Circle. $\vec r\,'=\langle -a\sin t,a\cos t\rangle$ has length $a$, so $\vec T=\langle -\sin t,\cos t\rangle$. Then $\vec T\,'=\langle -\cos t,-\sin t\rangle$, which already has unit length, so $\vec N=\langle -\cos t,-\sin t\rangle$. This points directly at the center. On a circle the inside of the curve is always toward the center.

Helix. $\vec r\,'=\langle -\sin t,\cos t,1\rangle$ has length $\sqrt2$, so $\vec T=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\langle -\sin t,\cos t,1\rangle$. Differentiating and normalizing gives $\vec N=\langle -\cos t,-\sin t,0\rangle$. The normal is horizontal and points toward the central axis, even though the curve climbs. All of the vertical motion is in $\vec T$.

Problem. A ball is launched from $(0,2)$ at $20$ m/s, aimed $60^\circ$ above horizontal, under gravity $\vec a=\langle 0,-10\rangle$. Split the acceleration into its tangential and normal parts, and read off what each does at launch and at the top.

Gravity is a constant downward vector, but it has two effects on the motion. The component along $\vec T$ changes the speed, and the component along $\vec N$ bends the path. As the ball moves, one component grows while the other shrinks.

$$ \vec v(t)=\langle 10,\,10\sqrt3-10t\rangle,\qquad |\vec v|=\sqrt{100+(10\sqrt3-10t)^2}. $$

Since $\vec a=\langle 0,-10\rangle$, the tangential component is $a_T=\dfrac{\vec a\cdot\vec v}{|\vec v|}$ and the normal component is $a_N=\dfrac{|\,\vec a\times\vec v\,|}{|\vec v|}$, which here is $a_N=\dfrac{100}{|\vec v|}$ since the horizontal speed never changes.

At launch. $\vec v=\langle 10,10\sqrt3\rangle$, $|\vec v|=20$. Then $a_T=\dfrac{-100\sqrt3}{20}=-5\sqrt3\approx-8.7$, so the ball is slowing as it climbs, and $a_N=\dfrac{100}{20}=5$, so gravity is also bending the path.

At the top. The vertical speed is zero, $\vec v=\langle 10,0\rangle$, $|\vec v|=10$. Now $a_T=0$, so the speed is momentarily not changing, and $a_N=\dfrac{100}{10}=10$, so all of gravity goes into bending the path.

Problem. Find the curvature of $y=x^2$ at the origin, then the curvature of the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{4}+y^2=1$ at $(2,0)$ and $(0,1)$.

Curvature measures how sharply the curve bends, and its reciprocal is the radius of the circle that best fits the curve at that point, called the osculating circle. Start with the graph of a function. For $y=x^2$,

$$ \kappa=\frac{|y''|}{(1+(y')^2)^{3/2}},\qquad \text{at }x=0,\ y'=0,\ y''=2,\ \kappa=2. $$

So the osculating circle at the vertex has radius $\tfrac12$. Now parametrize the ellipse by $\vec r(t)=\langle 2\cos t,\sin t\rangle$. The curvature of a plane curve is $\kappa=\dfrac{|x'y''-y'x''|}{|\vec r\,'|^3}=\dfrac{2}{|\vec r\,'|^3}$ with $|\vec r\,'|^2=4\sin^2t+\cos^2t$.

At $(2,0)$, $t=0$. $|\vec r\,'|=1$, so $\kappa=2$ and the radius is $\tfrac12$. The ends of the ellipse bend sharply. The normal points in the direction $(-1,0)$, so the osculating circle is centered at $\bigl(\tfrac32,0\bigr)$.

At $(0,1)$, $t=\pi/2$. $|\vec r\,'|=2$, so $\kappa=\tfrac14$ and the radius is $4$. The top of the ellipse bends gently, and the osculating circle is centered at $(0,-3)$.

The osculating circle. Its center lies on the inward normal. The circle is small at the ends, where the curve bends sharply, and large along the top, where it bends gently.

Problem. The curve $\vec R(t)=\langle t,t,1-2t^2\rangle$ is the dome $z=1-x^2-y^2$ cut along the diagonal $y=x$, with $\vec R(\tfrac12)$ at $P=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$. Find the curvature, the center of the osculating circle at $P$, and compare the direction from $P$ to that center with the dome's normal $\langle 1,1,1\rangle$.

$$ \vec R\,'=\langle 1,1,-4t\rangle,\qquad \vec R\,''=\langle 0,0,-4\rangle. $$

At $t=\tfrac12$, $\vec R\,'=\langle 1,1,-2\rangle$ with $|\vec R\,'|=\sqrt6$, and the acceleration $\langle 0,0,-4\rangle$ points straight down. Then

$$ \vec R\,'\times\vec R\,''=\langle -4,4,0\rangle,\qquad \kappa=\frac{|\vec R\,'\times\vec R\,''|}{|\vec R\,'|^3}=\frac{\sqrt{32}}{6\sqrt6}=\frac{2\sqrt3}{9}. $$

The radius is $\rho=1/\kappa=\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}$, and the center is at that distance from $P$ along the principal normal, below the dome, at $(-1,-1,-1)$. The direction from $P$ to the center is $\langle -\tfrac32,-\tfrac32,-\tfrac32\rangle$, opposite to the surface normal $\langle 1,1,1\rangle$. So here the two directions lie on the same line, and the curve bends down into the dome, away from the side the normal points to. This happens because the plane $y=x$ contains the surface normal $\langle 1,1,1\rangle$, and for such slices the two directions always lie on the same line. For a slice whose plane does not contain the normal, they need not.

Functions of several variables

We use the gradient in four ways. Its dot product with a small displacement gives the linear approximation, its dot product with a velocity gives the chain rule, its dot product with a unit direction gives the directional derivative, and setting it equal to zero gives the candidates for a max or min. The problems below treat each one in one variable first and then in two variables. The running example is the dome $z=1-x^2-y^2$ at $P=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$.

Problem. Consider the dome $z=1-x^2-y^2$ at $P=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$. (a) Find $\nabla f(P)$. (b) Use it to write the tangent plane and estimate $f(0.51,0.49)$. (c) Find the rate of change at $P$ along the diagonal $\vec u=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\langle 1,1\rangle$. (d) Where is the gradient zero, and what kind of point is it?

(a) $\nabla f=\langle -2x,-2y\rangle$, so at $P$

$$ \nabla f(P)=\langle -1,-1\rangle. $$

We now use this vector in three ways. The fourth use, the chain rule, is not part of this problem.

(b) The dot product with a displacement gives the linearization. The tangent plane is $z=f(P)+\nabla f(P)\cdot\langle x-\tfrac12,y-\tfrac12\rangle$,

$$ L(x,y)=\tfrac12-(x-\tfrac12)-(y-\tfrac12)=\tfrac32-x-y. $$

At $(0.51,0.49)$ the displacement is $\langle 0.01,-0.01\rangle$, and $\nabla f(P)\cdot\langle 0.01,-0.01\rangle=0$, so $L=\tfrac12$. The true value is $f(0.51,0.49)=0.4998$, which is slightly smaller. The tangent plane lies above the dome, so the estimate is too high.

(c) The dot product with a unit direction gives the directional derivative.

$$ D_{\vec u}f(P)=\nabla f(P)\cdot\vec u=\langle -1,-1\rangle\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\langle 1,1\rangle=-\sqrt2. $$

We get the same number from the diagonal slice. Parametrize $y=x$ by $\langle t,t\rangle$, so $z=1-2t^2$ and the arc length is $s=\sqrt2\,t$. Then $\dfrac{dz}{ds}=\dfrac{dz/dt}{ds/dt}=\dfrac{-4t}{\sqrt2}$, which at $t=\tfrac12$ is $-\sqrt2$.

(d) Set the gradient equal to zero to find the extrema. $\nabla f=\langle -2x,-2y\rangle=\vec 0$ only at the origin, where the dome has its highest point $(0,0,1)$. This is a maximum.

Problem. Build the tangent plane to the dome at $P$ from the tangent lines to the two traces, and confirm that it is the linearization.

First we see where the plane comes from. Fix $y=\tfrac12$. The dome becomes a curve in a vertical plane, and its tangent line at $P$ has slope $f_x(P)=-1$. So the vector $\vec a=\langle 1,0,-1\rangle$ is tangent to that trace. Fix $x=\tfrac12$ instead. The tangent vector to this trace is $\vec b=\langle 0,1,-1\rangle$, with slope $f_y(P)=-1$.

The plane through $P$ spanned by $\vec a$ and $\vec b$ has normal

$$ \vec a\times\vec b=\langle 1,1,1\rangle, $$

so $1(x-\tfrac12)+1(y-\tfrac12)+1(z-\tfrac12)=0$, that is $z=\tfrac32-x-y$. This is the linearization from the previous problem. The two trace slopes are the two partials, and the plane is the only plane containing both tangent lines.

Drag to rotate. The orange plane touches the blue dome at $P$ and lies above it everywhere, which is why its estimate is too high. The red arrow in the $xy$-plane is the gradient $\langle -1,-1\rangle$.

Problem. When does a tangent line overestimate and when does it underestimate? Answer in one variable first, then for the dome.

One variable: Take $f(x)=\sqrt x$ at $a=4$. Then $f'(x)=\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$, so $f'(4)=\tfrac14$ and

$$ L(x)=2+\tfrac14(x-4),\qquad L(4.1)=2.025. $$

The true value $\sqrt{4.1}=2.0249$ is slightly lower. The graph is concave down, so it lies below its tangent line, and the line overestimates. If the graph is concave up, the line underestimates. The sign of the error is determined by the concavity.

The dome: The same reasoning works for surfaces. The estimate from the tangent plane is too high where the surface bends below the plane and too low where it bends above. The dome bends below its tangent plane everywhere, so its linearization always overestimates, which is why the estimate in part (b) above was too high. A saddle bends below in one direction and above in another, so its tangent plane is too high in some directions and too low in others.

Where the graph is concave down (top), the tangent line lies above it and overestimates. Where the graph is concave up (bottom), the tangent line lies below it and underestimates.

Problem. Find the tangent plane to the saddle $z=x^2+xy-y^2$ at $(1,1,1)$. Near that point, is the estimate from the plane always too high, always too low, or neither?

$f_x=2x+y$ and $f_y=x-2y$, so at $(1,1)$ the gradient is $\langle 3,-1\rangle$ and

$$ z=1+3(x-1)-1(y-1)=3x-y-1. $$

Whether the estimate is high or low depends on the shape of the surface. This surface is a saddle. It rises in one direction and falls in another, so it crosses its tangent plane instead of staying on one side. The estimate is too high along the falling direction and too low along the rising one. So the answer is neither, unlike the dome.

Problem. Read partial derivatives off a contour map. The level curves of $f$ near $(2,1)$ are the ellipses of $f=x^2/4+y^2$. Estimate $f_x(2,1)$ and $f_y(2,1)$ from the picture, then check against calculus.

A partial derivative is rise over run, and on a contour map the rise is the difference between contour labels and the run is the distance we move. Moving a little to the right from $(2,1)$ we cross contours going up, and the closer together they are, the steeper the climb. A step of the same size upward crosses more contours, since the ellipses are closer together in the $y$ direction.

Calculus confirms this. $f_x=\tfrac{x}{2}=1$ and $f_y=2y=2$ at $(2,1)$. The function is twice as steep in the $y$ direction as in the $x$ direction, which matches the spacing of the contours. The gradient $\langle 1,2\rangle$ crosses every contour at a right angle and points in the direction of steepest ascent.

Red is the step to the right used to estimate $f_x$, and blue is the step upward used to estimate $f_y$. Green is the gradient, which crosses the contours at a right angle and points uphill.

Problem. Sketch the level curves of $f(x,y)=x^2+4y^2$ for $k=1,4,9$.

The equation $x^2+4y^2=k$ can be rewritten as $\dfrac{x^2}{k}+\dfrac{y^2}{k/4}=1$, an ellipse with semi-axes $\sqrt k$ and $\sqrt k/2$. The contours are nested ellipses, squashed in the $y$ direction because $f$ increases faster in that direction.

Problem. Sketch the domain of $f(x,y)=\ln(y-x^2)$.

The logarithm needs $y>x^2$, which is the open region above the parabola.

Problem. Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^3+y^3}{x^2+y^2}$, and show $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}$ does not exist.

Polar coordinates show the difference between these two. The first becomes

$$ \frac{r^3(\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta)}{r^2}=r(\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta), $$

and since $|\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta|\le 2$ the expression is squeezed to $0$ as $r\to0$, whatever the angle. The limit is $0$.

The second becomes $\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=\cos2\theta$, which does not involve $r$. The value depends on the angle of approach. It is $1$ along $y=0$ and $-1$ along $x=0$. A limit that depends on direction does not exist.

Problem. Let $z=x^2y$ with $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. Find $\partial z/\partial\theta$ by the chain rule.

The chain rule is the dot product of the gradient with the velocity of the inputs. Adding the contributions through $x$ and through $y$,

$$ \frac{\partial z}{\partial\theta}=2xy(-r\sin\theta)+x^2(r\cos\theta)=-2r^3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta+r^3\cos^3\theta=r^3\cos\theta(\cos^2\theta-2\sin^2\theta). $$

Problem. Reparametrize the dome by $x=s\cos t,\ y=s\sin t$. Find $\partial z/\partial s$ and $\partial z/\partial t$, and explain why $\partial z/\partial t=0$ everywhere.

In polar coordinates the dome is $z=1-x^2-y^2=1-s^2$. The height depends on $s$ alone.

$$ \frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=-2s,\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t}=0. $$

The chain rule gives the same result. Changing $t$ moves us around a circle of fixed radius, which is a level curve, so the height does not change. Thus $\partial z/\partial t=0$ expresses the rotational symmetry of the dome. At $(s,t)=(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{\pi}{4})$ we are at $P$, and $\partial z/\partial s=-2\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=-\sqrt2$, the same rate of steepest descent that the directional derivative gave.

Problem. The length, width, and height of a box are changing. At one instant $\ell=1,\ w=2,\ h=2$ meters, with $\ell'=w'=2$ and $h'=-3$ m/s. How fast is the volume changing?

The volume $V=\ell w h$ depends on three changing quantities, so its rate of change is given by the chain rule with three terms.

$$ \frac{dV}{dt}=wh\,\frac{d\ell}{dt}+\ell h\,\frac{dw}{dt}+\ell w\,\frac{dh}{dt}=(2\cdot2)(2)+(1\cdot2)(2)+(1\cdot2)(-3)=8+4-6=6. $$

The volume is growing at $6$ cubic meters per second. Each term is the area of a face times the rate of change of the perpendicular dimension, which is the rate at which that face sweeps out volume. Two faces are moving outward and one is moving inward, and overall the volume grows.

Problem. Show $u=f(x-at)+g(x+at)$ satisfies the wave equation $u_{tt}=a^2u_{xx}$.

The solution is a wave moving right plus a wave moving left. With $\xi=x-at$ and $\eta=x+at$, the chain rule gives $u_t=-af'(\xi)+ag'(\eta)$ and $u_x=f'(\xi)+g'(\eta)$, so

$$ u_{tt}=a^2 f''(\xi)+a^2 g''(\eta),\qquad u_{xx}=f''(\xi)+g''(\eta), $$

and $u_{tt}=a^2u_{xx}$. ✓ Any shape moving left or right at speed $a$ solves the wave equation.

Problem. Find the tangent plane and the normal line to the sphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=9$ at $(1,2,2)$.

The gradient of the defining function gives both the plane and the line, because it is perpendicular to the surface.

$$ \nabla F=\langle 2x,2y,2z\rangle=\langle 2,4,4\rangle\ \parallel\ \langle 1,2,2\rangle. $$

The tangent plane is $1(x-1)+2(y-2)+2(z-2)=0$, that is $x+2y+2z=9$. The normal line runs along the same vector, $\langle 1,2,2\rangle+t\langle 1,2,2\rangle$, which is the radius extended. On a sphere the outward normal is always the radial direction.

Problem. Find the directional derivative of $f(x,y)=x^2y^3$ at $P(1,2)$ in the direction $\vec v=\hat\imath+\hat\jmath$, and the steepest ascent there.

The unit vector is $\hat u=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\langle 1,1\rangle$, and $\nabla f=\langle 2xy^3,3x^2y^2\rangle$, which at $P$ is $\langle 16,12\rangle$. So

$$ D_{\hat u}f=\nabla f\cdot\hat u=\frac{16+12}{\sqrt2}=14\sqrt2. $$

Over all directions the largest value is $|\nabla f|=20$, attained when $\hat u$ points along $\nabla f=\langle 16,12\rangle$. This is the direction of steepest ascent, and the rate is the length of the gradient. In the perpendicular direction the derivative is zero, and this is the direction of the level curve.

Problem. Show $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to the level curve through a point, and that it need not point toward the origin. Use $f(x,y)=x^2+3y^2$.

If we walk along a level curve, $f$ stays the same, so the directional derivative along it is zero. But $D_{\hat u}f=\nabla f\cdot\hat u$, and that vanishes exactly when $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to the tangent direction. So the gradient meets every level curve at a right angle, and it points in the direction in which $f$ increases fastest.

It is tempting to think that the gradient points along the line through the origin. Test this on $f=x^2+3y^2$, whose level curves are ellipses squashed in $y$. Here $\nabla f=\langle 2x,6y\rangle$, and at $(1,1)$,

$$ \nabla f=\langle 2,6\rangle, $$

which is not a multiple of the position vector $\langle 1,1\rangle$. It leans toward the $y$-axis, because $f$ increases faster in $y$ than in $x$. Only on the axes, where the ellipse is symmetric, does the gradient line up with the radial direction.

Problem. Interpret the second derivative test in terms of the shape of the surface. Compare the bowl $z=x^2+y^2$, the elliptic bowl $z=x^2+3y^2$, the same bowl turned $45^\circ$ as $z=2x^2+2xy+2y^2$, the saddle $z=x^2-y^2$, and the dome. What does each shape look like, and what role does the cross term play?

Each of these surfaces has exactly one critical point, at the origin, and it is a max, a min, or a saddle. We can classify it by looking at the slices instead of computing a determinant.

  • $z=x^2+y^2$ is a round bowl. Every slice through the bottom is an upward parabola, so it is a minimum. The level curves are circles with no special direction.
  • $z=x^2+3y^2$ is the same bowl squashed. It still increases in every direction, so it is still a minimum, but now it rises gently along $x$ and steeply along $y$. There are now two perpendicular special directions, the gentlest and the steepest.
  • $z=2x^2+2xy+2y^2$ is that elliptic bowl rotated $45^\circ$. It is still a minimum with one gentle and one steep direction, but these now lie along the diagonals. The $xy$ term records the rotation. Without it the special directions lie along the axes.
  • $z=x^2-y^2$ is the saddle. It goes up along $x$ and down along $y$, so it is neither a max nor a min. The two lines $y=\pm x$ at height zero separate the part above the $xy$-plane from the part below.
  • The dome $z=1-x^2-y^2$ is the round bowl turned upside down. It is a maximum, and like the round bowl it has no special direction.

The determinant test expresses these shapes in numbers. $D>0$ with $f_{xx}>0$ is a bowl, $D>0$ with $f_{xx}<0$ is a dome, and $D<0$ is a saddle. The cross term $f_{xy}$ measures how far the special directions have rotated off the axes.

The contours of each shape in turn. They are circles for the round bowl, tilted ellipses once the cross term is present, and hyperbolas for the saddle.

Problem. Find and classify all critical points of $f(x,y)=x^3-3x+y^2-2y$.

Start with one variable. The cubic $g(x)=x^3-3x$ has $g'=3x^2-3=0$ at $x=\pm1$, a local max at $x=-1$ and a local min at $x=1$. Adding $y^2-2y$ makes the surface curve upward in the $y$ direction. At $x=1$ the surface already curves up in $x$, and now it curves up in $y$ too, so that point is the bottom of a bowl. At $x=-1$ the surface curves down in $x$ but up in $y$, so the local max of the cubic becomes a saddle.

$f_x=3x^2-3=0$ at $x=\pm 1$, and $f_y=2y-2=0$ at $y=1$. So the critical points are $(1,1)$ and $(-1,1)$.

$D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2=(6x)(2)-0=12x$.

At $(1,1)$, $D=12>0$ and $f_{xx}=6>0$, which is a local min with $f=-3$. At $(-1,1)$, $D=-12<0$, which is a saddle with $f=1$. The negative $D$ is what makes it a saddle.

Problem. Classify the critical point of $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2-2x$, then find the absolute max and min on the closed disk $x^2+y^2\le 4$.

On a closed region the max and min can occur only at an interior critical point or on the boundary. Check both.

Interior. $f_x=2x-2=0$, $f_y=2y=0$ give $(1,0)$. Here $D=4>0$ and $f_{xx}=2>0$, so this is a local min with $f(1,0)=-1$.

Boundary. Parametrize $x=2\cos t,\ y=2\sin t$, so $f=4-4\cos t$. The max is at $\cos t=-1$, giving $f(-2,0)=8$, and the min is at $\cos t=1$, giving $f(2,0)=0$.

Comparing, the absolute min is $-1$ at $(1,0)$ and the absolute max is $8$ at $(-2,0)$. The shifted bowl has its lowest point inside the disk and its highest point on the far side of the boundary.

Problem. Find the absolute max and min of $f(x,y)=x^2+2y^2-4x-2y$ on the rectangle $R=[0,3]\times[0,2]$.

Find the interior critical point, then treat each of the four edges as a one-variable problem, then check the corners.

  • Interior. $f_x=2x-4=0$, $f_y=4y-2=0$ give $(2,\tfrac12)$, where $f=-\tfrac92$.
  • Edge $x=0$. $f=2y^2-2y$. The critical point is $y=\tfrac12$, where $f=-\tfrac12$. The corners give $f(0,0)=0,\ f(0,2)=4$.
  • Edge $x=3$. $f=2y^2-2y-3$. The critical point is $y=\tfrac12$, where $f=-\tfrac72$. The corners give $f(3,0)=-3,\ f(3,2)=1$.
  • Edge $y=0$. $f=x^2-4x$. The critical point is $x=2$, where $f=-4$.
  • Edge $y=2$. $f=(x-2)^2$. The critical point is $x=2$, where $f=0$.

Comparing all of these values, the abs min $=-\tfrac92$ is at $(2,\tfrac12)$ and the abs max $=4$ is at $(0,2)$. The lowest value is at the interior critical point and the highest is at a corner.

Multiple integrals

Problem. Evaluate $\displaystyle\iint_D xy\,dA$, where $D$ is bounded by $y=x^2$ and $y=x$.

Slice the region into vertical strips. For each $x$ in $[0,1]$ the strip runs from the lower curve $y=x^2$ up to the upper curve $y=x$, since $x^2\le x$ there.

$$ \iint_D xy\,dA=\int_0^1\int_{x^2}^x xy\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1 x\cdot\frac{x^2-x^4}{2}\,dx=\frac12\Bigl[\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{x^6}{6}\Bigr]_0^1=\frac{1}{24}. $$

A vertical strip sweeps across the region. Its bottom lies on $y=x^2$, its top lies on $y=x$, and the inner integral adds up one strip.

Problem. Find the centroid of the region bounded by $y=x^2$ and $y=1$.

The centroid is the balance point of the region, which is the average position. By symmetry $\bar x=0$. For $\bar y$, divide the moment about the $x$-axis by the area.

$$ A=\int_{-1}^1(1-x^2)\,dx=\tfrac43,\qquad M_x=\iint_D y\,dA=\int_{-1}^1\frac{1-x^4}{2}\,dx=\tfrac45, $$

so $\bar y=\dfrac{4/5}{4/3}=\dfrac35$. The centroid is $(0,\tfrac35)$, which is above the midline because the region is wider at the top.

Problem. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1\int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,dy\,dx$ by reversing the order of integration.

The inner integral $\int e^{y^2}\,dy$ has no elementary antiderivative, so the given order does not work. Reversing the order puts the integral over $x$ inside, and that one we can do. The region $\{0\le x\le1,\ x\le y\le1\}$ is the triangle below $y=1$ and above $y=x$, which described in the other order is $0\le y\le1,\ 0\le x\le y$.

$$ \int_0^1\int_0^y e^{y^2}\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1 y\,e^{y^2}\,dy=\Bigl[\tfrac{e^{y^2}}{2}\Bigr]_0^1=\tfrac{e-1}{2}. $$

The factor $y$ from the inner integral is the factor needed to integrate $e^{y^2}$. Here reversing the order is what makes the problem solvable.

The same triangle swept two ways, vertical strips for $dy\,dx$ and horizontal strips for $dx\,dy$. Only one order can be integrated.

Problem. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\pi\int_x^\pi\frac{\sin y}{y}\,dy\,dx$.

Again the inner integral cannot be done as written, so reverse the order. The region becomes $0\le y\le\pi,\ 0\le x\le y$, and the inner integral over $x$ multiplies by the length $y$.

$$ \int_0^\pi\frac{\sin y}{y}\cdot y\,dy=\int_0^\pi\sin y\,dy=2. $$

The $y$ in the denominator cancels with the width of the strip.

Problem. For the solid $E$ bounded by $y=x^2$, $z=0$, $y+z=1$, set up $\iiint_E f\,dV$ in all six orders.

There are six orders of integration for the same solid. The natural description is $-1\le x\le 1$, $x^2\le y\le 1$, $0\le z\le 1-y$. Each order integrates the variables in a different sequence, and the limits change to match.

  1. $dz\,dy\,dx$, $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1\int_{x^2}^1\int_0^{1-y} f\,dz\,dy\,dx$
  2. $dz\,dx\,dy$, $\displaystyle\int_0^1\int_{-\sqrt y}^{\sqrt y}\int_0^{1-y} f\,dz\,dx\,dy$
  3. $dy\,dz\,dx$, $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1\int_0^{1-x^2}\int_{x^2}^{1-z} f\,dy\,dz\,dx$
  4. $dy\,dx\,dz$, $\displaystyle\int_0^1\int_{-\sqrt{1-z}}^{\sqrt{1-z}}\int_{x^2}^{1-z} f\,dy\,dx\,dz$
  5. $dx\,dy\,dz$, $\displaystyle\int_0^1\int_0^{1-z}\int_{-\sqrt y}^{\sqrt y} f\,dx\,dy\,dz$
  6. $dx\,dz\,dy$, $\displaystyle\int_0^1\int_0^{1-y}\int_{-\sqrt y}^{\sqrt y} f\,dx\,dz\,dy$

The limits look different but describe the same solid. Choosing the order that makes the limits simple is most of the work in setting up a triple integral.

Problem. Find the volume under $z=1-x^2-y^2$ and above $z=0$ using polar coordinates.

The dome meets the $xy$-plane in the unit circle, so the region is the unit disk. Polar coordinates fit this region and turn $1-x^2-y^2$ into $1-r^2$. The extra $r$ comes from the area $r\,dr\,d\theta$ of a small polar rectangle.

$$ V=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1(1-r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\Bigl[\tfrac{r^2}{2}-\tfrac{r^4}{4}\Bigr]_0^1=\frac{\pi}{2}. $$

Problem. Find the area inside the cardioid $r=1+\sin\theta$ and outside the circle $r=1$.

Area in polar coordinates is $\tfrac12\int r^2\,d\theta$, and for a region inside one curve and outside another we subtract the inner area from the outer. The curves meet where $1+\sin\theta=1$, that is $\theta=0,\pi$, so the region lies over $\theta\in[0,\pi]$.

$$ A=\frac12\int_0^\pi\bigl[(1+\sin\theta)^2-1\bigr]\,d\theta=\frac12\int_0^\pi\bigl(2\sin\theta+\sin^2\theta\bigr)\,d\theta=2+\frac{\pi}{4}. $$

A ray sweeps across the region. Its near end lies on the circle $r=1$ and its far end on the cardioid, and the integral adds up the part between them.

Problem. Find the volume inside the cylinder $x^2+y^2=1$, below $z=4$, and above $z=1-x^2-y^2$, using cylindrical coordinates.

Cylindrical coordinates fit this region, which is a disk in $x,y$ bounded below and above by two surfaces in $z$. The bottom is the dome $z=1-r^2$ and the top is the plane $z=4$.

$$ V=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1\int_{1-r^2}^{4} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\int_0^1 r\,(3+r^2)\,dr=2\pi\Bigl[\tfrac{3r^2}{2}+\tfrac{r^4}{4}\Bigr]_0^1=\frac{7\pi}{2}. $$

The height $3+r^2$ is the distance between the top and the bottom, which is larger near the edge of the disk, where the dome is lower.

Problem. Consider the first octant region inside $x^2+y^2+z^2=4$ with constant density. Find $\bar z$.

Spherical coordinates suit a ball, with $0\le\rho\le 2$ and $0\le\phi,\theta\le\pi/2$ for the first octant. The volume element is $dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$. Take the density to be $1$, since a constant density cancels in $\bar z$.

$$ m=\tfrac18\cdot\tfrac43\pi(8)=\tfrac{4\pi}{3},\qquad M_{xy}=\iiint z\,dV=\int_0^{\pi/2}\!d\theta\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\cos\phi\sin\phi\,d\phi\int_0^2\!\rho^3\,d\rho=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\tfrac12\cdot4=\pi. $$

So $\bar z=M_{xy}/m=\dfrac{\pi}{4\pi/3}=\dfrac34$. The centroid sits below the midpoint height $z=1$ because the region has more volume near the bottom, where it is wide, than near the top, where it is narrow.

Line integrals

Problem. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r$ for $\vec F=\langle xy,x^2\rangle$ along $y=x^2$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$. Is the work path independent?

First test whether $\vec F$ is conservative. Writing $\vec F=\langle P,Q\rangle$, we have $P_y=x$ but $Q_x=2x$. These disagree, so $\vec F$ is not a gradient and the work depends on the path. So we have to parametrize and integrate.

Take $x=t,\ y=t^2$, so $d\vec r=\langle 1,2t\rangle\,dt$ and $\vec F=\langle t^3,t^2\rangle$.

$$ \int_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\int_0^1\bigl(t^3+2t^3\bigr)\,dt=\int_0^1 3t^3\,dt=\frac34. $$

A different route from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ can give a different number, because $P_y\ne Q_x$ means the field is not conservative.

Problem. Show $\vec F=\langle e^y,\,xe^y+e^z,\,ye^z\rangle$ is conservative and evaluate $\displaystyle\int_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r$ from $(0,0,0)$ to $(1,1,1)$.

When the field is a gradient, the work depends only on the endpoints. Check the mixed partials. Writing $\vec F=\langle P,Q,R\rangle$, we have $P_y=e^y=Q_x$, $P_z=0=R_x$, $Q_z=e^z=R_y$. They match, so a potential exists.

Integrate $f_x=e^y$ to $f=xe^y+g(y,z)$. Then $f_y=xe^y+g_y=xe^y+e^z$ gives $g=ye^z+h(z)$, and $f_z=ye^z+h'=ye^z$ gives $h=0$. So $f=xe^y+ye^z$.

$$ \int_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=f(1,1,1)-f(0,0,0)=(e+e)-0=2e. $$

This is the fundamental theorem for line integrals, the analogue for gradients of the fundamental theorem from first-year calculus. So we find the potential and subtract its values at the endpoints.

A conservative field is the gradient of a potential. The work from one point to another is the change in potential, whatever path the particle takes.

Problem. Use Green's theorem to find the area of the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1$.

Green's theorem relates a line integral around a closed curve to a double integral over the region it encloses. Choosing $\vec F$ so that $Q_x-P_y=1$ makes the double integral equal to the area, and $\tfrac12\oint(x\,dy-y\,dx)$ comes from one such choice.

Parametrize $x=a\cos t,\ y=b\sin t$. Then $x\,dy-y\,dx=ab\,dt$, so

$$ A=\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}ab\,dt=\pi ab. $$

The area is computed from the boundary alone, which is what Green's theorem allows.

Problem. Evaluate $\displaystyle\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r$ for $\vec F=\langle e^x-y^3,\cos y+x^3\rangle$ around the unit circle, counterclockwise.

The integrand looks complicated, but Green's theorem involves only $Q_x-P_y$, and the terms $e^x$ and $\cos y$ contribute nothing to it.

$$ Q_x-P_y=3x^2+3y^2,\qquad \oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\iint_D 3(x^2+y^2)\,dA. $$

In polar coordinates this is $3\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r^3\,dr\,d\theta=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$. So Green's theorem turns a complicated line integral into an easy double integral in polar coordinates.

The loop $C$ bounds the disk $D$. Green's theorem replaces the line integral around $C$ by the double integral of $Q_x-P_y$ over $D$.

Curl and divergence

Problem. Find the curl and divergence of $\vec F=\langle xz,xyz,-y^2\rangle$.

Divergence measures how much the field spreads out from a point, and curl measures how much it rotates around a point.

$$ \operatorname{curl}\vec F=\begin{vmatrix}\hat\imath&\hat\jmath&\hat k\\\partial_x&\partial_y&\partial_z\\xz&xyz&-y^2\end{vmatrix}=\langle -2y-xy,\ x,\ yz\rangle, $$

$$ \operatorname{div}\vec F=z+xz+0=z(1+x). $$

The divergence is a number at each point, and the curl is a vector pointing along the axis of rotation.

Problem. Prove $\operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}\vec F)=0$ for any smooth $\vec F$.

A field that is a curl does not spread out, and the proof uses only the equality of mixed partials. Writing $\vec F=\langle P,Q,R\rangle$,

$$ \operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}\vec F)=(R_{yx}-Q_{zx})+(P_{zy}-R_{xy})+(Q_{xz}-P_{yz}). $$

By Clairaut's theorem $R_{xy}=R_{yx}$, $Q_{xz}=Q_{zx}$, $P_{yz}=P_{zy}$, so the six terms cancel in pairs. The sum is $0$. ✓ This is why a curl can never have sources. The divergence theorem equates the flux of a field through a closed surface with the integral of its divergence over the enclosed solid, so the flux of a curl through any closed surface is $0$.

A rotating field. Particles circulate without piling up or thinning out, so its divergence is zero everywhere.

Problem. Compare the divergence of the outflow field $\vec F=\langle x,y\rangle$ and of the rotation field $\vec G=\langle -y,x\rangle$.

$\operatorname{div}\vec F=1+1=2$ is positive everywhere, so every point is a small source. $\operatorname{div}\vec G=0$, so the rotation neither spreads out nor gathers. For the curl the roles are reversed, since $\vec G$ rotates and $\vec F$ does not. So divergence and curl answer two different questions about a field, how much it spreads and how much it rotates.

The outflow field. Particles move away from the origin, which indicates positive divergence.

Lagrange multipliers

To optimize $f$ subject to a constraint $g=0$, we move through the level curves of $f$ until one is tangent to the constraint curve. At the point of tangency the two gradients are parallel, $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$. Solve that together with the constraint, then compare values of $f$ at the candidates.

Problem. Find three positive numbers summing to $100$ with the largest product.

Maximize $P=xyz$ subject to $x+y+z=100$. The gradients are parallel,

$$ \langle yz,xz,xy\rangle=\lambda\langle 1,1,1\rangle\ \Longrightarrow\ yz=xz=xy. $$

Dividing, $x=y=z$, so each is $\tfrac{100}{3}$ and $P_{\max}=\bigl(\tfrac{100}{3}\bigr)^3$. The product is greatest when the numbers are equal, which is common in symmetric problems like this one.

Problem. Find the max and min of $f(x,y)=3x+y$ on the circle $x^2+y^2=10$.

The level lines of $3x+y$ are parallel straight lines. Move them as far as the circle allows. The extreme values occur where a line is tangent to the circle, which is where $\nabla f$ is along the radius.

$$ \langle 3,1\rangle=\lambda\langle 2x,2y\rangle\ \Longrightarrow\ x=\tfrac{3}{2\lambda},\ y=\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}. $$

The constraint gives $\tfrac{9+1}{4\lambda^2}=10$, so $\lambda=\pm\tfrac12$. Then $\lambda=\tfrac12$ gives $(3,1)$ with $f=10$, the max, and $\lambda=-\tfrac12$ gives $(-3,-1)$ with $f=-10$, the min. The extremes are the two points where the gradient $\langle 3,1\rangle$ is radial.

Problem. Find the absolute max and min of $f(x,y)=xy$ on the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{4}+y^2=1$.

The level curves $xy=k$ are hyperbolas. As $|k|$ grows they move outward until one is tangent to the ellipse, and the tangency gives $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$.

$$ \langle y,x\rangle=\lambda\bigl\langle\tfrac{x}{2},2y\bigr\rangle\ \Longrightarrow\ y=\tfrac{\lambda x}{2},\quad x=2\lambda y. $$

Substituting gives $x=\lambda^2 x$, so $\lambda=\pm1$. For $\lambda=1$, $y=\tfrac{x}{2}$ and the constraint forces $x=\pm\sqrt2$, which gives $(\sqrt2,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$ and its negative, with $f=1$. For $\lambda=-1$ the points give $f=-1$. So the max is $1$ and the min is $-1$, at the four points where a hyperbola is tangent to the ellipse.

Watch the arrows. A point moves along the ellipse with $\nabla f$ in green and $\nabla g$ in purple. They are parallel only at the four points of tangency, which are the marked extrema.

Problem. Find the point on the plane $x-y+z=4$ closest to $(1,2,3)$.

Minimize the squared distance $d^2=(x-1)^2+(y-2)^2+(z-3)^2$ subject to $g=x-y+z-4=0$. At the closest point the level spheres of $d^2$ touch the plane, so $\nabla(d^2)$ is along the plane's normal.

$$ 2(x-1)=\lambda,\quad 2(y-2)=-\lambda,\quad 2(z-3)=\lambda, $$

so $x=1+\tfrac{\lambda}{2},\ y=2-\tfrac{\lambda}{2},\ z=3+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}$. The constraint gives $2+\tfrac{3\lambda}{2}=4$, so $\lambda=\tfrac43$ and the closest point is $\bigl(\tfrac53,\tfrac43,\tfrac{11}{3}\bigr)$, at distance $\dfrac{2\sqrt3}{3}$. The shortest path to a plane runs straight along its normal, which is what $\nabla(d^2)\parallel\nabla g$ says.
