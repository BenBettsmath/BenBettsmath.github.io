Field extensions and degrees

Def. Extension degree. (D&F §13.1) $E/F$ is a field extension if $F \subseteq E$ are fields. The degree is $[E:F] := \dim_F E$, viewing $E$ as a vector space over $F$. Degree is just dimension, the most powerful lens in field theory. The tower law $[K:F] = [K:E][E:F]$ whenever $F \subseteq E \subseteq K$ follows from vector space dimension multiplying in chains. So $[E:F] \mid [K:F]$.
Def. Algebraic element, minimal polynomial. (D&F §13.1) $\alpha \in E$ is algebraic over $F$ if some nonzero $f \in F[x]$ has $f(\alpha) = 0$. Algebraic = satisfies a polynomial equation, the gateway to finite extensions. The minimal polynomial $m_{\alpha,F} \in F[x]$ is the unique monic irreducible polynomial vanishing on $\alpha$; any $f$ with $f(\alpha) = 0$ satisfies $m_{\alpha,F} \mid f$. From the ideal $\ker(\text{evaluation at } \alpha)$, we get $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(m_{\alpha,F})$ and $[F(\alpha):F] = \deg m_{\alpha,F}$.
Example. $\sqrt{2}$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ with $m_{\sqrt 2, \mathbb{Q}} = x^2 - 2$, and $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$. $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$ with minimal poly $x^3 - 2$ (Eisenstein). Composite: $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$ because $\gcd(2,3)=1$ forces the minimal polynomials to remain irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$.
Non-example. $\pi$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$ (no nonzero $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ has $f(\pi) = 0$). Hence $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ has infinite degree over $\mathbb{Q}$. The field $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ is not finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space.
Fun fact. Coprimality forces disjointness. If $[F(\alpha):F] = m$ and $[F(\beta):F] = n$ with $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $[F(\alpha, \beta):F] = mn$. The minimal polynomials of $\beta$ over $F$ and $F(\alpha)$ remain the same (one cannot divide the other), so they conspire to produce the full product degree. This is quals' favorite trick for computing degrees of compositums.
Proof sketch. Tower law gives $[F(\alpha, \beta) : F] = [F(\alpha, \beta) : F(\alpha)] [F(\alpha) : F]$. Both $m$ and $n$ divide the left side, so $mn \mid [F(\alpha, \beta) : F]$ when $\gcd(m,n)=1$. But $[F(\alpha, \beta) : F(\alpha)] \leq n$ because $m_{\beta,F}$ stays irreducible over $F(\alpha)$ (its degree does not drop), giving the upper bound $[F(\alpha, \beta) : F] \leq mn$.
Computation. $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ generates the whole compositum. Let $\alpha = \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3$. Square: $\alpha^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}$, so $\sqrt{6} = (\alpha^2 - 5)/2 \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$. Then $2\sqrt{6} = \alpha^2 - 5$, square again: $(\alpha^2 - 5)^2 = 24$ gives $\alpha^4 - 10\alpha^2 + 1 = 0$. This polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (rational root test kills all $\pm 1$, and it does not factor as product of quadratics). Hence $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)$ of degree $4$.
Thm (Primitive Element). (D&F §14.4, Thm. 25) Any finite separable extension $E/F$ is simple, i.e., $E = F(\gamma)$ for some $\gamma$ (a "primitive element"). Separability says distinct roots, and then one generic element generates everything. For example $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3)$. In characteristic $0$, all finite extensions are separable, so all have primitive elements.
