Field extensions and degrees

Def. Extension degree. (D&F §13.1) Let $F \subseteq E$ be fields. Then $E/F$ is a field extension, and its degree is $[E:F] := \dim_F E$, viewing $E$ as a vector space over $F$. So the degree is a dimension, and linear algebra applies to it. The tower law says that if $F \subseteq E \subseteq K$ are fields, then $[K:F] = [K:E][E:F]$. It holds because if $\{a_i\}$ is a basis of $K$ over $E$ and $\{b_j\}$ is a basis of $E$ over $F$, then the products $\{a_i b_j\}$ are a basis of $K$ over $F$. So $[E:F] \mid [K:F]$.
Def. Algebraic element, minimal polynomial. (D&F §13.1) Let $E/F$ be a field extension. An element $\alpha \in E$ is algebraic over $F$ if there exists a nonzero $f \in F[x]$ with $f(\alpha) = 0$. Equivalently, $F(\alpha)$ is a finite extension of $F$. One direction holds because the powers $1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \ldots$ can't all be linearly independent over $F$ in a finite-dimensional $F$-vector space. For $\alpha$ algebraic over $F$, the minimal polynomial $m_{\alpha,F} \in F[x]$ is the unique monic polynomial that is irreducible over $F$ and vanishes at $\alpha$. Every $f \in F[x]$ with $f(\alpha) = 0$ satisfies $m_{\alpha,F} \mid f$. This is because evaluation at $\alpha$ is a map $F[x] \to E$ whose kernel is $(m_{\alpha,F})$, so $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(m_{\alpha,F})$ and $[F(\alpha):F] = \deg m_{\alpha,F}$.
Example. $\sqrt{2}$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ with $m_{\sqrt 2, \mathbb{Q}} = x^2 - 2$, and $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$. Also $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$, with minimal polynomial $x^3 - 2$ (irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by Eisenstein's criterion at the prime $2$). For a compositum (the field generated by two subfields), $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$ because $\sqrt 3 \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$, so $x^2 - 3$ stays irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$ and the tower law applied to $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)$ gives $2 \cdot 2$.
Non-example. $\pi$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$ (no nonzero $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ has $f(\pi) = 0$). Hence $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ has infinite degree over $\mathbb{Q}$. The field $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ is not finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space.
Remark. Coprime degrees multiply. If $[F(\alpha):F] = m$ and $[F(\beta):F] = n$ with $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $[F(\alpha, \beta):F] = mn$. This is because the tower law, applied to $F \subseteq F(\alpha) \subseteq F(\alpha,\beta)$ and to $F \subseteq F(\beta) \subseteq F(\alpha,\beta)$, shows that both $m$ and $n$ divide $[F(\alpha,\beta):F]$. So $mn$ does too, and it can't be bigger than $mn$. This is the usual way to compute the degree of a compositum on quals.
Proof sketch. The tower law gives $[F(\alpha, \beta) : F] = [F(\alpha, \beta) : F(\alpha)] [F(\alpha) : F]$. Both $m$ and $n$ divide the left side, so $mn \mid [F(\alpha, \beta) : F]$ when $\gcd(m,n)=1$. But $[F(\alpha, \beta) : F(\alpha)] \leq n$ because $\beta$ is still a root of $m_{\beta,F}$, which has degree $n$, so its minimal polynomial over $F(\alpha)$ has degree at most $n$. This gives the upper bound $[F(\alpha, \beta) : F] \leq mn$.
Computation. $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ generates the whole compositum. Let $\alpha = \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3$. Squaring gives $\alpha^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}$, so $\sqrt{6} = (\alpha^2 - 5)/2 \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$. Squaring $2\sqrt{6} = \alpha^2 - 5$ gives $(\alpha^2 - 5)^2 = 24$, so $\alpha^4 - 10\alpha^2 + 1 = 0$. This polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (the only possible rational roots are $\pm 1$ and neither works, and it doesn't factor as a product of two quadratics). So $\alpha$ has degree $4$, and since $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)$, which also has degree $4$, they're equal.
Thm (Primitive Element). (D&F §14.4, Thm. 25) Let $E/F$ be a finite separable extension. Then $E/F$ is simple, i.e., there exists $\gamma \in E$ with $E = F(\gamma)$ (a "primitive element"). Separability means the conjugates of the generators are all distinct. Then for generators $\alpha, \beta$ of $E$ over $F$, a generic combination like $\alpha + c\beta$ with $c \in F$ differs from all of its conjugates, so it generates $E$ over $F$. For example $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3)$. In characteristic $0$, all finite extensions are separable, so all have primitive elements.
