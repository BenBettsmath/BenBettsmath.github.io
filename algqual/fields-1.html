Field extensions and degrees

Def. Extension degree. (D&F §13.1) $E/F$ is a field extension if $F \subseteq E$ are fields. The degree is $[E:F] := \dim_F E$, viewing $E$ as a vector space over $F$. So the degree is just a dimension, and we get to use all of linear algebra on it. The tower law $[K:F] = [K:E][E:F]$ whenever $F \subseteq E \subseteq K$ is true because if $\{a_i\}$ is a basis of $K$ over $E$ and $\{b_j\}$ is a basis of $E$ over $F$, then the products $\{a_i b_j\}$ are a basis of $K$ over $F$. So $[E:F] \mid [K:F]$.
Def. Algebraic element, minimal polynomial. (D&F §13.1) $\alpha \in E$ is algebraic over $F$ if some nonzero $f \in F[x]$ has $f(\alpha) = 0$. Equivalently, $F(\alpha)$ is a finite extension of $F$. One direction is because the powers $1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \ldots$ can't all be linearly independent in a finite-dimensional space. The minimal polynomial $m_{\alpha,F} \in F[x]$ is the unique monic irreducible polynomial vanishing on $\alpha$. Any $f$ with $f(\alpha) = 0$ satisfies $m_{\alpha,F} \mid f$. This is because evaluation at $\alpha$ is a map $F[x] \to E$ whose kernel is $(m_{\alpha,F})$, so $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(m_{\alpha,F})$ and $[F(\alpha):F] = \deg m_{\alpha,F}$.
Example. $\sqrt{2}$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ with $m_{\sqrt 2, \mathbb{Q}} = x^2 - 2$, and $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$. $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$ with minimal poly $x^3 - 2$ (Eisenstein). Composite: $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$ because $\sqrt 3 \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$, so $x^2 - 3$ stays irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$ and the tower law gives $2 \cdot 2$.
Non-example. $\pi$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$ (no nonzero $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ has $f(\pi) = 0$). Hence $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ has infinite degree over $\mathbb{Q}$. The field $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ is not finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space.
Remark. Coprime degrees multiply. If $[F(\alpha):F] = m$ and $[F(\beta):F] = n$ with $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $[F(\alpha, \beta):F] = mn$. This is because both $m$ and $n$ divide $[F(\alpha,\beta):F]$ by the tower law, so $mn$ does too, and it can't be bigger than $mn$. This is the usual way to compute the degree of a compositum on quals.
Proof sketch. Tower law gives $[F(\alpha, \beta) : F] = [F(\alpha, \beta) : F(\alpha)] [F(\alpha) : F]$. Both $m$ and $n$ divide the left side, so $mn \mid [F(\alpha, \beta) : F]$ when $\gcd(m,n)=1$. But $[F(\alpha, \beta) : F(\alpha)] \leq n$ because $\beta$ is still a root of $m_{\beta,F}$, which has degree $n$, so its minimal polynomial over $F(\alpha)$ has degree at most $n$. This gives the upper bound $[F(\alpha, \beta) : F] \leq mn$.
Computation. $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ generates the whole compositum. Let $\alpha = \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3$. Square: $\alpha^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}$, so $\sqrt{6} = (\alpha^2 - 5)/2 \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$. Then $2\sqrt{6} = \alpha^2 - 5$, square again: $(\alpha^2 - 5)^2 = 24$ gives $\alpha^4 - 10\alpha^2 + 1 = 0$. This polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (the only possible rational roots are $\pm 1$ and neither works, and it doesn't factor as a product of two quadratics). So $\alpha$ has degree $4$, and since $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)$, which also has degree $4$, they're equal.
Thm (Primitive Element). (D&F §14.4, Thm. 25) Any finite separable extension $E/F$ is simple, i.e., $E = F(\gamma)$ for some $\gamma$ (a "primitive element"). The idea is that separability means the conjugates of the generators are all different, and then a generic combination like $\alpha + c\beta$ can be told apart from its conjugates, so it generates everything. For example $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3)$. In characteristic $0$, all finite extensions are separable, so all have primitive elements.
