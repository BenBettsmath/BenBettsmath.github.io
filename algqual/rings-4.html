Factorization: ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD

Def. Factorization classes. (D&F §8.1-8.3) Let $R$ be an integral domain.
  • $R$ is a UFD if every nonzero non-unit factors uniquely (up to units and order) into irreducibles. In a UFD, irreducible and prime become the same. In a UFD, irreducible = prime.
  • $R$ is a PID if every ideal is principal. PIDs are "maximal among domains without unique factorization."
  • $R$ is an ED (Euclidean domain) if it has a norm $N\colon R \setminus 0 \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ with division algorithm: for $a, b \neq 0$ there exist $q, r$ with $a = qb + r$ and $r = 0$ or $N(r) < N(b)$.
The chain ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD is one-way: both implications hold, and neither reverses.
Examples. $\mathbb{Z}$ is ED with $N(a) = |a|$. $F[x]$ is ED with $N(f) = \deg f$. $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is ED with $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2$. $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ ($\omega = e^{2\pi i / 3}$) is ED with $N(a + b\omega) = a^2 - ab + b^2$. Every field is trivially ED, PID, and UFD.
Non-examples. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ isn't a UFD: $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$ (genuine counterexample to unique factorization). $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD but not a PID: $(2, x)$ isn't principal (polynomial rings jump the ED/PID gap). $\mathbb{Z}[(1 + \sqrt{-19})/2]$ is a PID but not ED (prime gaps can prevent the Euclidean algorithm).
Fun fact. The ED-to-UFD chain is strict. At each step, you lose structure: ED's have a norm and division algorithm (very rigid); PIDs drop the norm but keep principal ideals; UFDs drop principals but keep unique factorization. No domain has all three unless it's also one of the earlier classes. $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[(1+\sqrt{-19})/2]$ show gaps are real.
Def. Integral element, integrally closed. (D&F §15.3) $\alpha$ in an overring $S \supseteq R$ is integral over $R$ if $\alpha$ satisfies a monic polynomial $\alpha^n + r_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1} + \cdots + r_0 = 0$ with $r_i \in R$. Integrality is the algebraic analog of finiteness: $R$ is integrally closed in $S$ if every element of $S$ integral over $R$ already lies in $R$. A domain is integrally closed (in its fraction field) iff it equals its integral closure.
Example. Every UFD is integrally closed in its fraction field. (D&F §9.3, Prop. 9) $\mathbb{Z}$ is integrally closed in $\mathbb{Q}$: by the rational root theorem, rational roots of monic integer polynomials are integers.
Non-example. $\mathbb{C}[x^2, x^3]$ isn't integrally closed in $\mathbb{C}(x)$: $x$ satisfies $t^2 - x^2 = 0$ but isn't in the subring (it has odd degree in the generators).
Fun fact. Integral closure is a UFD repair kit. If $R$ is not a UFD but is integrally closed, the fault lies elsewhere (irreducibles aren't prime). But if $R$ isn't integrally closed, its integral closure $\overline{R}$ is a bigger ring that might be a UFD or PID. This is why $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]$ often sits in a larger ring of integers.
Prop. Ring of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$. (D&F §15.3, Prop. 26) Let $d$ be squarefree. The integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ is $$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)} = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d] & \text{if } d \equiv 2, 3 \pmod 4,\\ \mathbb{Z}\!\left[\tfrac{1+\sqrt d}{2}\right] & \text{if } d \equiv 1 \pmod 4. \end{cases}$$ The $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ case splits: $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$ sits strictly inside the full ring of integers. The element $\omega = (1+\sqrt d)/2$ satisfies the monic $\omega^2 - \omega + (1-d)/4 = 0$ but has no integer coefficients in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$. So $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$ is not integrally closed when $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ (e.g. $d = -11, -7, -3, 5, 13, \ldots$), hence not a UFD.
Rules. How to show a ring is not a UFD. (D&F §8.3)
  1. Exhibit two distinct irreducible factorizations of one element. A multiplicative norm $N$ helps: if $N(ab) = N(a)N(b)$ is strictly multiplicative, then $N(p)$ prime forces $p$ irreducible.
  2. Find an irreducible that isn't prime: $p \mid ab$ but $p \nmid a$ and $p \nmid b$. This breaks the UFD property directly.
  3. Show the ring isn't integrally closed in its fraction field. UFDs are always integrally closed. (D&F §9.3)
Computation. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD. $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$. With norm $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$: $N(2) = 4$, $N(3) = 9$, $N(1 \pm \sqrt{-5}) = 6$. For $2 = \alpha\beta$ nontrivial, $N(\alpha)N(\beta) = 4$ requires both norms $2$, but $a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ has no integer solutions. So $2, 3, 1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$ are all irreducible. The two factorizations use different irreducibles and are genuinely distinct: units are $\pm 1$.
Fun fact. Norms can measure irreducibility. For imaginary quadratic fields $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]$ with $d < 0$, the norm $N(\alpha) = |\alpha|^2$ is always multiplicative and positive. This makes norms powerful: if $N(p)$ is prime, then $p$ is irreducible (no nontrivial factorization survives taking norms). This technique is the main way to certify irreducibles in these rings.
Computation. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{10}]$ is not a UFD. $10 = 2 \cdot 5 = \sqrt{10} \cdot \sqrt{10}$. With norm $N(a + b\sqrt{10}) = |a^2 - 10b^2|$ (note the real quadratic case): $N(2) = 4$, $N(5) = 25$, $N(\sqrt{10}) = 10$. Since $a^2 - 10b^2 = \pm 2$ has no integer solutions, $2$ is irreducible. But $2 \mid 10 = \sqrt{10} \cdot \sqrt{10}$ while $2 \nmid \sqrt{10}$, so $2$ is an irreducible that is not prime. This kills UFD property directly.
Computation. $\mathbb{C}[x^2, x^3]$ is not a UFD. The fraction field is $\mathbb{C}(x)$ (since $x = x^3 / x^2 \in \mathrm{Frac}$). The element $x \in \mathbb{C}(x)$ satisfies $t^2 = x^2$ over $\mathbb{C}[x^2, x^3]$ but $x \notin \mathbb{C}[x^2, x^3]$ (everything has even or $\geq 3$ degree in $x$). Ring isn't integrally closed. Concretely, $x^6 = (x^2)^3 = (x^3)^2$ shows $x^2, x^3$ are "irreducibles" with two factorizations of their product.
Computation. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-11}]$ is not a UFD. $12 = 2 \cdot 6 = (1 + \sqrt{-11})(1 - \sqrt{-11})$. With norm $N(a + b\sqrt{-11}) = a^2 + 11b^2$: $N(2) = 4$, $N(6) = 36$, $N(1 \pm \sqrt{-11}) = 12$. Since no element has norm $2, 3,$ or $6$, the four factors $2, 6, 1 \pm \sqrt{-11}$ are all irreducible. Two distinct factorizations of $12$ use these irreducibles.
Thm (Gauss's lemma). (D&F §9.3, Thm. 7) UFD is closed under polynomial rings: if $R$ is a UFD then $R[x]$ is a UFD.
Thm. Irreducibility tests in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. (D&F §9.4)
  • Eisenstein. If a prime $p$ divides all $a_0, \ldots, a_{n-1}$ but $p \nmid a_n$ and $p^2 \nmid a_0$, then $f = \sum a_i x^i$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. (This is the power-series collapse test: modulo $p$, you get $f \equiv a_n x^n$, forcing no factorization to survive.)
  • Reduction mod $p$. If $\bar f \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]$ is irreducible with $\deg \bar f = \deg f$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. (Degree preservation kills factorizations when reducing.)
  • Rational roots. Rational roots of $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ are $\pm \frac{\text{divisor of } a_0}{\text{divisor of } a_n}$. (From Gauss's lemma applied to linear factors.)
