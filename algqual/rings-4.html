Factorization: ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD

Def. Factorization classes. (D&F §8.1-8.3) Let $R$ be an integral domain.
  • $R$ is a UFD if every nonzero non-unit factors uniquely (up to units and order) into irreducibles. In a UFD, irreducible = prime, since if an irreducible $p$ divides $ab$ then it has to show up in the factorization of $a$ or of $b$.
  • $R$ is a PID if every ideal is principal. In a PID gcds always exist, since $(a, b) = (d)$ for some $d$, and this is what ends up making factorization unique.
  • $R$ is an ED (Euclidean domain) if it has a norm $N\colon R \setminus 0 \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ with division algorithm: for $a, b \neq 0$ there exist $q, r$ with $a = qb + r$ and $r = 0$ or $N(r) < N(b)$.
The idea behind ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD is that a division algorithm makes every ideal generated by an element of smallest norm, and principal ideals give gcds, which give unique factorization. Neither implication reverses.
Examples. $\mathbb{Z}$ is ED with $N(a) = |a|$. $F[x]$ is ED with $N(f) = \deg f$. $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is ED with $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2$. $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ ($\omega = e^{2\pi i / 3}$) is ED with $N(a + b\omega) = a^2 - ab + b^2$. Every field is trivially ED, PID, and UFD.
Non-examples. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ isn't a UFD: $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$ (and these really are different factorizations, see below). $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD but not a PID: $(2, x)$ isn't principal, since a generator would have to divide both $2$ and $x$, so it would be $\pm 1$, but $(2, x) \neq \mathbb{Z}[x]$. $\mathbb{Z}[(1 + \sqrt{-19})/2]$ is a PID but not ED (the proof that it isn't Euclidean shows it has no universal side divisor, see D&F §8.1).
Fun fact. The ED-to-UFD chain is strict. Each step loses some structure. A Euclidean domain has a division algorithm. A PID loses the division algorithm but still has gcds coming from principal ideals. A UFD loses principal ideals but still has unique factorization. $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[(1+\sqrt{-19})/2]$ show that both gaps really happen.
Def. Integral element, integrally closed. (D&F §15.3) $\alpha$ in an overring $S \supseteq R$ is integral over $R$ if $\alpha$ satisfies a monic polynomial $\alpha^n + r_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1} + \cdots + r_0 = 0$ with $r_i \in R$. The monic polynomial is what makes $R[\alpha]$ finitely generated as an $R$-module, since it lets us rewrite $\alpha^n$ in terms of lower powers. $R$ is integrally closed in $S$ if every element of $S$ integral over $R$ already lies in $R$. A domain is integrally closed (in its fraction field) iff it equals its integral closure.
Example. Every UFD is integrally closed in its fraction field. (D&F §9.3, Prop. 9) $\mathbb{Z}$ is integrally closed in $\mathbb{Q}$: by the rational root theorem, rational roots of monic integer polynomials are integers.
Non-example. $\mathbb{C}[x^2, x^3]$ isn't integrally closed in $\mathbb{C}(x)$: $x$ satisfies $t^2 - x^2 = 0$ but isn't in the subring, since no element of the subring has an $x$ term.
Fun fact. Integral closure and UFDs. Being integrally closed doesn't make a ring a UFD, for example $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is integrally closed and still not a UFD. But a ring that isn't integrally closed can't be a UFD, and often the fix is to pass to the integral closure $\overline{R}$, which is a bigger ring that might be a UFD or PID. This is why $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]$ often sits inside a bigger ring of integers.
Prop. Ring of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$. (D&F §15.3, Prop. 26) Let $d$ be squarefree. The integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ is $$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)} = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d] & \text{if } d \equiv 2, 3 \pmod 4,\\ \mathbb{Z}\!\left[\tfrac{1+\sqrt d}{2}\right] & \text{if } d \equiv 1 \pmod 4. \end{cases}$$ So when $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$ is strictly smaller than the full ring of integers. The element $\omega = (1+\sqrt d)/2$ satisfies the monic $\omega^2 - \omega + (1-d)/4 = 0$, and $(1-d)/4$ is an integer exactly when $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, but $\omega \notin \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$ since its coefficients are halves. So $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$ is not integrally closed when $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ (e.g. $d = -11, -7, -3, 5, 13, \ldots$), hence not a UFD.
Rules. How to show a ring is not a UFD. (D&F §8.3)
  1. Exhibit two distinct irreducible factorizations of one element. A multiplicative norm $N$ helps. If $N(ab) = N(a)N(b)$ and $N(p)$ is prime, then a factorization $p = ab$ gives $N(p) = N(a)N(b)$, so one of $a, b$ has norm $1$ and is a unit. So $p$ is irreducible.
  2. Find an irreducible that isn't prime: $p \mid ab$ but $p \nmid a$ and $p \nmid b$. This breaks the UFD property directly.
  3. Show the ring isn't integrally closed in its fraction field. UFDs are always integrally closed. (D&F §9.3)
Computation. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD. $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$. With norm $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$: $N(2) = 4$, $N(3) = 9$, $N(1 \pm \sqrt{-5}) = 6$. If $2 = \alpha\beta$ with neither one a unit, then neither has norm $1$, so $N(\alpha)N(\beta) = 4$ means both norms are $2$. But $a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ has no integer solutions. So $2$ is irreducible, and the same argument works for $3$ and $1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$. These really are different factorizations, since the only units are $\pm 1$, so $2$ isn't a unit times $1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$.
Fun fact. Norms can measure irreducibility. For imaginary quadratic fields $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]$ with $d < 0$, the norm $N(\alpha) = |\alpha|^2$ is always multiplicative and positive. So if $N(p)$ is prime, then $p$ is irreducible, since any factorization of $p$ gives a factorization of $N(p)$. This is the usual way to check that elements of these rings are irreducible.
Computation. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{10}]$ is not a UFD. $10 = 2 \cdot 5 = \sqrt{10} \cdot \sqrt{10}$. With norm $N(a + b\sqrt{10}) = |a^2 - 10b^2|$ (note the real quadratic case): $N(2) = 4$, $N(5) = 25$, $N(\sqrt{10}) = 10$. Since $a^2 - 10b^2 = \pm 2$ has no integer solutions (reducing mod $5$, $\pm 2$ isn't a square), $2$ is irreducible. But $2 \mid 10 = \sqrt{10} \cdot \sqrt{10}$ while $2 \nmid \sqrt{10}$, so $2$ is an irreducible that is not prime. So $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{10}]$ can't be a UFD.
Computation. $\mathbb{C}[x^2, x^3]$ is not a UFD. The fraction field is $\mathbb{C}(x)$ (since $x = x^3 / x^2 \in \mathrm{Frac}$). The element $x \in \mathbb{C}(x)$ satisfies $t^2 = x^2$ over $\mathbb{C}[x^2, x^3]$ but $x \notin \mathbb{C}[x^2, x^3]$, since no element of the subring has an $x$ term. So the ring isn't integrally closed, and so it isn't a UFD. Concretely, $x^6 = (x^2)^3 = (x^3)^2$ gives two different factorizations, and $x^2$ and $x^3$ are irreducible since the subring has nothing of degree $1$.
Computation. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-11}]$ is not a UFD. $12 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-11})(1 - \sqrt{-11})$. With norm $N(a + b\sqrt{-11}) = a^2 + 11b^2$: $N(2) = 4$, $N(3) = 9$, $N(1 \pm \sqrt{-11}) = 12$. Since no element has norm $2$, $3$ or $6$, the factors $2, 3, 1 \pm \sqrt{-11}$ are all irreducible, so these are two different factorizations of $12$ into irreducibles. (This also follows from the ring of integers fact above, since $-11 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.)
Thm (Gauss's lemma). (D&F §9.3, Thm. 7) If $R$ is a UFD then $R[x]$ is a UFD. So we can build lots of UFDs, like $\mathbb{Z}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, out of ones we already know.
Thm. Irreducibility tests in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. (D&F §9.4)
  • Eisenstein. If a prime $p$ divides all $a_0, \ldots, a_{n-1}$ but $p \nmid a_n$ and $p^2 \nmid a_0$, then $f = \sum a_i x^i$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. (The idea is that mod $p$ we get $f \equiv a_n x^n$, so a factorization $f = gh$ would have to reduce to $x^i \cdot x^j$ up to constants. Then $g$ and $h$ both have constant term divisible by $p$, so $p^2 \mid a_0$.)
  • Reduction mod $p$. If $\bar f \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]$ is irreducible with $\deg \bar f = \deg f$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. (A factorization over $\mathbb{Z}$ would reduce to a factorization mod $p$, and keeping the degree means neither factor collapses to a constant.)
  • Rational roots. Rational roots of $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ are $\pm \frac{\text{divisor of } a_0}{\text{divisor of } a_n}$. (If $p/q$ is a root in lowest terms, plugging in and clearing denominators gives $p \mid a_0$ and $q \mid a_n$.)
