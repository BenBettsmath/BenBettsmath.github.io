Local rings and power series

Def. Local ring. (D&F §7.1) $R$ is local if it has a unique maximal ideal. Equivalently, the non-units form an ideal. So every element is either a unit or in the maximal ideal. This is because a non-unit generates a proper ideal, which sits inside some maximal ideal, and there's only one.
Example. Every field is local (maximal ideal is $(0)$). The $p$-adics $\mathbb{Z}_p$ are local with maximal ideal $p\mathbb{Z}_p$, as is the localization $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)} = \{a/b : p \nmid b\}$ with maximal ideal $p\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$.
Non-example. $\mathbb{Z}$ has one maximal ideal per prime, so it isn't local. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{10}]$ isn't either: $(2, \sqrt{10})$ and $(5, \sqrt{10})$ are distinct maximal ideals.
Fun fact. Why local rings are nice. In a local ring every element that isn't a unit is in $\mathfrak{m}$, so there's only one maximal ideal to keep track of. This makes a lot of arguments easier than in a general ring, where different maximal ideals can get in each other's way. Localization is how we turn a ring into a local one, by looking at one prime at a time.
Prop. $K[[x]]$ is a local PID. (D&F §7.2, Ex. 3) $K[[x]]$ is the formal power series $\sum_{n \geq 0} a_n x^n$. A series is a unit iff $a_0 \neq 0$. This is because we can solve for the coefficients of the inverse one at a time, starting from the constant term, and the only thing we ever divide by is $a_0$. So the non-units are the series with $a_0 = 0$, which is the ideal $(x)$, and the ring is local. Any nonzero ideal $I$ has an element of smallest $x$-order $k$, which is $x^k$ times a unit, so $I = (x^k)$. Hence $K[[x]]$ is a PID, in fact a discrete valuation ring.
Computation. Inverting $1 - x$ in $K[[x]]$: want $\sum a_n x^n$ with $(1-x)\sum a_n x^n = 1$. Coefficients give $a_0 = 1$, $a_n = a_{n-1}$, so $(1-x)^{-1} = 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 + \cdots$. Inverting $2 + x$ in $\mathbb{Q}[[x]]$: pull out $2$ to get $\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + x/2)^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum (-x/2)^n$.
