Local rings and power series

Def. Local ring. (D&F §7.1) $R$ is local if it has a unique maximal ideal. Equivalently, the non-units form an ideal: an element is either a unit or lies in the maximal ideal, with no middle ground.
Example. Every field is local (maximal ideal is $(0)$). The $p$-adics $\mathbb{Z}_p$ are local with maximal ideal $p\mathbb{Z}_p$, as is the localization $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)} = \{a/b : p \nmid b\}$ with maximal ideal $p\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$.
Non-example. $\mathbb{Z}$ has one maximal ideal per prime, so it isn't local. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{10}]$ isn't either: $(2, \sqrt{10})$ and $(5, \sqrt{10})$ are distinct maximal ideals.
Fun fact. Local rings are "monoidal". In a local ring, there's one monopoly of "bad" elements: they all live in the unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$. This makes local rings much easier to work with than global rings, where different primes can interfere. Localization is the art of forcing a ring to become local.
Prop. $K[[x]]$ is a local PID. (D&F §7.2, Ex. 3) $K[[x]]$ is the formal power series $\sum_{n \geq 0} a_n x^n$. A series is a unit iff $a_0 \neq 0$: invert coefficients from the constant term outward. Non-units form the ideal $(x)$, making the ring local. Any nonzero ideal has an element of minimal $x$-order $k$, so $I = (x^k)$. Hence $K[[x]]$ is a PID, in fact a discrete valuation ring.
Computation. Inverting $1 - x$ in $K[[x]]$: want $\sum a_n x^n$ with $(1-x)\sum a_n x^n = 1$. Coefficients give $a_0 = 1$, $a_n = a_{n-1}$, so $(1-x)^{-1} = 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 + \cdots$. Inverting $2 + x$ in $\mathbb{Q}[[x]]$: pull out $2$ to get $\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + x/2)^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum (-x/2)^n$.
