Sylow theorems

Thm (Sylow). (D&F §4.5, Thm. 18) Let $|G| = p^a m$ with $p \nmid m$.
  1. Existence: Sylow $p$-subgroups (subgroups of order $p^a$) exist.
  2. Conjugacy: All Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugate. Any $p$-subgroup nests in some Sylow $p$-subgroup.
  3. Count: The number $n_p$ of Sylow $p$-subgroups satisfies $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ and $n_p \mid m$. Two congruences to squeeze out solutions.
Prop. Extra Sylow facts. (D&F §4.5)
  • $P \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$ and $P \leq H \leq G$ implies $P \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(H)$. (Syl$p$ subgroups descend.)
  • $N_G(P)/C_G(P) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Aut}(P)$ constrains $n_p$ via $|N_G(P)/C_G(P)| \mid |\mathrm{Aut}(P)|$.
  • If $n_p = 1$ for *all* primes $p \mid |G|$, then $G \cong P_1 \times \cdots \times P_k$ where $P_i$ is the unique Sylow $p_i$-subgroup. (All Sylow subgroups normal means $G$ is their direct product.)
Note. Simplicity proof template (how to prove $G$ is not simple).
  1. List $n_p$ candidates for each $p \mid |G|$ using $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$, $n_p \mid m$.
  2. If $n_p = 1$ for some $p$, the unique Sylow is normal; done, $G$ is not simple.
  3. Else count elements: each Sylow $p$-subgroup (if cyclic of prime order) contributes $p-1$ nonidentity elements of order $p$. If $n_p(p-1) + (\text{other elements}) > |G|$, contradiction: $n_p$ can't be that large.
  4. If $n_p$ is small, use coset action: $G \to S_{n_p}$ has nontrivial kernel when $n_p! < |G|$, giving a nontrivial normal subgroup.
  5. Use automorphism constraint: $N_G(P)/C_G(P) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Aut}(P)$ implies $|N_G(P)/C_G(P)| \mid |\mathrm{Aut}(P)|$, ruling out large $n_p$.
Computation. Worked Sylow examples.

$|G| = pq$, $p < q$. $n_q \equiv 1 \pmod q$ and $n_q \mid p$, so $n_q \in \{1, p\}$; $p < q$ forces $n_q = 1$. If also $p \nmid q-1$ then $n_p = 1$, so $G \cong \mathbb{Z}/pq$.

$|G| = 30$. $n_5 \in \{1, 6\}$, $n_3 \in \{1, 10\}$. If both $>1$ then $6 \cdot 4 + 10 \cdot 2 = 44 > 30$, a contradiction, so $n_3 = 1$ or $n_5 = 1$.

$|G| = 56 = 2^3 \cdot 7$. $n_7 \in \{1, 8\}$. If $n_7 = 8$ then $8 \cdot 6 = 48$ elements of order $7$, leaving just $8$ for the Sylow $2$-subgroup, so $n_2 = 1$.

$|G| = 80 = 2^4 \cdot 5$. $n_5 \in \{1, 16\}$. If $n_5 = 16$ then $16 \cdot 4 = 64$ elements of order $5$, leaving $16$ for $2$-power elements, so $n_2 = 1$.

$|G| = 132 = 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 11$. $n_{11} \in \{1, 12\}$. If $n_{11} = 12$ then $12 \cdot 10 = 120$ elements of order $11$, leaving $12$, too few for both $n_2 > 1$ and $n_3 > 1$. So one of those Sylows is normal.

$|G| = p^2 q$ with $p > q$. $n_p \mid q$ and $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$. With $p > q$, only $n_p = 1$ works.
