Prime, maximal, and radical ideals

Def. Prime and maximal ideals. (D&F §7.4) In a commutative ring $R$ with $1$: an ideal $\mathfrak{p} \neq R$ is prime if $ab \in \mathfrak{p}$ implies $a \in \mathfrak{p}$ or $b \in \mathfrak{p}$, i.e. $R/\mathfrak{p}$ is a domain. An ideal $\mathfrak{m} \neq R$ is maximal if nothing lies strictly between $\mathfrak{m}$ and $R$, i.e. $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field. Every maximal ideal is prime since every field is a domain, but primes don't have to be maximal. In a PID every nonzero prime is maximal, because $(p) \subseteq (a)$ means $p = ab$ for some $b$, and then primality forces $a$ or $b$ to be a unit.
Example. In $\mathbb{Z}$, $(p)$ is maximal iff $p$ is prime. In $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$, $(x)$ is prime but not maximal ($\mathbb{Q}[x,y]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Q}[y]$, a domain not a field); $(x, y)$ is maximal ($\mathbb{Q}[x,y]/(x,y) \cong \mathbb{Q}$).
Non-example. In $\mathbb{Z}$, $(6)$ is neither prime nor maximal, since $2 \cdot 3 = 6 \in (6)$ but neither $2$ nor $3$ is in $(6)$. In $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$ the ideal $(x^2, xy)$ is not prime, since $x \cdot y = xy \in (x^2, xy)$ but $x, y \notin (x^2, xy)$. (Also the quotient $\mathbb{Q}[x,y]/(x^2,xy)$ has the nonzero nilpotent $x$, which a domain can't have.)
Thm (CRT). (D&F §7.6, Thm. 17) If $I + J = R$ then the map $r \mapsto (r + I, r + J)$ induces $R/(IJ) = R/(I \cap J) \cong R/I \times R/J$. For $n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$, $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \cong \prod_i \mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i}\mathbb{Z}$.
Remark. CRT recovers $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ from prime powers. (D&F §7.6) In $\mathbb{Z}$ we have $(a) + (b) = (\gcd(a,b))$, so $I + J = R$ is the ring version of two numbers being coprime. Powers of different primes are coprime, so the CRT splits $\mathbb{Z}/n$ up according to the prime factorization of $n$. This is also the same statement as saying a system of congruences with coprime moduli has exactly one solution mod the product.
Note. Maximal ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. By the lattice isomorphism theorem, the ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are the ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ containing $(n)$, which are $(d)/(n)$ for divisors $d$ of $n$. So the maximal ones are $(p)/(n)$ for primes $p \mid n$. For $n = 100 = 2^2 \cdot 5^2$, the maximal ideals are $(2)$ and $(5)$.
Def. Radical of an ideal. (D&F §7.4) The radical of $I \leq R$ is $\sqrt{I} := \{r \in R : r^n \in I \text{ for some } n\}$, an ideal with $I \subseteq \sqrt{I} = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p} \supseteq I} \mathfrak{p}$ (intersection of primes containing $I$). The idea is that $\sqrt{I}$ is everything that becomes nilpotent in $R/I$. Since a prime can't contain $r^n$ without containing $r$, every prime containing $I$ also contains $\sqrt{I}$. $I$ is radical if $I = \sqrt I$; primes are radical. The nilradical is $\mathrm{nil}(R) = \sqrt{(0)}$.
Thm. Nilradical equals intersection of primes. (D&F §7.4, Ex. 26) $\mathrm{nil}(R) := \{r : r^n = 0 \text{ for some } n\} = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p} \text{ prime}} \mathfrak{p}.$
Remark. The nilradical is the smallest radical ideal. The nilradical is the smallest ideal $I$ such that $R/I$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements (is reduced). This is because $R/\mathrm{nil}(R)$ is reduced, and any $I$ with $R/I$ reduced has to contain every nilpotent of $R$.
Note. Proof sketch. $\subseteq$: if $r$ is nilpotent then $r^n = 0 \in \mathfrak{p}$ for every prime, and $r^n \in \mathfrak{p}$ forces $r \in \mathfrak{p}$.
$\supseteq$: if $r$ isn't nilpotent then $S = \{1, r, r^2, \ldots\}$ avoids $0$. Apply Zorn to the ideals disjoint from $S$ to get a maximal such $\mathfrak{p}$. That $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime, since if $a, b \notin \mathfrak{p}$ then $\mathfrak{p} + (a)$ and $\mathfrak{p} + (b)$ both meet $S$, and multiplying those two elements gives an element of $S$ in $\mathfrak{p} + (ab)$. And $r \notin \mathfrak{p}$ since $r \in S$.
Prop. Sum of nilpotents. (D&F §7.3, Ex. 8) In a commutative ring, if $x^n = 0$ and $y^m = 0$ then $(x+y)^{m+n-1} = 0$, since every binomial term contains $x^n$ or $y^m$. So nilpotents form an ideal.
Prop. Maximal ideals of $C[0,1]$. (D&F §7.4, Ex. 33) Let $R$ be the continuous functions $[0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$. For each $t \in [0,1]$, $\mathfrak{m}_t = \{f \in R : f(t) = 0\}$ is the kernel of evaluation $f \mapsto f(t)$, and $R/\mathfrak{m}_t \cong \mathbb{R}$ is a field, so $\mathfrak{m}_t$ is maximal. Every maximal ideal has this form.
Proof of surjectivity. Say $M$ is an ideal in no $\mathfrak{m}_t$. For each $t \in [0,1]$ pick $f_t \in M$ with $f_t(t) \neq 0$. Each $f_t$ is nonzero near $t$, and by compactness of $[0,1]$ finitely many such neighborhoods at $t_1, \ldots, t_n$ cover it. Then $g = f_{t_1}^2 + \cdots + f_{t_n}^2 \in M$ is strictly positive on $[0,1]$, so $1/g$ is continuous and $g$ is a unit in $R$. So $M = R$.
Prop. Rings where $x^n = x$. (D&F §7.1, Ex. 12-15)
  • Boolean rings ($x^2 = x$). These are always commutative with characteristic $2$, but not every commutative ring of characteristic $2$ is Boolean, for example $\mathbb{F}_4$. From $(x+x)^2 = x + x$ we get $x + x = 0$, so char $2$. Expanding $(x+y)^2 = x+y$ to $x + xy + yx + y = x + y$ gives $xy + yx = 0$, and $-1 = 1$ forces $xy = yx$.
  • $x^3 = x$. Setting $x = 2$ gives $8 = 2$, so $6 = 0$ and $\mathrm{char}(R) \mid 6$. So the characteristic is $2$, $3$ or $6$, and each one happens, for example $\mathbb{Z}/2$, $\mathbb{Z}/3$ and $\mathbb{Z}/6$. (These aren't the only ones, products like $(\mathbb{Z}/3)^n$ work too.)
Example. The power set $\mathcal{P}(X)$ of any set $X$ is a Boolean ring under symmetric difference and intersection. (D&F §7.1 Ex. 15) Every subset satisfies $A \cap A = A$, so every element is idempotent, and commutativity is automatic.
Non-example. $\mathbb{Z}/4$ isn't Boolean, since $2^2 = 0 \neq 2$.
Remark. Finite Boolean rings are products of copies of $\mathbb{Z}/2$. By the CRT, every finite Boolean ring is isomorphic to a direct product of copies of $\mathbb{Z}/2$, one for each maximal ideal. This is because $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field where $x^2 = x$, so $x(x-1) = 0$ and the only elements are $0$ and $1$. Infinite Boolean rings don't have to be products though, for example the finite and cofinite subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ form a countably infinite Boolean ring, and an infinite product of $\mathbb{Z}/2$'s is uncountable.
