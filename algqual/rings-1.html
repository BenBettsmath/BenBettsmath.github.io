Ideals, primes, and maximals

Def. Prime and maximal ideals. (D&F §7.4) In a commutative ring $R$ with $1$: an ideal $\mathfrak{p} \neq R$ is prime if $ab \in \mathfrak{p}$ implies $a \in \mathfrak{p}$ or $b \in \mathfrak{p}$, i.e. $R/\mathfrak{p}$ is a domain. An ideal $\mathfrak{m} \neq R$ is maximal if nothing lies strictly between $\mathfrak{m}$ and $R$, i.e. $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field. Maximal always forces prime, but prime doesn't force maximal (except in PIDs). In a PID every nonzero prime is maximal.
Example. In $\mathbb{Z}$, $(p)$ is maximal iff $p$ is prime. In $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$, $(x)$ is prime but not maximal ($\mathbb{Q}[x,y]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Q}[y]$, a domain not a field); $(x, y)$ is maximal ($\mathbb{Q}[x,y]/(x,y) \cong \mathbb{Q}$).
Non-example. In $\mathbb{Z}$, $(6)$ is neither prime nor maximal: $6 = 2 \cdot 3$ but neither $2$ nor $3$ lies in $(6)$. In $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$ the ideal $(x^2, xy)$ is not prime: the product $x \cdot xy = x^2y \in (x^2, xy)$, yet $x, y \notin (x^2, xy)$ (quotient $\mathbb{Q}[x,y]/(x^2,xy)$ has nilpotents).
Thm (CRT). (D&F §7.6, Thm. 17) If $I + J = R$ then the map $r \mapsto (r + I, r + J)$ induces $R/(IJ) = R/(I \cap J) \cong R/I \times R/J$. For $n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$, $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \cong \prod_i \mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i}\mathbb{Z}$.
Fun fact. CRT recovers $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ from prime powers. (D&F §7.6) The coprimality condition $I + J = R$ is exactly what you get when ideals are coprime. For $\mathbb{Z}$, the prime powers $p_i^{a_i}$ generate coprime ideals, so the CRT isomorphism is an algebraic manifestation of the prime factorization of integers. This is why the CRT is the workhorse for solving systems of congruences.
Note. Maximal ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. The ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ correspond to divisors of $n$; the maximal ones are $(p)/(n)$ for primes $p \mid n$. For $n = 100 = 2^2 \cdot 5^2$, the maximal ideals are $(2)$ and $(5)$.
Def. Radical of an ideal. (D&F §7.4) The radical of $I \leq R$ is $\sqrt{I} := \{r \in R : r^n \in I \text{ for some } n\}$, an ideal with $I \subseteq \sqrt{I} = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p} \supseteq I} \mathfrak{p}$ (intersection of primes containing $I$). Radicals measure "invisible zeros": every prime over $I$ sees $\sqrt{I}$ as its minimal radical hull. $I$ is radical if $I = \sqrt I$; primes are radical. The nilradical is $\mathrm{nil}(R) = \sqrt{(0)}$.
Thm. Nilradical equals intersection of primes. (D&F §7.4, Ex. 26) $\mathrm{nil}(R) := \{r : r^n = 0 \text{ for some } n\} = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p} \text{ prime}} \mathfrak{p}.$
Fun fact. The nilradical is the smallest radical ideal. The nilradical is the smallest ideal $I$ such that $R/I$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements (is reduced). Any prime ideal contains all nilpotents, so their intersection is the set of elements killed by every prime.
Note. Proof sketch. $\subseteq$: if $r$ is nilpotent then $r^n = 0 \in \mathfrak{p}$ for every prime, and primes are radical.
$\supseteq$: if $r$ isn't nilpotent then $S = \{1, r, r^2, \ldots\}$ avoids $0$. Apply Zorn to the ideals disjoint from $S$ to get a maximal such $\mathfrak{p}$; that $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime, and $r \notin \mathfrak{p}$.
Prop. Sum of nilpotents. (D&F §7.3, Ex. 8) In a commutative ring, if $x^n = 0$ and $y^m = 0$ then $(x+y)^{m+n-1} = 0$, since every binomial term contains $x^n$ or $y^m$. So nilpotents form an ideal.
Prop. Maximal ideals of $C[0,1]$. (D&F §7.4, Ex. 33) Let $R$ be the continuous functions $[0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$. For each $t \in [0,1]$, $\mathfrak{m}_t = \{f \in R : f(t) = 0\}$ is the kernel of evaluation $f \mapsto f(t)$, and $R/\mathfrak{m}_t \cong \mathbb{R}$ is a field, so $\mathfrak{m}_t$ is maximal. Every maximal ideal has this form.
Proof of surjectivity. Say $M$ is an ideal in no $\mathfrak{m}_t$. For each $t \in [0,1]$ pick $f_t \in M$ with $f_t(t) \neq 0$. Each $f_t$ is nonzero near $t$, and by compactness of $[0,1]$ finitely many such neighborhoods at $t_1, \ldots, t_n$ cover it. Then $g = f_{t_1}^2 + \cdots + f_{t_n}^2 \in M$ is strictly positive on $[0,1]$, hence a unit in $R$, so $M = R$.
Prop. Rings where $x^n = x$. (D&F §7.1, Ex. 12-15)
  • Boolean rings ($x^2 = x$). These are precisely the commutative rings of characteristic 2. From $(x+x)^2 = x + x$ we get $x + x = 0$, so char $2$. Expanding $(x+y)^2 = x+y$ to $x + xy + yx + y = x + y$ gives $xy + yx = 0$, and $-1 = 1$ forces $xy = yx$.
  • $x^3 = x$. Setting $x = 2$ gives $8 = 2$, so $6 = 0$ and $\mathrm{char}(R) \mid 6$. There is one such ring per divisor of $6$: $\mathbb{Z}/2$ (char $2$), $\mathbb{Z}/3$ (char $3$), $\mathbb{Z}/6$ (char $6$).
Example. The power set $\mathcal{P}(X)$ of any set $X$ is a Boolean ring under symmetric difference and intersection. (D&F §7.1 Ex. 15) Elements (subsets) satisfy $A = A$ (idempotent), and commutativity is automatic.
Non-example. $\mathbb{Z}/4$ isn't Boolean: $2^2 = 0 \neq 2$. So "$x^2 = x$" is a real restriction.
Fun fact. Boolean rings are products of copies of $\mathbb{Z}/2$. By the CRT, every Boolean ring is isomorphic to a direct product of copies of $\mathbb{Z}/2$ (one for each maximal ideal). So the structure of Boolean rings is completely determined: they are exactly $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^S$ for sets $S$.
