Solvable and nilpotent groups

Def. Solvable. (D&F §3.4) Set $G^{(0)} = G$, $G^{(i+1)} = [G^{(i)}, G^{(i)}]$ (the derived series). Then $G$ is solvable if this series reaches $1$. Each commutator subgroup measures how far the previous step is from abelian, so solvable means $G$ is built out of finitely many abelian pieces. Equivalently, $G$ has a subnormal series $G = G_0 \supseteq G_1 \supseteq \cdots \supseteq G_k = 1$ with abelian quotients.
Def. Nilpotent. (D&F §6.1) The lower central series is $G_0 = G$, $G_{i+1} = [G, G_i]$ (commutators with the whole group). The upper central series is $Z_0 = 1$, $Z_{i+1}/Z_i = Z(G/Z_i)$ (centers of quotients stacked up). The idea is that taking commutators with all of $G$ over and over eventually kills everything, while the derived series only takes commutators of each term with itself. $G$ is nilpotent if either reaches $1$ (lower) or $G$ (upper). Nilpotent implies solvable, since $G^{(i)} \subseteq G_i$.
Example. $S_3$ is solvable: $S_3 \supset A_3 \supset 1$ with abelian quotients $\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/3$. $D_8$ and $Q_8$ are nilpotent of class $2$ (centers of order $2$, abelian quotient by the center). Nilpotent is a lot stronger than solvable. It says that if we keep modding out by the center, we eventually get to the trivial group. Every $p$-group is nilpotent, by using the fact that $p$-groups have nontrivial center over and over.
Non-example. $A_5$ isn't solvable: it's simple and nonabelian, so $[A_5, A_5] = A_5$ and the derived series sticks, never reaching $1$. $S_3$ isn't nilpotent, since $Z(S_3) = 1$, so the upper central series never grows.
Prop. $G/Z(G)$ cyclic implies $G$ abelian. (D&F §3.1, Ex. 36) If $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, generated by a coset $gZ(G)$, then every element of $G$ is $g^i z$ with $z \in Z(G)$. So any two elements look like $g^i z$ and $g^j z'$, and these commute because powers of $g$ commute with each other and $z, z'$ commute with everything. Contrapositive: nonabelian $G$ has $|G/Z(G)| > 1$ and noncyclic, so in particular $|G/Z(G)| \neq p$ for any prime $p$.
Thm. Nilpotency toolkit. (D&F §6.1) Subgroups and quotients of nilpotent groups are nilpotent. (This is because $\gamma_i(H) \subseteq \gamma_i(G)$ for a subgroup $H$, and $\gamma_i(G/N)$ is the image of $\gamma_i(G)$ in a quotient, so if $\gamma_i(G)$ gets to $1$ so do these.) Finite direct products of nilpotent groups are nilpotent: $\gamma_i(G \times H) = \gamma_i(G) \times \gamma_i(H)$. The one thing that does not work is extensions. $A_3$ and $S_3/A_3$ are both nilpotent but $S_3$ isn't, and this is a big difference from solvable groups.
Thm. Nilpotent iff product of Sylows. (D&F §6.1, Thm. 3) For finite $G$, these are equivalent:
  • $G$ is nilpotent;
  • every Sylow subgroup of $G$ is normal;
  • $G \cong P_1 \times \cdots \times P_k$ where each $P_i$ is the unique Sylow $p_i$-subgroup;
  • every maximal subgroup of $G$ is normal.
Thm. Solvability toolkit. (D&F §3.4, §6.1)
  • $p$-groups are solvable (they are nilpotent).
  • Subgroups and quotients of solvable groups are solvable.
  • If $N \trianglelefteq G$ with $N$ and $G/N$ both solvable, so is $G$. (This is because the projection $\pi: G \to G/N$ sends $G^{(i)}$ onto $(G/N)^{(i)}$, so once that's trivial we have $G^{(i)} \subseteq N$, and then the derived series of $N$ finishes the job.)
  • Burnside: $|G| = p^a q^b$ implies $G$ solvable. (D&F §19.2)
  • Feit-Thompson: $|G|$ odd implies $G$ solvable. (The proof is very long and way beyond D&F.)
  • $S_n$ is solvable for $n \leq 4$ (explicit towers: $S_3 \supset A_3 \supset 1$; $S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset 1$). $S_n$ for $n \geq 5$ is not.
  • $UT_n(F)$, upper triangular invertible matrices over a field, is solvable. Sending a matrix to its diagonal is a homomorphism onto an abelian group, and its kernel is the matrices with $1$s on the diagonal. Those have their own series with abelian quotients, clearing one superdiagonal at a time.
Prop. Center and commutator of a nonabelian group of order $p^3$. (D&F §5.4, §6.1) For nonabelian $G$ with $|G| = p^3$: $Z(G) = [G,G] \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. The class equation gives $Z(G) \neq 1$, and since $G$ is nonabelian, $|Z(G)| \neq p^3$. If $|Z(G)| = p^2$ then $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, forcing $G$ abelian. So $|Z(G)| = p$, and $G/Z(G)$ has order $p^2$, hence abelian, so $[G,G] \subseteq Z(G)$. Equality holds since $[G,G] \neq 1$ for nonabelian $G$.
Def. Automorphisms. (D&F §4.4) $\mathrm{Aut}(G)$ is the group of isomorphisms $G \to G$. Each $g \in G$ gives an inner automorphism $\iota_g\colon x \mapsto g x g^{-1}$ (conjugation). So conjugating by an element is always an automorphism, and these inner ones form a normal subgroup since $\phi\,\iota_g\,\phi^{-1} = \iota_{\phi(g)}$ for any automorphism $\phi$. The image of the map $G \to \mathrm{Aut}(G)$, $g \mapsto \iota_g$, is $\mathrm{Inn}(G) \trianglelefteq \mathrm{Aut}(G)$, with $G/Z(G) \cong \mathrm{Inn}(G)$ (the kernel is exactly the elements whose conjugation does nothing). The quotient $\mathrm{Out}(G) := \mathrm{Aut}(G)/\mathrm{Inn}(G)$ is the outer automorphism group.
Prop. Trivial automorphism group. (D&F §4.4) If $|\mathrm{Aut}(G)| = 1$ then every conjugation is the identity, so $G$ is abelian. Once $G$ is abelian, inversion $g \mapsto g^{-1}$ is an automorphism. For this to be trivial, $g = g^{-1}$ for all $g$, so $g^2 = 1$ for every element. So a group with trivial automorphism group has to be abelian with every element of order at most $2$. The converse is false, since $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$ has automorphism group $S_3$.
Example. For cyclic $\mathbb{Z}/n$: automorphisms correspond to units of $\mathbb{Z}/n$, i.e. $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/n) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$. So $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/6) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ (generators $\pm 1$), and $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/2) = \{1\}$. This is because an automorphism of $\mathbb{Z}/n$ is determined by where it sends $1$, and $1$ has to go to another generator.
Non-example. $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ (inversion and identity), nontrivial even though $\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic. This fits the proposition above, since inversion is a nontrivial automorphism of $\mathbb{Z}$.
