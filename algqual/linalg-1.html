Minimal and characteristic polynomials

Def. Characteristic and minimal polynomials. (D&F §12.2) For $T \colon V \to V$ on an $n$-dimensional $k$-vector space:
  • $\chi_T(x) = \det(xI - T)$ is the characteristic polynomial, monic of degree $n$.
  • $m_T(x)$ is the minimal polynomial, the monic generator of the ideal of polynomials vanishing on $T$.
  • Cayley-Hamilton: $\chi_T(T) = 0$. So $m_T \mid \chi_T$, and they share the same irreducible factors (up to multiplicity).
  • $m_T$ is the largest invariant factor. Writing invariant factors as $a_1 \mid a_2 \mid \cdots \mid a_r$, their product is $\chi_T$.
So $m_T$ is the smallest polynomial that kills $T$, and $\chi_T$ also kills $T$ by Cayley-Hamilton, which is why one divides the other.
Example. For the companion matrix of $f = x^3 + 2x + 1$, both $\chi$ and $m$ equal $f$. The companion matrix is built so that $f$ is the smallest polynomial that kills it. For $T = \mathrm{diag}(2, 2, 3)$, $\chi = (x-2)^2 (x - 3)$ but $m = (x - 2)(x - 3)$, because $T$ is diagonal, so $(T - 2)(T - 3) = 0$ already, even though $2$ shows up twice.
Remark. A Jordan block $J_2(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\chi = x^2$ and $m = x^2$, so for a single Jordan block the polynomials coincide. But stack two copies of $J_2(0)$ block-diagonally and $\chi$ stays $x^4$ while $m = x^2$. So the minimal polynomial only sees the largest Jordan block for each eigenvalue, while $\chi$ sees the total size.
Prop. Trace and determinant from eigenvalues. (D&F §12.3) $\mathrm{tr}(A) = \sum \lambda_i$ and $\det(A) = \prod \lambda_i$, summing and multiplying eigenvalues with multiplicity. Trace is cyclic, so $\mathrm{tr}(AB) = \mathrm{tr}(BA)$. Thus $\mathrm{tr}(AB - BA) = 0$ always.
Remark. Why $AB - BA \neq I$ in finite dimension. Taking traces: $\mathrm{tr}(AB - BA) = 0$ but $\mathrm{tr}(I_n) = n$. So if $\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid n$ (which is always true over $\mathbb{C}$), there are no $A, B$ with $AB - BA = I$. This is why the Heisenberg relation from quantum mechanics needs infinite-dimensional spaces, and it shows up on quals a lot.
Counterexample in infinite dimension. Let $V = k[x]$ with $u \colon p \mapsto xp$ (multiply by $x$) and $v \colon p \mapsto p'$ (formal derivative). Compute $(uv - vu)(p) = x p' - (xp)' = x p' - p - xp' = -p$, so $uv - vu = -\mathrm{id}$. So over $k[x]$ the commutator relation works. This doesn't contradict the above, since there's no trace on all operators of an infinite-dimensional space, so the trace argument doesn't go through.
