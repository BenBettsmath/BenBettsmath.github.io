Minimal and characteristic polynomials

Def. Characteristic and minimal polynomials. (D&F §12.2) For $T \colon V \to V$ on an $n$-dimensional $k$-vector space:
  • $\chi_T(x) = \det(xI - T)$ is the characteristic polynomial, monic of degree $n$.
  • $m_T(x)$ is the minimal polynomial, the monic generator of the ideal of polynomials vanishing on $T$.
  • Cayley-Hamilton: $\chi_T(T) = 0$. So $m_T \mid \chi_T$, and they share the same irreducible factors (up to multiplicity).
  • $m_T$ is the largest invariant factor. Writing invariant factors as $a_1 \mid a_2 \mid \cdots \mid a_r$, their product is $\chi_T$.
The minimal polynomial captures the smallest algebraic relation on $T$, while the characteristic polynomial is the big picture. Cayley-Hamilton bridges them.
Example. For the companion matrix of $f = x^3 + 2x + 1$, both $\chi$ and $m$ equal $f$. The companion matrix "sees" the full polynomial. For $T = \mathrm{diag}(2, 2, 3)$, $\chi = (x-2)^2 (x - 3)$ but $m = (x - 2)(x - 3)$ because repeated eigenvalues are diagonalizable here (distinct eigenvalues).
Fun fact. A Jordan block $J_2(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\chi = x^2$ and $m = x^2$, so for a single Jordan block the polynomials coincide. But stack two copies of $J_2(0)$ block-diagonally and $\chi$ stays $x^4$ while $m = x^2$. The minimal polynomial feels the Jordan structure, not just the size.
Prop. Trace and determinant from eigenvalues. (D&F §12.3) $\mathrm{tr}(A) = \sum \lambda_i$ and $\det(A) = \prod \lambda_i$, summing and multiplying eigenvalues with multiplicity. Trace is cyclic, so $\mathrm{tr}(AB) = \mathrm{tr}(BA)$. Thus $\mathrm{tr}(AB - BA) = 0$ always.
Fun fact. Why $AB - BA \neq I$ in finite dimension. Taking traces: $\mathrm{tr}(AB - BA) = 0$ but $\mathrm{tr}(I_n) = n$. So if $\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid n$ (which is always true for $\mathbb{C}$), the equation $AB - BA = I$ is algebraically impossible. This kills the noncommutative Heisenberg relation in finite dimension. Quals love this.
Counterexample in infinite dimension. Let $V = k[x]$ with $u \colon p \mapsto xp$ (multiply by $x$) and $v \colon p \mapsto p'$ (formal derivative). Compute $(uv - vu)(p) = x p' - (xp)' = x p' - p - xp' = -p$, so $uv - vu = -\mathrm{id}$. Over $k[x]$, the commutator relation works. Infinite-dimensional vector spaces have no trace, so the trace argument collapses.
