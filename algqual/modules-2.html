Exact sequences

Def. Short exact sequence. (D&F §10.5) A diagram $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$ is exact if $f$ is injective, $g$ is surjective, and $\mathrm{im}\, f = \ker g$. So exactness says $f$ makes $A$ a submodule of $B$, and $C$ is what's left over, $C \cong B/f(A)$. The sequence splits if $B \cong A \oplus C$ (as an isomorphism of the diagram), equivalently if there is a section $C \to B$ of $g$ or a retraction $B \to A$ of $f$.
Example (split). $0 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3 \to \mathbb{Z}/3 \to 0$ splits, since the middle is already the direct sum.
Non-example (non-split). $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$. A split would give $\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$, but the right side has torsion and $\mathbb{Z}$ does not.
Fun fact. When sequences don't split. (D&F §10.5) A splitting means $B$ is just $A$ and $C$ sitting side by side, so $B$ has to contain a copy of $C$ as a summand, which is what a section of $g$ gives. This fails when $B$ glues $A$ and $C$ together in a way that can't be undone, for example when $C$ has torsion but $B$ doesn't, or when some ring element moves part of $C$ into $A$. Here are the standard examples.
Remark. Three standard non-split examples.
  • Over $\mathbb{Z}$: $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$. A split would need $\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$, but the right side has torsion and $\mathbb{Z}$ doesn't.
  • Over $\mathbb{C}[x]$: $0 \to (x)/(x^2) \to \mathbb{C}[x]/(x^2) \to \mathbb{C}[x]/(x) \to 0$. As vector spaces this does split, both sides are $\mathbb{C}^2$, so the problem is the action of $x$. Write an element of the middle as $(c, d)$ for $c + dx \bmod x^2$. Multiplying by $x$ sends $(c, d) \mapsto (0, c)$, which isn't always $0$. But $x$ kills both $(x)/(x^2)$ and $\mathbb{C}[x]/(x)$, so it would kill their direct sum too.
  • Over $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: $0 \to (1+i) \to \mathbb{Z}[i] \to \mathbb{Z}[i]/(1+i) \cong \mathbb{F}_2 \to 0$. A splitting would give an idempotent of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ other than $0$ and $1$ (see the next bullet), but $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a domain, so it only has $0$ and $1$.
  • General principle (domains). If $R$ is a domain and $0 \neq I \subsetneq R$, then $0 \to I \to R \to R/I \to 0$ never splits. A splitting writes $R = I \oplus J$ as ideals, so $1 = i + e$ with $i \in I$ and $e \in J$. Then $e = e \cdot 1 = ei + e^2$, and $ei \in I \cap J = 0$, so $e^2 = e$. In a domain this forces $e \in \{0,1\}$, which makes $I = R$ or $I = 0$.
Prop. Sequence from two maximal ideals. (D&F §10.5) If $I, J$ are distinct maximal ideals of $R$, then $I + J = R$, and the sequence $$0 \to IJ \to I \oplus J \xrightarrow{(a, b) \mapsto a - b} R \to 0$$ (with first map $x \mapsto (x, x)$) is exact. The kernel of the second map is $I \cap J$, which equals $IJ$ since $I + J = R$. The sequence splits because $R$ is free, so $I \oplus J \cong IJ \oplus R$. So if $I$ and $J$ are projective, so is $IJ$, as a direct summand of a projective module.
Thm (Short Five Lemma). (D&F §10.5, Prop. 24) In a commutative diagram of short exact sequences $$\begin{array}{ccccccccc} 0 &\to& A &\to& B &\to& C &\to& 0 \\ & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle \alpha} & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle \beta} & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle \gamma} & & \\ 0 &\to& A' &\to& B' &\to& C' &\to& 0 \end{array}$$ if $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ are injective (resp. surjective, isomorphisms) then so is $\beta$.
Proof template (diagram chase). For $\beta$ injective: take $b \in \ker \beta$. Its image in $C$ lies in $\ker \gamma = 0$, so $b$ comes from some $a \in A$ via the top row. The image of $\alpha(a)$ in $B'$ is $\beta(b) = 0$, and $A' \hookrightarrow B'$ gives $\alpha(a) = 0$. With $\alpha$ injective, $a = 0$, so $b = 0$. Surjectivity is a similar chase going the other way.
