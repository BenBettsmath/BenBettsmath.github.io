Exact sequences

Def. Short exact sequence. (D&F §10.5) A diagram $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$ is exact if $f$ is injective, $g$ is surjective, and $\mathrm{im}\, f = \ker g$. Exactness says f embeds A as the kernel of the quotient map g: B ↠ C. The sequence splits if $B \cong A \oplus C$ (as an isomorphism of the diagram), equivalently if there is a section $C \to B$ of $g$ or a retraction $B \to A$ of $f$.
Example (split). $0 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3 \to \mathbb{Z}/3 \to 0$ splits trivially.
Non-example (non-split). $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$. A split would give $\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$, but the right side has torsion and $\mathbb{Z}$ does not.
Fun fact. Most short exact sequences don't split. (D&F §10.5) A split is an isomorphism $B \cong A \oplus C$ respecting the diagram. For this to hold, middle has to contain a copy of $C$ as a summand, requiring a section of $g$. But $B$ often encodes obstruction data: a hidden constraint that forces $B$ to be irreducibly between $A$ and $C$. Three canonically obstructed cases.
Remark. Three standard non-split examples.
  • Over $\mathbb{Z}$: $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$. A split would need $\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$, but the right side has torsion and $\mathbb{Z}$ doesn't.
  • Over $\mathbb{C}[x]$: $0 \to (x)/(x^2) \to \mathbb{C}[x]/(x^2) \to \mathbb{C}[x]/(x) \to 0$. The obstruction is the action of $x$, not dimension. Element of the middle as $(c, d)$ for $c + dx \bmod x^2$. Multiply by $x$: $(c, d) \mapsto (0, c)$, the constant sliding forward and the $x$ slot vanishing. This nonzero action of $x$ on the middle cannot split into summands where $x$ dies (the two copies of $\mathbb{C}$).
  • Over $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: $0 \to (1+i) \to \mathbb{Z}[i] \to \mathbb{Z}[i]/(1+i) \cong \mathbb{F}_2 \to 0$. A split would be an idempotent in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ projecting to $\mathbb{F}_2$, but $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ has only idempotents $0, 1$.
  • General principle (domains). If $R$ is a domain and $0 \neq I \subsetneq R$, then $0 \to I \to R \to R/I \to 0$ never splits. A split gives an idempotent $e$ with $eR = M$ and $(1-e)R = I$, so $e^2 = e$ in the domain forces $e \in \{0,1\}$, making $I = 0$ or $I = R$.
Prop. Sequence from two maximal ideals. (D&F §10.5) If $I, J$ are distinct maximal ideals of $R$, then $I + J = R$, and the sequence $$0 \to IJ \to I \oplus J \xrightarrow{(a, b) \mapsto a - b} R \to 0$$ (with first map $x \mapsto (x, x)$) is exact. If $I$ and $J$ are projective, so is $IJ$, since the sequence splits.
Thm (Short Five Lemma). (D&F §10.5, Prop. 24) In a commutative diagram of short exact sequences $$\begin{array}{ccccccccc} 0 &\to& A &\to& B &\to& C &\to& 0 \\ & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle \alpha} & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle \beta} & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle \gamma} & & \\ 0 &\to& A' &\to& B' &\to& C' &\to& 0 \end{array}$$ if $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ are injective (resp. surjective, isomorphisms) then so is $\beta$.
Proof template (diagram chase). For $\beta$ injective: take $b \in \ker \beta$. Its image in $C$ lies in $\ker \gamma = 0$, so $b$ comes from some $a \in A$ via the top row. The image of $\alpha(a)$ in $B'$ is $\beta(b) = 0$, and $A' \hookrightarrow B'$ gives $\alpha(a) = 0$. With $\alpha$ injective, $a = 0$, so $b = 0$. Surjectivity dualizes.
