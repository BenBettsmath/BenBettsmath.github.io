Basic toolkit

Def. Normal subgroup. (D&F §3.1) $H \trianglelefteq G$ iff $gHg^{-1} = H$ for every $g \in G$. This is exactly the condition that makes the coset space $G/H$ a group. Normal does not mean elements commute. It means conjugation shuffles $H$ into itself, possibly scrambling it internally.
Fun fact. Every kernel is normal, and every normal subgroup is a kernel, namely of the quotient map $G \to G/H$. So "normal subgroup" and "kernel of some homomorphism" are the same class of objects seen from two angles. (D&F §3.1)
Example. $A_n \trianglelefteq S_n$ (kernel of the sign map), and $Z(G) \trianglelefteq G$ always.
Non-example. $\langle (1\,2) \rangle$ is not normal in $S_3$. Conjugating by $(1\,2\,3)$ turns $(1\,2)$ into $(2\,3)$, which escapes the subgroup. Conjugation relabels the points a transposition moves, so a single transposition's cyclic subgroup is never normal in $S_n$ for $n \geq 3$.
Fun fact. Normality is not transitive. In $D_8$ take a reflection $s$. Then $\langle s \rangle \trianglelefteq \langle s, r^2 \rangle \trianglelefteq D_8$, yet $\langle s \rangle$ is not normal in $D_8$ since $rsr^{-1} = sr^{-2} \notin \langle s \rangle$. Each step has index 2, and index 2 forces normality, but the composite fails. Quals love this. (D&F §3.1)
Thm. The four isomorphism theorems. (D&F §3.3)
  1. First: $G/\ker\phi \cong \mathrm{im}\,\phi$ for any homomorphism $\phi$. The workhorse. Almost every "identify this quotient" problem is a hunt for the right $\phi$.
  2. Second (diamond): $H/(H \cap N) \cong HN/N$ when $N \trianglelefteq G$ and $H \leq G$. Draw the diamond of subgroups, the two parallel sides give isomorphic quotients.
  3. Third: $(G/N)/(H/N) \cong G/H$ when $N \trianglelefteq H \trianglelefteq G$. Quotients of quotients cancel like fractions.
  4. Fourth (lattice): Subgroups of $G/N$ correspond bijectively with subgroups of $G$ containing $N$, preserving containment, normality, and index. The quotient's subgroup lattice is a floor of the original lattice.
Prop. Cheap normality certificates. (D&F §3.2, 4.2)
  • $[G:H] = 2 \Rightarrow H \trianglelefteq G$. The cheapest proof of normality in the book.
  • More generally, if $[G:H] = p$ is the smallest prime dividing $|G|$, then $H \trianglelefteq G$.
  • $Z(G)$ and $[G,G]$ are always normal. So is any subgroup of $Z(G)$.
  • $H, K \trianglelefteq G \Rightarrow H \cap K \trianglelefteq G$ and $HK \trianglelefteq G$.
Fun fact. Counting with products. (D&F §3.2) For finite subgroups, $|HK| = \dfrac{|H|\,|K|}{|H \cap K|}$ whether or not $HK$ is a subgroup. And $HK$ is a subgroup iff $HK = KH$, which holds for free when either factor is normal. The counting formula is the engine behind many Sylow arguments later.
Prop. Commutator subgroup. (D&F §5.4) $[G,G]$ is generated by the commutators $[x,y] = xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$. It measures how far $G$ is from abelian, and it is the smallest normal subgroup with abelian quotient. So $G/[G,G]$, the abelianization, is the largest abelian shadow of $G$. Any homomorphism from $G$ to an abelian group factors through it.
