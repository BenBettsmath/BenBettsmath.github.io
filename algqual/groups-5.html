Group actions

Def. Group action. (D&F §4.1) An action of $G$ on a set $X$ is a homomorphism $G \to \mathrm{Sym}(X)$, equivalently a map $G \times X \to X$ with $1 \cdot x = x$ and $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$. So each $g$ shuffles the points of $X$, and doing $h$ and then $g$ is the same as doing $gh$. The orbit of $x$ is $Gx = \{gx : g \in G\}$, and the stabilizer is $G_x = \{g : gx = x\}$, a subgroup of $G$.
Example. $S_n$ acts on $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ (one orbit, stabilizer of $i$ is $S_{n-1}$). $G$ acts on itself by left multiplication (one orbit, trivial stabilizer). And $G$ acts on itself by conjugation (orbits are conjugacy classes, and stabilizers are centralizers). These three are the ones that come up the most.
Non-example. The trivial action $g \cdot x = x$ is a fine action, it just doesn't tell us anything. A common mistake is writing $g \cdot x = xg$ and calling it a left action. It isn't one, since $(gh) \cdot x = xgh$ but $g \cdot (h \cdot x) = xhg$. Using $g \cdot x = xg^{-1}$ fixes the order, and for $G$ acting on itself we usually stick to $gxg^{-1}$ for conjugation or $gx$ for left multiplication.
Thm (Orbit-stabilizer). (D&F §4.1, Prop. 2) $$|Gx| = [G:G_x].$$ This is because $gx = hx$ exactly when $h^{-1}g \in G_x$, so the points of the orbit match up with the cosets of $G_x$. Almost every counting argument with group actions comes back to this.
Thm (Class equation). (D&F §4.3, Thm. 7) $$|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_i [G:C_G(g_i)],$$ the sum running over one representative $g_i$ per conjugacy class of size $>1$. This is just orbit-stabilizer for the conjugation action. The orbits split $G$ into pieces, the center is exactly the elements whose orbit is only themselves, and every other orbit has size $[G:C_G(g_i)]$.
Cor. (D&F §4.3, Cor. 9) If $|G| = p^n$ then $Z(G) \neq 1$. This is because every term in the sum of the class equation is a power of $p$ bigger than $1$, so it's divisible by $p$, and so is $|G|$. So $p$ divides $|Z(G)|$ too. Groups of order $p^2$ are abelian, since otherwise $G/Z(G)$ would have order $p$ and be cyclic, which forces $G$ to be abelian anyway.
Thm (Coset action / Cayley). (D&F §4.2, Thm. 3) $G$ acts on the left cosets of $H$ by left multiplication, giving $\phi\colon G \to S_{[G:H]}$. Then $\ker\phi = \text{Core}_G(H) = \bigcap_{g \in G} gHg^{-1}$ (the core of $H$), which is normal in $G$ and contained in $H$. If $[G:H] = n$ and $n! < |G|$, then $\phi$ can't be injective, so $\ker\phi$ is nontrivial, and it's proper since it sits inside $H$. This gives a nontrivial normal subgroup of $G$.
