Splitting fields, normality, separability

Def. Splitting field. (D&F §13.4) The splitting field of $f \in F[x]$ over $F$ is the smallest extension of $F$ in which $f$ splits completely into linear factors. Splitting fields exist and are unique up to isomorphism, so this is the canonical home for a polynomial's roots. Construction: adjoin roots one at a time; no field is smaller. Uniqueness: any two splitting fields are isomorphic over $F$ by a deep recursion on the number of irreducible factors.
Example. Splitting field of $x^2 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$, degree $2$. Splitting field of $x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \zeta_3)$, degree $6$ (must adjoin both a real cube root and a primitive third root of unity).
Non-example. $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ is not the splitting field of $x^3 - 2$. The other two roots $\omega \sqrt[3]{2}, \omega^2 \sqrt[3]{2}$ where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ lie outside $\mathbb{R}$, so they are not in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$. This makes $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ not normal.
Computation. Splitting field of $x^4 + 4$. Sophie Germain identity: $x^4 + 4 = (x^2 - 2x + 2)(x^2 + 2x + 2)$. Roots of $x^2 - 2x + 2 = 0$: discriminant $4 - 8 = -4$, so $x = \frac{2 \pm 2i}{2} = 1 \pm i$. Roots of $x^2 + 2x + 2 = 0$: similarly $x = -1 \pm i$. All four roots live in $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, so the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ of degree $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
Def. Normal extension. (D&F §13.4) $E/F$ is normal if $E$ is the splitting field of some family of polynomials over $F$. Equivalently, every irreducible $f \in F[x]$ with one root in $E$ has all roots in $E$. Normality is the rigidity condition that forces automorphisms to permute roots faithfully. Without it, the extension escapes our control.
Def. Separability. (D&F §13.5) $f \in F[x]$ is separable if it has no repeated roots (in an algebraic closure). Check: $\gcd(f, f') = 1$, so the derivative is coprime to $f$. Element: $\alpha$ is separable over $F$ if its minimal polynomial is separable. Extension: $E/F$ is separable if every element is. Field: $F$ is perfect if every irreducible in $F[x]$ is separable. All of characteristic $0$ is perfect. Finite fields are perfect (Frobenius is surjective).
Example. In characteristic $0$: if $f$ is irreducible and $f' = 0$, then $\deg f' < \deg f$ and $f' \neq 0$, contradiction. So all irreducibles are separable. Finite fields $\mathbb{F}_q$: Frobenius $x \mapsto x^q$ is a bijection, so $\mathbb{F}_q$ is perfect.
Non-example (inseparable!). Over $F = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$, the polynomial $x^p - t$ is irreducible by Eisenstein at $t$. But $(x^p - t)' = 0$ in characteristic $p$, so $x^p - t = (x - \sqrt[p]{t})^p$ has a single root of multiplicity $p$. Hence the extension $\mathbb{F}_p(t, \sqrt[p]{t})/\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ is not separable, and $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ is not perfect.
Prop (Frobenius). (D&F §13.5, Prop. 35) In characteristic $p$, the map $\varphi(a) = a^p$ is a ring homomorphism (the Frobenius endomorphism). Since $\ker \varphi = \{a : a^p = 0\}$, in an integral domain $\ker \varphi = 0$, so Frobenius is injective. On a finite field $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, Frobenius is surjective, hence bijective: an automorphism. This makes every finite field perfect.
Fun fact. Normal + Separable = Galois. A finite extension $E/F$ is Galois iff it is normal and separable. Normality traps the automorphisms and forces them to act as root permutations. Separability ensures that there are no repeated roots to worry about. Together, they give $|\mathrm{Aut}(E/F)| = [E:F]$, the full automorphism group.
