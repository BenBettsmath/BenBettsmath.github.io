Splitting fields, normal and separable extensions

Def. Splitting field. (D&F §13.4) Let $F$ be a field and $f \in F[x]$. The splitting field of $f$ over $F$ is the smallest extension of $F$ in which $f$ splits completely into linear factors. Splitting fields exist and are unique up to isomorphism, so it makes sense to talk about the splitting field of $f$. To build one, we adjoin the roots one at a time, using $F[x]/(g)$ to get a root of an irreducible factor $g$. Uniqueness comes from extending an isomorphism one root at a time, by induction on the degree.
Example. The splitting field of $x^2 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$, of degree $2$. The splitting field of $x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \zeta_3)$, of degree $6$ (we need both a cube root of $2$ and a primitive third root of unity, and the degree is $3 \cdot 2$ since $\zeta_3$ isn't real).
Non-example. $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ is not the splitting field of $x^3 - 2$. The other two roots $\omega \sqrt[3]{2}, \omega^2 \sqrt[3]{2}$ where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ lie outside $\mathbb{R}$, so they are not in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$. This makes $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ not normal, meaning that the irreducible polynomial $x^3 - 2$ has one root in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ but not all of its roots.
Computation. Splitting field of $x^4 + 4$. By the Sophie Germain identity, $x^4 + 4 = (x^2 - 2x + 2)(x^2 + 2x + 2)$. The equation $x^2 - 2x + 2 = 0$ has discriminant $4 - 8 = -4$, so its roots are $x = \frac{2 \pm 2i}{2} = 1 \pm i$. Similarly, $x^2 + 2x + 2 = 0$ has roots $x = -1 \pm i$. All four roots lie in $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, so the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, of degree $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
Def. Normal extension. (D&F §13.4) A field extension $E/F$ is normal if $E$ is the splitting field of some family of polynomials over $F$. Equivalently, every $f \in F[x]$ that is irreducible over $F$ and has one root in $E$ has all its roots in $E$. This condition ensures that an automorphism of $E$ fixing $F$ always has somewhere to send each root, since all the conjugates of the root (the other roots of its minimal polynomial over $F$) are already in $E$.
Def. Separability. (D&F §13.5) A polynomial $f \in F[x]$ is separable if it has no repeated roots in an algebraic closure of $F$. To check this we can use $\gcd(f, f') = 1$, since a repeated root of $f$ is also a root of $f'$. An element $\alpha$ algebraic over $F$ is separable over $F$ if its minimal polynomial over $F$ is separable. An algebraic extension $E/F$ is separable if every element of $E$ is separable over $F$. A field $F$ is perfect if every irreducible in $F[x]$ is separable. Every field of characteristic $0$ is perfect, and so is every finite field (since the Frobenius map $a \mapsto a^p$ is surjective on a finite field of characteristic $p$).
Example. Let $F$ be a field of characteristic $0$ and let $f \in F[x]$ be irreducible over $F$. Then $f' \neq 0$ and $f'$ has smaller degree than $f$, so $\gcd(f, f')$ is a proper divisor of the irreducible $f$, which means it's $1$. So every irreducible polynomial over $F$ is separable. For a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ of characteristic $p$, the Frobenius map $x \mapsto x^p$ is an injective map from the finite set $\mathbb{F}_q$ to itself, hence a bijection, so $\mathbb{F}_q$ is perfect.
Non-example (inseparable). Over $F = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$, the polynomial $x^p - t$ is irreducible over $F$ by Eisenstein's criterion at the prime $t$ of $\mathbb{F}_p[t]$. But $(x^p - t)' = 0$ in characteristic $p$, so $x^p - t = (x - \sqrt[p]{t})^p$ has a single root of multiplicity $p$. Hence the extension $\mathbb{F}_p(t, \sqrt[p]{t})/\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ is not separable, and $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ is not perfect.
Prop (Frobenius). (D&F §13.5, Prop. 35) On a commutative ring of prime characteristic $p$, the map $\varphi(a) = a^p$ is a ring homomorphism (the Frobenius endomorphism). Since $\ker \varphi = \{a : a^p = 0\}$, in an integral domain $\ker \varphi = 0$, so Frobenius is injective. On a finite field $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, Frobenius is also surjective, since an injective map from a finite set to itself is onto. So it's an automorphism. This makes every finite field perfect.
Remark. Normal + Separable = Galois. A finite extension $E/F$ is Galois iff it is normal and separable. Normality ensures that every embedding of $E$ fixing $F$ lands back in $E$, and separability ensures that there are $[E:F]$ distinct embeddings, since the roots are distinct. Together they give $|\mathrm{Aut}(E/F)| = [E:F]$.
