Rational canonical form

Thm (RCF). (D&F §12.2, Thm. 16-17) Let $T$ be an operator on an $n$-dimensional vector space $V$ over a field $k$. Then $T$ has a unique rational canonical form, a block diagonal matrix $\mathrm{diag}(C_{a_1}, \ldots, C_{a_r})$ of companion matrices for polynomials $a_1 \mid a_2 \mid \cdots \mid a_r$ in $k[x]$. These satisfy $\sum \deg a_i = n$, $\prod a_i = \chi_T$, and $a_r = m_T$. So two operators are similar iff they have the same invariant factors. This comes from viewing $V$ as a $k[x]$-module where $x$ acts by $T$, and applying the structure theorem for modules over the PID $k[x]$.
Def. Companion matrix. (D&F §12.2) For monic $f = x^d + b_{d-1} x^{d-1} + \cdots + b_0 \in k[x]$, the companion matrix $C_f$ is the $d \times d$ matrix with $1$'s on the diagonal just below the main diagonal, last column $(-b_0, -b_1, \ldots, -b_{d-1})^T$, and zeros elsewhere. Then $\chi_{C_f} = m_{C_f} = f$. It's the matrix of multiplication by $x$ on $k[x]/(f)$ in the basis $1, x, \ldots, x^{d-1}$, which is why $f$ is its minimal polynomial.
Remark. Rational canonical form is computed in the base field. (D&F §12.2, Cor. 18) Let $A, B$ be $n \times n$ matrices over a field $K$ and let $L \supseteq K$ be an extension field. Then $A$ and $B$ are similar over $K$ if and only if they are similar over $L$. This is because the invariant factors are computed from $xI - A$ by row and column operations in $K[x]$, so they don't change when we pass to $L[x]$. This appears often on quals.
Thm (Jordan canonical form). (D&F §12.3) Let $T$ be an operator on a finite-dimensional vector space over an algebraically closed field. Then $T$ is similar to a block diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks $J_k(\lambda) = \lambda I_k + N_k$, where $N_k$ is the $k \times k$ nilpotent shift (the matrix with $1$s on the superdiagonal and zeros elsewhere). There is one Jordan block for each elementary divisor $(x - \lambda)^k$. Jordan form needs all the eigenvalues to be in $k$, so over $\mathbb{Q}$ or a finite field, where the eigenvalues need not lie in the field, we use the rational canonical form instead.
Prop. Diagonalizability criterion. (D&F §12.3, Prop. 23) An operator $T$ on a finite-dimensional vector space over $k$ is diagonalizable over $k$ if and only if $m_T$ splits into distinct linear factors in $k[x]$. This is because a repeated root of $m_T$ corresponds exactly to a Jordan block of size bigger than $1$.
Example. If a square matrix $A$ over $k$ satisfies $A^n = I$ and $\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid n$, then $m_A \mid x^n - 1$. Since $x^n - 1$ is squarefree (its derivative $nx^{n-1}$ is coprime to it), every divisor is squarefree, so $A$ is diagonalizable over the algebraic closure of $k$.
Non-example. A Jordan block $J_2(\lambda)$ has $m = (x-\lambda)^2$, which is not squarefree, so it is not diagonalizable.
Computation. Classification by invariant factors: $3 \times 3$ matrices over $k$ with $\chi = (x-2)^2(x-3)$.
Invariant factor chains $a_1 \mid a_2$ with product $(x-2)^2(x-3)$ are:
  • $a_1 = 1, a_2 = (x-2)^2(x-3)$: One companion block. The RCF is $C_{a_2}$ with $m = a_2$.
  • $a_1 = (x-2), a_2 = (x-2)(x-3)$: Two blocks, $m = a_2 = (x-2)(x-3)$. The RCF is $\mathrm{diag}(C_{(x-2)}, C_{(x-2)(x-3)})$.
These are the two similarity classes. In terms of elementary divisors, these are $\{(x-2)^2, (x-3)\}$ for class 1 and $\{(x-2), (x-2), (x-3)\}$ for class 2.
Note. Matrices with $A^n = I$ over finite fields $\mathbb{F}_q$.
If $A^n = I$ then $m_A \mid x^n - 1$. Steps to enumerate similarity classes:
  1. Factor $x^n - 1$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$ into irreducibles. These correspond to Frobenius orbits of $n$th roots of unity under $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^q$. An orbit's length is the degree of its irreducible factor. Roots of order $d$ have orbit size equal to the multiplicative order of $q \pmod{d}$.
  2. If $p = \mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F}_q)$ and $p \nmid n$, then $x^n - 1$ is squarefree. Every divisor is too, so all matrices with $A^n = I$ are diagonalizable. Similarity classes correspond to multisets of irreducible factors whose degrees add up to the size of the matrix.
  3. If $p \mid n$, write $n = p^s n'$ with $p \nmid n'$. Then $x^n - 1 = (x^{n'}-1)^{p^s}$. Irreducible factors now repeat, so the matrices don't have to be diagonalizable anymore, and we have to keep track of the invariant factor chains $a_1 \mid \cdots \mid a_r$, which record the Jordan blocks.
  4. Count by partition type: split the size of the matrix into parts matching degrees of available irreducibles, then count multisets of factors by repetition pattern for each partition.
Computation. Matrices $A$ over $\mathbb{F}_7$ with $A^9 = I$ (Aug 2024 qual).
We have $p = 7$, $n = 9$, and $7 \nmid 9$. So $x^9 - 1$ is squarefree over $\mathbb{F}_7$. Factor it into irreducibles using the Frobenius orbits of the $9$th roots of unity under $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^7$.
  • Order $1$: The root $\zeta = 1$ is fixed by Frobenius, so its orbit has length $1$. The irreducible factor is $x - 1$.
  • Order $3$: For the primitive cube roots, $7 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$, so Frobenius fixes each of them. There are two roots and two orbits of length $1$. In $\mathbb{F}_7$ we have $2^3 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$ and $2^2 + 2 + 1 = 7 \equiv 0 \pmod{7}$, so the roots are $2$ and $4$, and the factors are $(x-2)(x-4)$.
  • Order $9$: There are six primitive $9$th roots. The order of $7 \pmod{9}$ is $3$ (since $7^1 \equiv 7$, $7^2 \equiv 4$, $7^3 \equiv 1 \pmod{9}$), so each orbit has size $3$, giving $6/3 = 2$ irreducible factors of degree $3$.
So $x^9 - 1 = (x-1)(x-2)(x-4)\,f_1 f_2$ over $\mathbb{F}_7$, three linear factors and two cubics, with degrees $1+1+1+3+3 = 9$. Now count. A conjugacy class of $3 \times 3$ matrices corresponds to a multiset of elementary divisors with degrees summing to $3$, and since $x^9-1$ is squarefree each elementary divisor is one of these five irreducibles to the first power. Total degree $3$ leaves exactly two possibilities.
  • One cubic factor, giving $2$ classes.
  • Three linear factors chosen from $\{x-1,\, x-2,\, x-4\}$ with repetition allowed. There are $\binom{5}{3} = 10$ multisets of size $3$ from $3$ symbols, hence that many classes.
The total is $10 + 2 = \boxed{12}$ conjugacy classes.
