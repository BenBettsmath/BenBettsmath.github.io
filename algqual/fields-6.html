Computing Galois groups

Def. Discriminant of a polynomial. (D&F §14.6) For monic $f \in F[x]$ with roots $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$ in a splitting field, the discriminant is $\Delta(f) = \prod_{i < j} (\alpha_i - \alpha_j)^2$. $\Delta$ is in $F$ even though the roots usually aren't, since it's symmetric in the roots, so every automorphism fixes it. $\Delta \neq 0$ iff all roots are distinct, i.e., $f$ is separable. Prop. Cubic Galois group via discriminant. (D&F §14.6) For $f = x^3 + px + q$ irreducible over $F$ with $\mathrm{char}(F) \neq 2,3$, the discriminant is $\Delta(f) = -4p^3 - 27q^2$. Since $f$ is irreducible its Galois group is a transitive subgroup of $S_3$, so it's either $A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$ or $S_3$, and the discriminant tells us which. If $\sqrt{\Delta} \in F$, then $\mathrm{Gal}(f) \subseteq A_3$, so $\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$. Otherwise $\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong S_3$ (nonabelian, the generic case).
Computation. Galois group of $x^3 - 3x + 1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. First check irreducibility: rational root test on $x^3 - 3x + 1$. Candidates $\pm 1$ give $1 - 3 + 1 = -1 \neq 0$ and $-1 + 3 + 1 = 3 \neq 0$, so irreducible. Write $f = x^3 - 3x + 1$ in form $x^3 + px + q$ where $p = -3, q = 1$. Discriminant: $\Delta = -4(-3)^3 - 27(1)^2 = -4(-27) - 27 = 108 - 27 = 81 = 9^2$. Since $\sqrt{\Delta} = 9 \in \mathbb{Q}$, the Galois group is $\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$.
Prop. Galois group of $x^n - a$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. (D&F §14.7) Assume $x^n - a$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ with $n \geq 3$. Let $\alpha = a^{1/n}$ and $\zeta_n$ a primitive $n$-th root. The splitting field is $K = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha, \zeta_n)$, since the roots are $\zeta_n^k \alpha$. For $n = p$ prime its degree is $[K:\mathbb{Q}] = p(p-1)$, since the two degrees $p$ and $p - 1$ are coprime. For general $n$ it can be smaller than $n\varphi(n)$, since $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ can overlap (for example $\sqrt 2 \in \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)$). The Galois group $G = \mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})$ is a subgroup of the semidirect product $\mathbb{Z}/n \rtimes (\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$. It's usually non-abelian. For example if $\alpha$ is real, then $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ misses the non-real roots $\zeta_n^k \alpha$, so $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}$ isn't Galois, and in an abelian group every subgroup would be normal.
Computation. Galois group of $x^5 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. Irreducibility: Eisenstein criterion at $p=2$ (leading coeff $1$ coprime to $2$, but $2 \mid 2$, $2^2 \nmid 2$). Splitting field: $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[5]{2}, \zeta_5)$. Degree: $[K:\mathbb{Q}] = 5 \cdot \varphi(5) = 5 \cdot 4 = 20$. Galois group: $\mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})$ is isomorphic to the non-abelian semidirect product $\mathbb{Z}/5 \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/4$, order $20$. It's non-abelian because $\mathbb{Z}/4 \cong (\mathbb{Z}/5)^\times$ acts on $\mathbb{Z}/5$ by multiplication. This is just how $\zeta_5 \mapsto \zeta_5^c$ moves the roots $\zeta_5^b \sqrt[5]{2}$ around, it sends $b$ to $cb$.
Prop. Cyclotomic and Kronecker-Weber theorem. (D&F §13.6, §14.5) $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, always abelian (since units form an abelian group). Kronecker-Weber: Every finite abelian extension $K/\mathbb{Q}$ is contained in some cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$. So every abelian extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ comes from roots of unity.
Thm. Every finite group is a Galois group over function fields. (D&F §14.6, Ex. 46) For any finite group $G$, there exists a Galois extension $L/K$ of fields with $\mathrm{Gal}(L/K) \cong G$. To build it, embed $G \hookrightarrow S_n$ by Cayley's theorem. Take $L = \mathbb{Q}(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ (purely transcendental), and let $G$ act by permuting the variables. Set $K = L^G$ (fixed field). Then $L/K$ is Galois with group $G$, since a finite group of automorphisms is always the Galois group over its fixed field (Artin). Note: $K$ is not usually $\mathbb{Q}$. The inverse Galois problem over $\mathbb{Q}$ (whether all finite groups realize over $\mathbb{Q}$) remains open.
Thm (Galois-Abel-Ruffini). (D&F §14.7) A polynomial $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is solvable by radicals (i.e., roots expressible in $+, -, \times, \div, \sqrt[n]{\ }$) iff its Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(f)$ is a solvable group. The idea is that adjoining an $n$-th root is a cyclic step, so a tower of radicals gives a chain of subgroups with cyclic quotients. For finite groups, solvable is the same as having a composition series with quotients of prime order. For $n \geq 5$, the symmetric group $S_n$ is not solvable. A generic polynomial of degree $n$ has Galois group $S_n$, so for $n \geq 5$ it isn't solvable by radicals. This settles classical problems on quintic and higher-degree equations.
Remark. Computing Galois groups in general. For small cases (cubics, quartics, $x^n - a$), the discriminant or a direct look at where the roots can go works. For higher degrees, a good trick is reducing mod primes $p$. The factorization of $f$ mod $p$ gives the cycle type of an element of the Galois group (Dedekind), which can be used to guess the group and sometimes prove it. There are algorithms that compute Galois groups in general, but they're much too slow to do by hand.
