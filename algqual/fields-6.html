Computing Galois groups

Def. Discriminant of a polynomial. (D&F §14.6) For monic $f \in F[x]$ with roots $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$ in a splitting field, the discriminant is $\Delta(f) = \prod_{i < j} (\alpha_i - \alpha_j)^2$. The discriminant is in $F$ even when the roots are not. It is symmetric in the roots, so every automorphism of the splitting field over $F$ fixes it. $\Delta \neq 0$ iff all roots are distinct, i.e., $f$ is separable. Prop. Cubic Galois group via discriminant. (D&F §14.6) For $f = x^3 + px + q$ irreducible over $F$ with $\mathrm{char}(F) \neq 2,3$, the discriminant is $\Delta(f) = -4p^3 - 27q^2$. Since $f$ is irreducible its Galois group is a transitive subgroup of $S_3$, so it's either $A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$ or $S_3$, and the discriminant tells us which. If $\sqrt{\Delta} \in F$, then $\mathrm{Gal}(f) \subseteq A_3$, so $\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$. Otherwise $\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong S_3$ (nonabelian, the generic case).
Computation. Galois group of $x^3 - 3x + 1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. First check irreducibility with the rational root test. The candidates $\pm 1$ give $1 - 3 + 1 = -1 \neq 0$ and $-1 + 3 + 1 = 3 \neq 0$, so the cubic $x^3 - 3x + 1$ has no rational root and is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. Write $f = x^3 - 3x + 1$ in the form $x^3 + px + q$ with $p = -3, q = 1$. The discriminant is $\Delta = -4(-3)^3 - 27(1)^2 = -4(-27) - 27 = 108 - 27 = 81 = 9^2$. Since $\sqrt{\Delta} = 9 \in \mathbb{Q}$, the Galois group is $\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$.
Prop. Galois group of $x^n - a$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. (D&F §14.7) Let $a \in \mathbb{Q}$ and assume $x^n - a$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ with $n \geq 3$. Let $\alpha = a^{1/n}$ and let $\zeta_n$ be a primitive $n$-th root of unity. The splitting field is $K = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha, \zeta_n)$, since the roots are $\zeta_n^k \alpha$. For $n = p$ prime its degree is $[K:\mathbb{Q}] = p(p-1)$, since the two degrees $p$ and $p - 1$ are coprime. For general $n$ it can be smaller than $n\varphi(n)$, since $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ can overlap (for example $\sqrt 2 \in \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)$). The Galois group $G = \mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})$ is a subgroup of the semidirect product $\mathbb{Z}/n \rtimes (\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$. It's usually non-abelian. For example, if $\alpha$ is real, then $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ does not contain the non-real roots $\zeta_n^k \alpha$, so $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}$ isn't Galois, and in an abelian group every subgroup would be normal.
Computation. Galois group of $x^5 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. The polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by the Eisenstein criterion at $p=2$ (the leading coefficient $1$ is coprime to $2$, and $2 \mid 2$, $2^2 \nmid 2$). The splitting field is $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[5]{2}, \zeta_5)$, of degree $[K:\mathbb{Q}] = 5 \cdot \varphi(5) = 5 \cdot 4 = 20$. The Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})$ is isomorphic to the non-abelian semidirect product $\mathbb{Z}/5 \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/4$, of order $20$. It's non-abelian because $\mathbb{Z}/4 \cong (\mathbb{Z}/5)^\times$ acts on $\mathbb{Z}/5$ by multiplication. This action is how $\zeta_5 \mapsto \zeta_5^c$ permutes the roots $\zeta_5^b \sqrt[5]{2}$, sending $b$ to $cb$.
Prop. Cyclotomic extensions and the Kronecker-Weber theorem. (D&F §13.6, §14.5) For every $n \geq 1$ and every primitive $n$-th root of unity $\zeta_n$, $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, which is abelian (since the units of a commutative ring form an abelian group). Kronecker-Weber: Every finite abelian extension $K/\mathbb{Q}$ is contained in some cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$. So every abelian extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ comes from roots of unity.
Thm. Every finite group is a Galois group over function fields. (D&F §14.6, Ex. 46) For any finite group $G$, there exists a Galois extension $L/K$ of fields with $\mathrm{Gal}(L/K) \cong G$. To build it, use Cayley's theorem to embed $G \hookrightarrow S_n$, where $n = |G|$. Take $L = \mathbb{Q}(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ (purely transcendental), and let $G$ act by permuting the variables. Set $K = L^G$ (fixed field). Then $L/K$ is Galois with group $G$, since Artin's theorem says that a finite group of automorphisms of a field $L$ is the Galois group of $L$ over its fixed field. Note: $K$ is not $\mathbb{Q}$. The inverse Galois problem over $\mathbb{Q}$ (whether every finite group is a Galois group over $\mathbb{Q}$) remains open.
Thm (Galois-Abel-Ruffini). (D&F §14.7) A polynomial $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is solvable by radicals (i.e., its roots can be expressed using $+, -, \times, \div, \sqrt[n]{\ }$) iff its Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(f)$ is a solvable group. Adjoining an $n$-th root is a cyclic step, so a tower of radicals gives a chain of subgroups with cyclic quotients. For finite groups, solvable is the same as having a composition series with quotients of prime order. For $n \geq 5$, the symmetric group $S_n$ is not solvable. A generic polynomial of degree $n$ has Galois group $S_n$, so for $n \geq 5$ it isn't solvable by radicals. This settles the classical problem of solving equations of degree $5$ and higher by radicals.
Remark. Computing Galois groups in general. For small cases (cubics, quartics, $x^n - a$), the discriminant or a direct analysis of where the roots can go suffices. For higher degrees, reduce mod primes $p$. Dedekind's theorem says that the factorization of $f$ mod $p$ gives the cycle type of an element of the Galois group, which can be used to guess the group and sometimes prove it. There are algorithms that compute Galois groups in general, but they're much too slow to do by hand.
