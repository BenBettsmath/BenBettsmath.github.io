Projective, injective, and flat modules

Def. Projective, injective, flat. (D&F §10.5) These are three conditions on a module. Projective and injective are dual to each other, and flat is the one that works well with tensor products.
  • An $R$-module $P$ is projective if for every surjection $M \twoheadrightarrow N$ of $R$-modules and every map $P \to N$ there exists a lift $P \to M$. Equivalently, $P$ is a direct summand of a free module. Over a PID, projective is the same as free, since submodules of free modules over a PID are free.
  • An $R$-module $I$ is injective if for every injection $N \hookrightarrow M$ of $R$-modules, every map $N \to I$ extends to a map $M \to I$. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, injective is the same as divisible (every element is divisible by every nonzero integer).
  • An $R$-module $M$ is flat if $- \otimes_R M$ preserves short exact sequences of $R$-modules. Projective implies flat, since free modules are flat ($N \otimes R^n \cong N^n$) and a direct summand of a flat module is flat.
Examples. Over $\mathbb{Z}$: $\mathbb{Z}^n$ is free, projective, and flat. $\mathbb{Q}$ is injective (it is divisible) and flat (it is a localization of $\mathbb{Z}$), but not projective.
Non-examples. $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is not projective over $\mathbb{Z}$, since the surjection $\mathbb{Z} \twoheadrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/2$ does not split. $\mathbb{Z}$ is not injective as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, since extending the identity map $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ along the injection $\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z}$ would need a map $f$ with $f(2) = 1$, and there isn't one.
Thm (Baer's Criterion). (D&F §10.5 Prop. 36) An $R$-module $Q$ is injective iff for every left ideal $I \subseteq R$ and every $R$-linear map $\varphi: I \to Q$, $\varphi$ extends to $R \to Q$.
Cor. Divisible $\Leftrightarrow$ injective over a PID. (D&F §10.5 Cor. 38) Let $R$ be a PID and $Q$ an $R$-module. Every ideal of $R$ is principal, so a map $(r) \to Q$ is determined by where $r$ goes, say to $q$, and extending it to $R$ means finding $q' \in Q$ with $rq' = q$. Hence, by Baer's criterion applied to the ideals $(r)$, $Q$ is injective iff $rQ = Q$ for every nonzero $r \in R$ (i.e. $Q$ is divisible).
Thm. Finitely generated flat $=$ projective over a Noetherian ring. (D&F §10.5 Cor. 42) If $R$ is Noetherian and $M$ is finitely generated, then $M$ is flat iff $M$ is projective. (More generally, finitely presented flat $=$ projective over any ring.)
Prop. $\mathbb{Q}$ is not projective over $\mathbb{Z}$. (D&F §10.5) If $\mathbb{Q}$ were projective over the PID $\mathbb{Z}$ it would be free, since projective modules over a PID are free. But any two nonzero rationals $p/q$ and $r/s$ satisfy a nontrivial $\mathbb{Z}$-linear relation $(rs)(p/q) - (pq)(r/s) = 0$, and in a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module there is no such relation between distinct basis elements. So a basis would have at most one element, but $\mathbb{Q}$ isn't cyclic, since no element generates all of $\mathbb{Q}$.
Prop. Projective $=$ injective $=$ flat over $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. (D&F §10.5, plus CRT §7.6 Thm. 17) Write $n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$, so $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \cong \prod_i \mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i}\mathbb{Z}$. Each $\mathbb{Z}/p^a\mathbb{Z}$ is a local Artinian principal ideal ring, hence quasi-Frobenius (injective as a module over itself). By Baer's criterion applied to the chain of ideals $0 \subset (p^{a-1}) \subset \cdots \subset (p) \subset \mathbb{Z}/p^a$, a finitely generated (f.g.) $\mathbb{Z}/p^a$-module is projective iff it is free iff it is injective. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem decomposition of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ above, the f.g. projective $=$ injective $=$ flat $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$-modules are exactly $$\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} \left(\mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i}\mathbb{Z}\right)^{k_i}, \qquad k_i \geq 0 \text{ independent.}$$ Such a module is free over $\mathbb{Z}/n$ iff all $k_i$ are equal, so projective $\ne$ free in general. (For example, $(\mathbb{Z}/8)^2$ is projective over $\mathbb{Z}/56 = \mathbb{Z}/8 \times \mathbb{Z}/7$ but not free over $\mathbb{Z}/56$.) Flat $=$ projective for these modules because §10.5 Cor. 42 says a finitely generated flat module over a Noetherian ring is projective, and $\mathbb{Z}/n$ is Noetherian.
Remark. Why the ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/n$ determine the indecomposables. The ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are exactly $(\bar d)$ for $d \mid n$, with $(\bar d) \cong \mathbb{Z}/(n/d)\mathbb{Z}$ as an abelian group. Among these, the ideals that split off as indecomposable direct summands of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are precisely the $\mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i}\mathbb{Z}$, one for each prime divisor. CRT identifies $\mathbb{Z}/n$ with $\prod_i \mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i}$, and the orthogonal idempotents $e_i = (0, \ldots, 1, \ldots, 0)$ are the elements of $\mathbb{Z}/n$ that generate the summands $(\bar e_i) \cong \mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i}$. Any other ideal (say $(\bar p_i) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i}$, or a product of strictly smaller primary parts) is not a direct summand, since no idempotent generates it. So the indecomposable direct summands of $\mathbb{Z}/n$ are exactly these prime-power pieces, and every direct summand is a sum of some of them.
Strategy. Finding all projective, injective, or flat modules of order $\leq M$ over $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. The procedure is the same for projective, injective, and flat modules, since they form one class by the proposition above. There are three steps.
  1. Factor $n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$.
  2. Identify the indecomposable summands. As ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/n$ they are $\mathbb{Z}/p_1^{a_1}, \ldots, \mathbb{Z}/p_k^{a_k}$, one per prime, and there are no others. The proposition and remark above show that every projective, injective, or flat module is built from these.
  3. Enumerate tuples $(k_1, \ldots, k_k)$ of nonnegative integers with $$\prod_{i=1}^k \bigl(p_i^{a_i}\bigr)^{k_i} \;\leq\; M.$$ Each such tuple gives one module $\bigoplus_i (\mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i})^{k_i}$ of order $\prod_i (p_i^{a_i})^{k_i}$.
Both directions hold. A projective, injective, or flat module is a direct sum of indecomposables (by the proposition), and any such sum is again projective, injective, and flat, since direct sums preserve all three properties. For projective modules, a direct sum of summands of free modules is a summand of a free module. Finite direct sums of injectives are injective. For flat modules, $- \otimes (M \oplus N) = (- \otimes M) \oplus (- \otimes N)$ preserves exactness on each summand.
Warning. Projective is not the same as free. Free $\mathbb{Z}/n$-modules are the diagonal cases $k_1 = k_2 = \cdots = k_k$. Over $\mathbb{Z}/56 = \mathbb{Z}/8 \times \mathbb{Z}/7$, $\mathbb{Z}/8$ is projective but not free, since it has the wrong order ($8 \neq 56^m$ for any $m \geq 0$). The qual asks for projective (resp. injective, flat), so enumerate the larger class and not only the free modules.
Computation. Injective abelian groups of order $\leq 100$ over $\mathbb{Z}/56\mathbb{Z}$ (Aug 2024 qual). Since $56 = 2^3 \cdot 7$, the injectives are $(\mathbb{Z}/8)^a \oplus (\mathbb{Z}/7)^b$ with $8^a \cdot 7^b \leq 100$. The tuples $(0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1), (2,0), (0,2)$ give six modules of orders $1, 8, 7, 56, 64, 49$. Over $\mathbb{Z}/56$ these are also exactly the projective (and flat) modules of order $\leq 100$, by the proposition above.
Computation. Projective abelian groups of order $\leq 125$ over $\mathbb{Z}/100\mathbb{Z}$ (Jan 2024 qual). Since $100 = 4 \cdot 25$, the projectives are $(\mathbb{Z}/4)^a \oplus (\mathbb{Z}/25)^b$ with $4^a \cdot 25^b \leq 125$. The tuples $(0,0), (1,0), (2,0), (3,0), (0,1), (1,1)$ give six modules of orders $1, 4, 16, 64, 25, 100$. This is more than the free modules $(\mathbb{Z}/100)^m$, which only give $0$ and $\mathbb{Z}/100$.
Computation. Injective abelian groups of order $\leq 200$ over $\mathbb{Z}/120\mathbb{Z}$ (Aug 2023 qual). Here $120 = 2^3 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$, so the indecomposable summands are $\mathbb{Z}/8, \mathbb{Z}/3, \mathbb{Z}/5$. We want $(\mathbb{Z}/8)^a \oplus (\mathbb{Z}/3)^b \oplus (\mathbb{Z}/5)^c$ with $8^a \cdot 3^b \cdot 5^c \leq 200$. Enumerate $(a,b,c)$:
  • $a = 0$: the pairs $(b, c)$ are $(0,0)\!:\!1$, $(0,1)\!:\!5$, $(0,2)\!:\!25$, $(0,3)\!:\!125$, $(1,0)\!:\!3$, $(1,1)\!:\!15$, $(1,2)\!:\!75$, $(2,0)\!:\!9$, $(2,1)\!:\!45$, $(3,0)\!:\!27$, $(3,1)\!:\!135$, $(4,0)\!:\!81$ (12 tuples).
  • $a = 1$: $(0,0)\!:\!8$, $(0,1)\!:\!40$, $(0,2)\!:\!200$, $(1,0)\!:\!24$, $(1,1)\!:\!120$, $(2,0)\!:\!72$ (6 tuples).
  • $a = 2$: $(0,0)\!:\!64$, $(1,0)\!:\!192$ (2 tuples).
  • $a \geq 3$: $8^3 = 512 > 200$, none.
Total: 20 injective $\mathbb{Z}/120\mathbb{Z}$-modules of order $\leq 200$. These are the same $20$ as the projective ones, since over $\mathbb{Z}/n$ the two notions coincide.
